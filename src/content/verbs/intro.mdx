## Foundational Concepts

### No Infinitive

Irish verbs do not have an infinitive form. Instead, Irish uses the verbal noun to express the concept of a verb in its unconjugated state.

### Verb Root and Stem

The **verb root** (`fréamh briathartha`) is the base form of the verb, identical to the 2nd person singular of the imperative.

- *`mol` = praise!*
- *`beannaigh` = bless!*

The **verb stem** (`stoc briathartha`) is the form to which conjugation endings are attached.

- In the 1st conjugation, the stem and root are usually identical. Some verbs ending in `-áil` require depalatalisation to form the stem (e.g., verb root `sabháil`, verb stem `sabhál-`). Monosyllabic verbs in `-igh` like `dóigh` or `léigh` drop the `-igh` before endings (e.g., verb root `dóigh`, verb stem `dó-`).
- In the 2nd conjugation, the stem is often extended by `-igh` or `-í-`, and syncope can occur in verbs ending in `-il`, `-ir`, or `-in` (e.g., verb root `imir`, verb stem `imr(igh)-`).

### Citation Form

The citation form is how a verb is listed in a dictionary. Since Irish has no infinitive, the **verb root** (2nd person singular imperative) is most commonly used.

- *`mol` = praise!*
- *`beannaigh` = bless!*

This applies even to irregular verbs where the imperative form differs from other tenses, such as `tar` (come) and `bí` (be). Some dictionaries may use the 1st person singular present (`molaim`, `beannaím`).

## Conjugation Types

Irish verbs are divided into two main patterns: the 1st and 2nd conjugations. The primary difference lies in how they form the future and conditional tenses.

- **1st Conjugation:** Uses an `-f-` marker.
  - *`molfaidh sé` = he will praise*
- **2nd Conjugation:** Uses an `-ó-` marker.
  - *`beannóidh sé` = he will bless*

Most other differences are due to spelling rules, particularly related to the `-igh` ending of 2nd conjugation verbs. When an ending like `-ann` is added to a verb root like `beannaigh`, the result follows a specific spelling change: `beannaigh` + `ann` becomes `beannaigheann`, which is simplified to `beannaíonn` in the modern standard.

### 1st Conjugation Verbs in -igh

Some monosyllabic verbs in the 1st conjugation also end in `-igh`. These verbs still use the `-f-` future marker.

- Verbs with a **long vowel** (`dóigh`, `léigh`) drop the `-igh` when endings are added.
  - *`dónn sé` = he burns*
- Verbs with a **short vowel** (`nigh`, `suigh`) behave orthographically like 2nd conjugation verbs.
  - *`níonn sé` = he washes*

### Broad and Slender Stems

The distinction between verbs with broad and slender stem-final consonants is purely orthographic. It follows the rule that broad consonants must be surrounded by broad vowels (a, o, u) and slender consonants by slender vowels (e, i).

While the spelling of endings changes to accommodate this rule, the pronunciation of vowel-initial endings remains the same.

- Broad: *`glanaim`*
- Slender: *`tuigim`*

With consonant-initial endings, the consonant itself can be broad or slender.

- Broad: *`glantar`*
- Slender: *`tuigtear`*

## Verb Forms and Functions

### Synthetic and Analytic Forms

Irish verb endings can be either synthetic or analytic.

**Synthetic Form (`foirm tháite`)**

A synthetic form combines the verb and its subject (a personal pronoun) into a single word. A separate personal pronoun cannot follow a synthetic verb form.

- *`tuigim` = I understand.*
- *`thuigeamar` = we understood.*
- Emphatic suffixes can be attached: *`tuigimse`* (I understand!), *`thuigeamarna`* (we understood!).

**Analytic Form (`foirm scartha`)**

An analytic form is a general verb form that requires a separate subject, which can be a pronoun or a noun.

- *`tuigeann tú` = you understand.*
- *`tuigeann an fear` = the man understands.*

The standard conjugation uses a mix of both forms. In many cases, the official standard allows either the analytic or synthetic form to be used, reflecting regional preferences. Munster Irish tends to favour synthetic forms, while northern dialects use analytic forms more frequently.

### The Relative Form

The relative form (`an fhoirm choibhneasta`) is a special analytic verb ending used in the present and future tenses in positive, direct relative clauses. It is marked by an `s` at the end. In the official standard, its use is optional, though it is widespread in Connacht and Ulster dialects.

It is used in the following situations:
- When the antecedent is the subject of the relative clause.
  - *`an fear a ghlanas an carr` = the man who cleans the car*
- In temporal clauses after `nuair` (when) and `sul` (before).
  - *`nuair a bheas siad réidh` = when they will be ready*
- In comparative clauses after `mar` (as/how) and `agus` (as).
  - *`mar a bheas choíche` = as it will always be*
- In cleft sentences, which are used for emphasis.
  - *`Is mise a chreideanns do scéal.` = It is I who believes your story.*
  - *`Céard a dhéananns tú?` = What do you do?*

### Dependent and Independent Forms

A distinction is made between dependent (`foirmeacha spléacha`) and independent (`foirmeacha neamhspléacha`) verb forms. This difference is most noticeable with the 11 irregular verbs.

- **Dependent forms** appear after particles like `an`, `ní`, `nach`, `go`, and `mura`. These particles cause eclipsis (or lenition in the case of `ní`).
- **Independent forms** are used in all other contexts, especially when the verb is at the start of a sentence or after the direct relative particle `a` or the conjunction `má`.

| Independent                   | Dependent                              |
|:------------------------------|:---------------------------------------|
| *`ólaim` = I drink*           | *`ní ólaim` = I do not drink*          |
| *`d’ól mé` = I drank*         | *`níor ól mé` = I did not drink*       |
| *`tá sé ann` = he is there*   | *`níl sé ann` = he is not there*       |
| *`bhí sé ann` = he was there* | *`ní raibh sé ann` = he was not there* |

### The Autonomous Form

The autonomous form (`an saorbhriathar`), also called the impersonal form, refers to an indefinite agent. It is often translated into English using "one", "they", "someone", or a passive construction.

- *`Tuigtear é.` = He is understood / People understand him.*
- *`Báitheadh anuraidh é.` = He drowned last year.* (Indicates an accident, not a crime).
- *`Táthar go maith.` = I am / We are well.* (Literally: "One is well").

The autonomous form is not a true passive. The object of the verb remains in the object form (`é`, not `sé`), and an agent cannot be specified with a preposition like `ag` (by) in modern Irish. It exists for all tenses.

### Idiomatic Uses of the Autonomous Form

The autonomous form is central to many common idioms, especially those describing fateful events or perceptions.

**Verbs of fateful events:**
- `cailleadh`: Used to mean "died".
  - *`Cailleadh go hóg é.` = He died young.* (Literally: "One-lost young him").
- `castar ar`: Used to mean "meets".
  - *`Casadh cailín orm.` = I met a girl.* (Literally: "One-turned a-girl on-me").
- `rugadh`: Used to mean "was born".
  - *`Rugadh mac di.` = She had a son.* (Literally: "One-bore a-son to-her").

**Verbs of perception/supposition:**
- `feictear do`: Used to mean "it seems to".
  - *`Feictear dom go bhfuil sí tinn.` = It seems to me that she is sick.* (Literally: "One-sees to-me that...").
- `samhlaítear do`: Used to mean "to imagine" or "to seem".
  - *`Ní hé a mhalairt a samhlaíodh dom.` = I had not imagined anything else.*
- `taibhrítear do`: Used to mean "to dream".
  - *`Is minic a taibhríodh dom go mbínn ag titim.` = I often dreamed that I was falling.*

## Mutations and Irregularity

### Lenition and Eclipsis of Verbs

Verbs with a consonant initial are lenited in the **preterite, imperfect,** and **conditional** tenses. This is because the verbal particle `do`, which causes lenition, historically preceded the verb. While `do` is now usually omitted, the lenition remains.

- *`thuig mé` < `do thuig mé` = I understood.*

A remnant of this particle is the `d'` prefix used before vowel-initial verbs in these tenses.

- *`d’ól mé` = I drank.*
- *`d’fhoghlaim mé` = I learned.*

Verbs are eclipsed or lenited after other verbal particles depending on the specific particle. The autonomous form in the preterite is never lenited.

### The Irregular Verbs

There are 11 irregular verbs in modern Irish. Their irregularity stems from several features:

- They use different verb **stems** in various tenses (e.g., `tá`, `bhí`, `beidh` for the verb `bí`).
- Five verbs have distinct **dependent forms** (`bí`, `déan`, `feic`, `téigh`, `faigh`).
- Six verbs use special **preterite verbal particles** without an `-r` ending.
- Six verbs have an **autonomous preterite** form ending in `-thas`.
