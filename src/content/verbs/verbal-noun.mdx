## Overview

In Irish, there is no infinitive verb form. Where English uses an infinitive (e.g., "to do"), Irish uses the verbal noun. The Irish verbal noun has similarities to both the infinitive and the gerund in English; it stands between a verb and a noun. For example, the English gerund can be used as a noun (e.g., *to paint* -> *painting* -> *the painting*).

The Irish verbal noun has significantly stronger noun-like (nominal) properties:

*   It has a broader meaning, often describing not just the activity but also its result.
*   It has varied nominal endings (e.g., `-acht`, `-íocht`), unlike the consistent "-ing" in English.
*   It can be masculine or feminine, depending on its ending.
*   It can be declined, meaning it can have a genitive case form.
*   It can take genitive attributes and possessive pronouns as objects.
*   It is frequently used with prepositions like `ag`, `a`, `do`, `chun`, `le`, and `tar éis`.

At the same time, the verbal noun also retains verb-like (verbal) properties:

*   It can be modified by adverbs.
*   It can take direct objects, which are connected to it with a preposition.

Other terms for the verbal noun include verbal substantive and noun of action.

## Formation of the Verbal Noun

The formation of the verbal noun is highly irregular. While some patterns exist, many common verbs have unique verbal noun endings. Dialects may also use different forms for the same verb (e.g., `feiscint` vs. `feiceáil` for "to see").

### First Conjugation Verbs

#### Regular Formations

*   **Ending in `-adh` or `-eadh` (masculine):** This is a common regular ending.
    *   *bris - briseadh* (to break)
    *   *mol - moladh* (to praise)
    *   *póg - pógadh* (to kiss)
    *   Sometimes the verb root is depalatalised (made broad): *buail - bualadh* (to hit).
*   **Ending in `-áil` (feminine):** Verbs ending in `-áil` often have a verbal noun with the same ending.
    *   *péinteáil - péinteáil* (to paint)
*   **Verbs ending in a long vowel + `-igh`:** The verbal noun ends in the long vowel (`-á`, `-é`, `-í`, `-ó`, `-ú`) and is masculine.
    *   *báigh - bá* (to drown)
    *   *pléigh - plé* (to discuss)
    *   *dóigh - dó* (to burn)
*   **Verbs ending in a short vowel/consonant + `-igh`:** The verbal noun typically ends in `-í` (masculine).
    *   *suigh - suí* (to sit)
    *   *nigh - ní* (to wash)

#### Irregular Formations

*   **Zero-ending (no change):** The verbal noun is the same as the verb stem.
    *   *ól - ól* (to drink)
    *   *fás - fás* (to grow)
    *   *léim - léim* (to jump)
*   **Zero-ending with depalatalisation:** The verb stem's final consonant becomes broad.
    *   *cuir - cur* (to put)
    *   *loit - lot* (to destroy)
*   **Ending in `-amh` (masculine):**
    *   *déan - déanamh* (to do)
    *   *seas - seasamh* (to stand)
    *   *léigh - léamh* (to read)
*   **Ending in `-áil` (feminine):**
    *   *fág - fágáil* (to leave)
    *   *gabh - gabháil* (to take)
    *   *tóg - tógáil* (to lift)
*   **Ending in `-úint` (feminine):**
    *   *lean - leanúint* (to follow)
    *   *créid - créidiúint* (to believe)
*   **Ending in `-an` (masculine):**
    *   *lig - ligean* (to allow)
    *   *leag - leagan* (to knock down)
*   **Ending in `-int` (feminine):**
    *   *féach - féachaint* (to look)
    *   *tuig - tuiscint* (to understand)
*   **Ending in `-t` (feminine):**
    *   *bain - baint* (to take out)
    *   *roinn - roinnt* (to share)
*   **Ending in `-im` or `-m` (feminine):**
    *   *tit - titim* (to fall)
    *   *seinn - seinm* (to play music)
*   **Ending in `-acht` (feminine):**
    *   *fan - fanacht* (to wait)
    *   *éist - éisteacht* (to listen)
*   **Rarer endings:** `-achtáil`, `-íl`, `-ach`, `-achan`, `-idh`.
    *   *mair - maireachtáil* (to live)
    *   *buaigh - buachan* (to win)
    *   *iarr - iarraidh* (to ask)

### Second Conjugation Verbs

#### Regular Formations

*   **Ending in `-ú` (masculine):** This is the standard formation for most second conjugation verbs.
    *   *saothraigh - saothrú* (to work hard)
*   **Ending in `-t` (feminine):** For verbs whose stems end in a slender `l`, `n`, or `r`.
    *   *oscail - oscailt* (to open)
    *   *imir - imirt* (to play)

#### Irregular Formations

*   **Ending in `-í` (masculine):**
    *   *éirigh - éirí* (to rise)
    *   *cónaigh - cónaí* (to live, dwell)
*   **Zero-ending:** The verbal noun is the same as the verb stem (minus `-igh`).
    *   *foghlaim - foghlaim* (to learn)
    *   *damhsaigh - damhsa* (to dance)
*   **Zero-ending with depalatalisation:**
    *   *ceangail - ceangal* (to connect)
    *   *siúil - siúl* (to walk)
*   **Ending in `-ach` (masculine):**
    *   *ceannaigh - ceannach* (to buy)
    *   *cuardaigh - cuardach* (to search)
*   **Ending in `-acht` (feminine):**
    *   *dúisigh - dúiseacht* (to awaken)
    *   *imigh - imeacht* (to go away)
*   **Ending in `-áil` or `-achtáil` (feminine):**
    *   *coinnigh - coinneáil* (to keep)
    *   *airigh - aireachtáil* (to perceive)
*   **Ending in `-amh` (masculine):**
    *   *smaoinigh - smaoineamh* (to think)
    *   *áirigh - áireamh* (to count)

### Verbal Nouns without a Verb

Some verbal nouns exist without a corresponding verb stem. These are often formed from nouns for people (especially professions) and are typically used to describe an ongoing activity.

*   *feirmeoir* (farmer) -> *Tá sé ag feirmeoireacht.* (He is working as a farmer.)
*   *siopadóir* (shopkeeper) -> *Tá sé ag siopadóireacht.* (He is shopping.)
*   *cabaire* (chatterer) -> *Tá siad ag cabaireacht.* (They are chatting.)
*   *scéalaí* (storyteller) -> *Tá sé ag scéalaíocht.* (He is telling stories.)

## Declension of the Verbal Noun

Because it is a noun, the verbal noun can change its form depending on its grammatical case.

### The Genitive Case

Verbal nouns have two main ways of forming the genitive, depending on how they are used.

#### The Verbal Adjective as Genitive

The verbal adjective form is used as the genitive when the verbal noun is used transitively, meaning it has a direct object.

*   *lucht **ólta** fuisce* (whiskey-drinkers, lit. "people of the drinking of whiskey")
*   *lucht **foghlamtha** na Gaeilge* (learners of Irish, lit. "people of the learning of Irish")
*   *chun a **mbuailte*** (to hit them)

Verbal nouns ending in `-adh`, `-ú`, and other long vowels always use the verbal adjective as their genitive form, regardless of usage.

*   `glanadh` -> genitive `glanta` (of cleaning)
*   `saothrú` -> genitive `saothraithe` (of working hard)

#### The Substantival Genitive

The normal noun genitive is used when the verbal noun is used intransitively, meaning it does not have a direct object.

*   *lá **bréithe*** (birthday, lit. "day of birth")
*   *lucht **óil*** (drinkers, lit. "people of drinking")
*   *lucht **foghlama*** (learners, lit. "people of learning")

Verbal nouns in `-áil`, `-úint`, and `-cht` always use their substantival genitive (`-ála`, `-úna`, `-chta`) and never the verbal adjective.

#### When Genitive and Verbal Adjective Coincide

For some verbal nouns, particularly those ending in `-t` (from second conjugation verbs), the substantival genitive and the verbal adjective have the same form.

*   `oscailt` -> genitive `oscailte` (of opening)
*   `imirt` -> genitive `imeartha` (of playing)

#### Rationale for the Verbal Adjective Genitive

A noun in the genitive case functions as an attribute, similar to an adjective. For example, in `teach Sheáin` (Seán's house), `Seáin` modifies `teach`. The verbal adjective is the primary attributive form of a verb (e.g., `teach tógtha` - a built house). When a verbal noun needs to act as an attribute (i.e., be in the genitive) while retaining its verbal sense, it logically adopts the form of the verb that is already designed for this purpose: the verbal adjective.

### The Dative Case

The dative case appears after most prepositions, most notably `ag` when forming the progressive aspect (e.g., `ag snámh` - swimming). For most verbal nouns, the dative form is identical to the nominative.

However, in some dialects, feminine verbal nouns of the second declension ending in a broad consonant (like `-ach`) have a distinct dative form ending in `-aigh`. These often describe sounds.

*   *Tá na capaill ag **seitrigh**.* (The horses are neighing.)
*   *Tá na ba ag **géimnigh**.* (The cows are mooing.)
*   *Thosaigh m’athair ag **béicigh**.* (My father began to shout.)
*   *Tá na bradáin ag **léimnigh**.* (The salmon are jumping.)

Masculine verbal nouns in `-ach`, such as `ceannach` (buying), do not have a special dative form.

### The Plural

Plural forms are only used when the verbal noun is treated as a countable noun.

*   **Verbal nouns in `-adh`:** The plural is formed by adding `-í` to the verbal adjective.
    *   `glanadh` (a cleaning) -> `glantaí` (cleanings)
*   **Verbal nouns in `-ú`:** The plural ending is `-uithe` or `-ithe`.
    *   `saothrú` (a cultivation) -> `saothruithe` (cultivations)
*   **Verbal nouns in `-áil`:** The plural ending is `-álacha`.
    *   `gabháil` (a capture) -> `gabhálacha` (captures)

## Short and Long Forms

Some verbs have two verbal noun forms: a short, common form and a longer, more specialised one. The longer forms often carry a more abstract, nominal, or durational meaning, while the shorter forms are more concrete and verbal.

*   `léamh` / `léitheoireacht` (reading)
*   `ní` / `níochán` (washing)
*   `léim` / `léimneach` (jumping)

The short form is typically used transitively (with an object), while the long form is used intransitively (without an object).

*   *Bhí sé ag **fí** an éadaigh.* (He was weaving the cloth.)
*   *Chaith sí an lá ag **fíochán**.* (She spent the day weaving.)
