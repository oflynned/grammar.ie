## Overview

In Irish, there are almost no true modal verbs whose meaning is solely modal. An exception is `féad` (can). Instead, modalities like "must", "can", and "should" are expressed through idiomatic phrases. These phrases often use the verb `bí` (to be) or the Copula `is`.

The conditional form of these auxiliary verbs (`bheadh` instead of `tá`, `ba` instead of `is`) is used to create a softened or hypothetical meaning, such as "would have to", "could", or "should".

Many of these phrases are formed with prepositions that introduce the logical subject of the sentence:
*   **`do` (to/for):** Used with the Copula to express an objective, factual, or moral obligation. For example, `b’éigean dom` (I had to).
*   **`le` (with):** Used with the Copula to express a subjective assessment, opinion, or desire. For example, `is maith liom` (I like).
*   **Impersonal:** Omitting the preposition and logical subject creates an impersonal meaning. For example, `is féidir` (it is possible).

Some verbs, like `caith` (to use, throw) and `glac` (to accept), take on a modal meaning in certain tenses, such as `caithfidh` (must) or `glacfadh` (should).

### Complementation

Modal expressions are typically followed by a verbal noun phrase or a `go/nach` clause.
*   *Ba mhaith liom teacht.* (I would like to come.)
*   *Ba mhaith liom go dtiocfá.* (I would like you to come.)

The choice between an infinitive construction and a `go/nach` clause can also distinguish between two types of modality for expressions of "must" and "can":
*   **Deontic Modality:** Expresses permission, obligation, or ability. It uses an infinitive construction.
    *   *Caithfidh sé an bád a phéinteáil.* (He must paint the boat.)
*   **Epistemic Modality:** Expresses a judgement about the truth or probability of a statement ("it must be the case that..."). It uses a `go/nach` clause.
    *   *Caithfidh go bpéinteálann sé an bád.* (It must be the case that he paints the boat.)

### Negation

Irish distinguishes clearly between negating the modal expression itself (external negation) and negating the action (internal negation).
*   **External Negation:** The particle `ní` negates the modal expression.
    *   *Ní chaithfidh sé imeacht.* (He does not have to go.)
*   **Internal Negation:** The particle `gan` negates the following verbal noun.
    *   *Caithfidh sé gan imeacht.* (He must not go.)

A double negation (`ní ... gan ...`) creates a reinforcing affirmative meaning.
*   *Ní féidir liom gan é a dhéanamh.* (I can't help but do it.)

Some modal expressions, like `ní mór dom` (I must), are formally negative but have a positive meaning. These cannot be negated further to mean "I don't have to".

## Must / Have to

### Using Caithfidh

The future tense of `caith` (`caithfidh`) is commonly used to mean "must".
*   *Caithfidh mé imeacht.* (I must go.)
*   *Caithfidh tú é a dhéanamh.* (You must do it.)

The conditional form, `chaithfinn`, can mean "I would have to" or "I had to". In Munster, other tenses like the present (`caithim`) are also used. Negation with `ní` is uncommon; forms of "need not" are preferred.

### Using Bí + ar

This construction literally means "is on me".
*   *Tá orm imeacht.* (I must go.)
*   *Bhéadh ar Sheán teacht.* (Seán would have to come.)

### Using Tar + ar

This construction literally means "came on me" and is often used for the past tense.
*   *Tháinig orm imeacht.* (I had to go.)
*   *Ní thiocfaidh ort pingin a íoc.* (You will not have to pay a penny.)

### Using Is éigean do

This copular phrase means "is compulsion to..." and is common in the past tense (`b’éigean do`).
*   *B’éigean dom é a dhéanamh.* (I had to do it.)

### Using Ní mór do

This phrase is formally negative but means "must".
*   *Ní mór duit bheith anseo.* (You must be here.)
*   *Ní mór dom dul ar scoil.* (I must go to school.)

Yes-no questions are formed with `cá` instead of `ní`, often carrying a rhetorical sense.
*   *Cá mór duit bheith anseo?* (Shouldn't you be here?)

### Using Ní foláir do/le

This is another formally negative phrase meaning "must". Using `do` implies objective necessity, while `le` implies a subjective assessment.
*   *Ní foláir dom é a rá.* (I must say it.) (Objective)
*   *Ní foláir liom é a rá.* (I think I should say it.) (Subjective)

### Other Constructions

*   **`bí` + Object + `le` + VN + `ag` + Subject:** Expresses necessity with transitive verbs.
    *   *Tá litir le scríobh agam.* (I have to write a letter.)
*   **`bí` + `ag` + Subject + `le` + VN (Ulster):** A construction likely adapted from English "have to".
    *   *Tá agam le dul.* (I have to go.)
*   **`bí` + `sé` + `d'fhiacha ar` + Subject + VN:** A less common form meaning "to be obliged to".
    *   *Tá sé d’fhiacha orm teacht.* (I am obliged to come.)

### Epistemic Must

To express an assumption ("it must be the case that..."), Irish uses several phrases followed by a clause.
*   *B’éigean dó gur thit a gcodladh orthu.* (They must have fallen asleep.)
*   *Ní foláir nó tá sí tinn.* (She must be sick.)
*   *Caithfidh go bhfuil sí ann.* (She must be there.)
*   *Ní féidir nó tá tú faoi dhraíocht.* (You must be under a spell.)

## Should / Ought to

### Using Is ceart do / Is cóir do

These two copular phrases are the most common ways to express "should". `Ceart` and `cóir` both mean "right" or "proper".
*   *Is ceart do Sheán teacht.* (Seán should come.)
*   *Ba chóir duit é a dhéanamh.* (You should do it.)

### Dialectal Forms

*   **`glacfadh` (Ulster):** The conditional of `glac` (to accept).
    *   *Ghlacfainn imeacht.* (I should better go.)
*   **`d'fhéad` (Ulster):** The past tense of `féad` (can), used to mean "should have".
    *   *D’fhéad tú labhairt leis.* (You should have spoken with him.)

### Other Constructions

*   **Expressing 'At least':** Phrases like `is gann do` or `is beag an rud do` express "should at least".
    *   *Is gann dó é.* (It is the least he should do.)
*   **Imperative:** The 3rd person imperative can be translated as "should".
    *   *Tagadh Seán!* (Seán should come!)
*   **Future Tense:** Used for questions involving offers or orders ("Shall I...?").
    *   *An gcabhróidh mé leat?* (Shall I help you?)
*   **Conditional:** The conditional tense can often be translated as "should".
    *   *Dhéanfadh sé cúis, dar liom.* (It should suffice, I think.)
*   **`is beite do` (Obsolete):** Uses the rare "participle of necessity" of `bí`.
    *   *Is beite do dhuine Dia a ghuí.* (One should pray to God.)

### Epistemic Should

To express an opinion about a likely event, the standard constructions are used, often with the conditional in the following clause.
*   *Ba cheart dó bheith tagtha faoi seo.* (He should have arrived by this time.)
*   *Ba cheart go mbeadh an teach críochnaithe.* (The house should be finished.)

Assumptions can also be expressed with verbs of thinking (`cheapfainn go...` - I would think that...) or phrases like `tá sé in ainm...` (he is said to...).
*   *Tá sé in ainm a bheith saibhir.* (He is said to be rich.)

## Had better / Should rather

These expressions are formed using the comparative form of adjectives like `maith` (good) or `cóir`/`ceart` (right).

*   **`is fearr do`:** Literally "is better for...".
    *   *Is fearr dom teacht.* (I had better come.)
    *   *B’fhearr do Sheán teacht.* (Seán had better come.)
*   **`is córa do`:** Literally "is more proper for...".
    *   *Sin mar is córa duit dul chun cainte leis.* (That's why you should all the more go and speak with him.)
*   **`is cirte do`:** Literally "is more right for...".
    *   *Aréir nó, ba chirte dom a rá, go moch ar maidin.* (Last night or, I should rather say, early this morning.)

## Can / Be able to

### Using Is féidir le/do

This is a very common way to express ability. Using `le` implies personal ability, while `do` suggests objective possibility.
*   *Is féidir liom Gaeilge a scríobh.* (I can write Irish.)
*   *Ní féidir do na páistí é a dhéanamh.* (It is not possible for the children to do it.)

### Using Tig le

`Tig` is a variant of `tagann` (comes). This construction is common in Connacht and Ulster. The past tense form `tháinig le` often implies success.
*   *Tig le Pól é a dhéanamh.* (Paul can do it.)
*   *Rinne mé chomh maith é agus a tháinig liom.* (I did it as well as I could.)

### Using Bí + in ann / ábalta

This construction means "to be able to" and is used to describe having the physical or mental capacity to do something.
*   *Tá mé in ann Gaeilge a scríobh.* (I can write Irish.)
*   *Tá mé ábalta Gaeilge a scríobh.* (I can write Irish.)

### Using Féad

`Féad` is one of the few true modal verbs in Irish. It is most common in Munster, especially in the future and conditional.
*   *Nach bhféadfá fanacht?* (Couldn't you stay?)
*   *Féadfaidh sé teacht lá ar bith.* (He can come any day.)

### Other Constructions

*   **`is acmhainn do`:** Expresses having the means or resources.
    *   *Is acmhainn dó teach a cheannach.* (He can afford to buy a house.)
*   **`téigh` + `ag`:** Implies success after an effort.
    *   *Chuaigh aige éirí ina sheasamh.* (He was able to stand up.)
*   **`bí` + `sé` + `ar cumas`:** A more formal way to say "to be able to".
    *   *Tá sé ar mo chumas é a dhéanamh.* (I can do it.)
*   **`faigh`:** The verb `faigh` (to get) can mean "to manage to do something".
    *   *Fuair sé an obair a dhéanamh.* (He was able to do the work.)
*   **Innate Abilities:** Some abilities are expressed using the "have" construction (`tá ... agam`).
    *   *Tá Gaeilge agam.* (I can speak Irish.)
    *   *Tá snámh agam.* (I can swim.)

### Negation and 'Cannot Help'

Ability is negated by negating the main verb or copula (`Ní féidir liom...`). A double negation (`ní féidir liom gan...`) means "cannot help but...".
*   *Ní féidir liom gan é a dhéanamh.* (I can't help but do it.)
*   *Níor fhéad mé gan gáire.* (I couldn't help laughing.)

### Epistemic Can

To express possibility ("it may be that..."), the modal expression is followed by a `go/nach` clause. The logical subject is usually omitted.
*   *Thiocfadh dó go dtearnadh botún.* (It could be that a mistake was made.)
*   *B’fhéidir go bhfuil sé ann.* (Perhaps it is there.)

## May / Be allowed to

### Using Bí + cead + ag

This is the standard way to express permission. `Cead` means "permission".
*   *Tá cead ag an bhfear fuisce a ól.* (The man is allowed to drink whiskey.)
*   *An bhfuil cead agam dul go dtí an leithreas?* (May I go to the toilet?)

### Using Ceadmhach

The adjective `ceadmhach` (permissible, allowed) can be used with `bí` or the Copula.
*   *Tá sé ceadmhach agam labhairt leo.* (I am allowed to speak with them.)
*   *Ní ceadmhach duit bean do dhearthár a bheith agat.* (You are not allowed to have your brother's wife.)

### Using Féadfaidh

The future of `féad` (`féadfaidh`) can be used to grant permission. The conditional (`bhféadfainn`) is used for polite requests.
*   *Féadfaidh tú imeacht anois.* (You may go now.)
*   *An bhfhéadfainn tuilleadh a bheith agam?* (May I have some more?)

### Using Is miste le/do

This phrase, literally meaning "to mind something", is used in polite questions for permission.
*   *An miste dom labhairt leat?* (May I speak with you?)
*   *Ní miste.* (Yes, you may. / I don't mind.)

### Expressing 'Must not'

Prohibition is usually expressed by negating `cead`.
*   *Níl cead agat imeacht.* (You may not go.)

Alternatively, an internal negation with `gan` can be used with a "must" construction, or a simple negative imperative.
*   *Caithfidh tú gan imeacht.* (You must not go.)
*   *Ná himigh!* (Don't go!)

## Like / May

### Using Is maith le / Is breá le

This is the most common way to express liking something. `Is breá le` is a more emphatic form. The conditional `ba mhaith liom` means "I would like".
*   *Is maith liom beoir.* (I like beer.)
*   *Ba mhaith liom fuisce a ól.* (I would like to drink whiskey.)
*   *Ba bhreá liom fuisce a ól.* (I would very much like to drink whiskey.)

### Using Taitin

The verb `taitin` means "to please". The thing liked is the grammatical subject.
*   *Taitníonn iasc liom.* (I like fish. Literally: Fish pleases with me.)
*   *Thaitin an capall leis.* (He liked the horse.)

### Other Constructions

*   **`bí` + `dúil` + `ag` + Subject + `i` + Object:** Expresses a deep liking or fondness.
    *   *Tá dúil agam sa tsiúl.* (I like hiking.)
*   **Optative:** Wishes ("May God help us!") are expressed with the present subjunctive.
    *   *Go bhfóire Dia orainn!* (May God help us!)

## Prefer / Would rather

### Using Is fearr le

This uses the comparative of `maith` (good) and literally means "is better with me".
*   *Is fearr liom tae ná caife.* (I prefer tea to coffee.)
*   *B’fhearr liom titim sa chomhrac.* (I would rather fall in battle.)

### Using Is measa le

Interestingly, `measa` (worse) can be used with a similar meaning to `fearr`, focusing on the potential loss of the preferred item.
*   *Is measa liom Seán a chailliúint ná Séamas.* (I would rather lose Séamas than Seán. Literally: Losing Seán is worse with me than Séamas.)

### Using Is rogha le

The noun `rogha` (choice) can be used instead of `fearr`.
*   *Is rogha liom imeacht anois.* (I would rather go now.)

## Want

### Using Bí + ó

This construction literally means "is from me".
*   *Tá braon fuisce uaim.* (I want some whiskey.)
*   *Tá uaim fuisce a ól.* (I want to drink whiskey.)

### Using Teastaigh

This verb can mean both "want" and "need", depending on context. It literally means "to be away/missing from".
*   *Teastaíonn arán uaidh.* (He wants/needs bread.)
*   *Teastaíonn uaim fuisce a ól.* (I want to drink whiskey.)

### Using Iarr

The verb `iarr` (to ask, request, try) is often used to mean "want", especially in the progressive aspect.
*   *Níl mé ag iarraidh éirí go fóill.* (I don't want to get up yet.)
*   *An bhfuil tú ag iarraidh seacláide?* (Do you want chocolate?)

### Other Constructions

*   **`is mian/áil/toil le`:** Copular phrases using nouns for "desire" or "will".
    *   *Is mian liom é a dhéanamh.* (I want/wish to do it.)
*   **`bí` + `fonn` + `ar`:** Expresses "to feel like" doing something.
    *   *Tá fonn orm é a dhéanamh.* (I feel like doing it.)
*   **Expressing Regret:** Retrospective wishes about missed opportunities are expressed with phrases of regret.
    *   *Is mairg nár fhan sa bhaile.* (I wish I had stayed at home.)

## Need

The concept of "need" often overlaps with "want" and "must". "Not need" is the most common way to express "not have to".

### Using Teastaigh

As mentioned under "Want", `teastaigh` can mean both "want" and "need".
*   *Tá sé ag teastáil go mór uaim.* (I need it urgently.)

### Using Ní gá do

This is the most common way to express "not have to" or "not need to". It is the negative of `is gá do` (it is necessary to).
*   *Ní gá duit é a dhéanamh.* (You don't have to/need to do it.)
*   *Ní gá duit ach clárú.* (You only need to register.)

### Other Constructions

*   **`bí` + `gá` + `ag` + Subject + `le` + Object:** Used for needing a specific object.
    *   *Tá gá aige leis an bpeann.* (He needs the pen.)
*   **`bí` + Object + `de dhíth ar` + Subject:** Another common phrase for needing something.
    *   *Tá braon fuisce de dhíth orm.* (I need a drop of whiskey.)
*   **`ní call do` (Connacht):** Used instead of `ní gá do`.
    *   *Ní call duit a theacht ar ais.* (You don't need to come back.)
*   **`níl feidhm ar/do` (Ulster):** Literally "there is no use for...".
    *   *Níl feidhm dom a rá go...* (I don't need to mention that...)

## Have

Irish has no single verb for "to have". Possession and states are expressed using the verb `bí` with different prepositions.

### General Possession (Bí + ag)

For general, alienable possession (things you can give away), `ag` (at) is used.
*   *Tá teach agam.* (I have a house.)
*   *Níl carr againn.* (We don't have a car.)

This form is also used for certain abilities.
*   *Tá Gaeilge agam.* (I can speak Irish.)
*   *Tá a fhios agam.* (I know.)

### Physical and Psychological States (Bí + ar)

For inalienable possession (body parts, components, illnesses, emotions), `ar` (on) is used.
*   *Tá slaghdán ar an gcailín.* (The girl has a cold.)
*   *Bhí áthas ar an bhfear.* (The man was happy.)
*   *Tá féasóg ar an seanfhear.* (The old man has a beard.)
*   *Cén t-ainm atá ort?* (What is your name?)

### Ownership (Is + le)

To state ownership emphatically, the Copula is used with `le` (with).
*   *Is liom an carr seo.* (This car is mine.)
*   *Is le Pól an teach.* (The house belongs to Paul.)

## The Verb Bí as an Auxiliary

The verb `bí` is the most common auxiliary verb in Irish. It is used for:
*   **Existence:** *Tá sé ann.* (He exists / He is there.)
*   **States:** Describing location, time, and qualities with adjectives. *Tá sé tinn.* (He is sick.)
*   **Aspect:** Forming the progressive, perfect, and prospective aspects.
    *   *Tá sé ag ól.* (He is drinking.)
    *   *Tá sé tar éis ól.* (He has just drunk.)
    *   *Tá sé ar tí ól.* (He is about to drink.)
*   **Classification:** *Tá sé ina mhúinteoir.* (He is a teacher.)
*   **'Have' Constructions:** As detailed above.
*   **Modal Constructions:** As a core part of many modal phrases.

## The Verb Déan as an Auxiliary

The verb `déan` (to do, make) can act as an auxiliary verb with a verbal noun, often for emphasis or to avoid complex conjugations.

Its primary uses include:
*   **Insistence:** *Déan é a cheannach!* (Go on and buy it!)
*   **Deliberateness:** *Déanfaidh siad cuid acu a mharú.* (They will actually kill some of them.)
*   **Promises:** *Déanfaidh mé é a chloisint!* (I will listen to it, I promise!)
*   **Semi-negative Sentences:** To express "only".
    *   *Ní dhearna sé ach gáire.* (But he only laughed.)
*   **Emphasis:** By fronting the verbal noun in a cleft sentence.
    *   *An fear a fheiceáil a rinne tú.* (You saw the man.)
*   **Progressive Passive:** *Tá uasghrádú á dhéanamh ar an suíomh seo.* (This site is currently being updated.)
*   **Short Answers (Munster):** As a substitute for the main verb.
    *   *Ar ólais an tae? - Dheineas.* (Did you drink the tea? - Yes.)

`Déan` can also be used intransitively to mean "to become".
*   *Rinne bean di.* (She became a woman.)
*   *Rinne fíon den uisce.* (The water became wine.)
