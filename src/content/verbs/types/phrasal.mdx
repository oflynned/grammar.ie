## Overview

In Old Irish, verbs were often modified by prefixes that could significantly alter their meaning. This is similar to how prefixes work in other languages. While this system is no longer productive in Modern Irish, some common verbs are remnants of this, such as `abair` (from `ad` + `beir`) or `tabhair` (from `to` + `beir`).

Today, only a few prefixes like `ath-` (re-), `frith-` (counter-), and `neamh-` (un-/non-) are commonly used. Instead, Modern Irish uses phrasal verbs and light verb constructions to expand the meaning of a small set of core verbs.

## Phrasal Verbs

Phrasal verbs, or `briathra frásacha`, are fixed combinations of a verb with prepositions and adverbs. Together, they carry a specific meaning that can differ greatly from the verb's original sense. The meaning is often highly context-dependent. This allows a few verbs, such as `cuir` and `bain`, to be used in a multitude of ways.

### Examples with Bain

The verb `bain` literally means "to pull (out)" or "extract".

- *Bhain mé na prátaí.* - I harvested the potatoes.

When combined with prepositions or adverbs, its meaning changes.

- **`bain amach`** (to pull out, obtain, reach): *Bhain mé an teach amach.* - I reached the house.
- **`bain ar`** (to get someone to do something): *Bhain mé air a theacht liom.* - I got him to come with me.
- **`bain as`** (to take out of, get out of): *Bhain sé seachtain asam é a dhéanamh.* - It took me a week to do it.
- **`bain chuig/chun`** (to start, begin with): *Bhain mé chun reatha.* - I started to run.
- **`bain de`** (to take away, remove from, reduce): *Bhain mé an siúl den bhád.* - I reduced the speed of the boat.
- **`bain do/le`** (to touch, concern): *Ná bain dó!* - Don't touch it! *Tá costas ag baint leis.* - There are costs associated with it.
- **`bain faoi`** (to settle, stay): *Bhain mé fúm san áit seo.* - I settled in this place.
- **`bain ó`** (to control, restrain, sharpen): *Bain uait!* - Control yourself! *Bhain mé ón scian.* - I sharpened the knife.

### Examples with Cuir

The verb `cuir` literally means "to put" or "place".

- **`cuir síos ar`** (to describe, report): *Chuir sé síos ar an gcuairt a thug sé uirthi.* - He reported on his visit to her.
- **`cuir isteach ar`** (to interrupt, disturb; to apply for): *Chuir sé isteach ar an phost.* - He applied for the post.
- **`cuir siar`** (to put back, postpone): Used to defer an event.
- **`cuir suas`** (to put up, erect): *Chuir sí suas an scáth fearthainne.* - She put up the umbrella.
- **`cuir suas le`** (to put up with, tolerate): *Chuir sé suas leis ar feadh deich nóiméad.* - He put up with it for 10 minutes.

### Verbal Nouns from Phrasal Verbs

Verbal nouns formed from phrasal verbs retain the accompanying preposition or adverb.

- `éirigh` (to rise) becomes `éirigh amach` (to rebel, revolt). The verbal noun is `an t-éirí amach` (the uprising), as in *Éirí Amach na Cásca* (the Easter Rising).
- `cuir` (to put) becomes `cuir as` (to deprive someone of something). The verbal noun phrase is `cur as seilbh` (dispossession, literally "putting out of possession").
- `bain` (to pull) becomes `bain le` (to concern). The verbal noun phrase is `baint le` (connection with), as in *Níl baint aige leis* (He has nothing to do with it).
