## Overview

Light verbs, or `briathra éadroma`, are general-purpose verbs that combine with nouns and prepositions to express a wide range of actions and states. Irish is a noun-oriented language, often preferring these constructions over specific lexical verbs.

Common light verbs include `bain`, `bí`, `cuir`, `déan`, `faigh`, `lig`, `tabhair`, and `tar`.

### Types of Light Verb Constructions

These constructions serve several functions:

- **Essential Formations**: Used for concepts that are primarily expressed this way.
  - *Rinne sé gáire.* - He laughed. (Literally: "He made laughter.")
  - *Tá a fhios agam.* - I know it. (Literally: "Is his knowledge at me.")
- **Punctual Events**: Used to describe a singular, specific action.
  - *Thug sé léim.* - He made a jump. (Instead of *Léim sé* - He jumped.)
  - *Bhain sé croitheadh as an mbuidéal.* - He shook the bottle (once). (Literally: "He took a shake out of the bottle.")
- **Causative Actions**: Used to describe causing someone or something to do an action.
  - *Bainfidh mé léim aisti.* - I will make her jump.
  - *Baineann sé allas asam.* - It makes me sweat. (Literally: "It takes sweat out of me.")
- **Auxiliary Functions**: Used for periphrasis or emphasis, especially with `déan`.
  - *Iad a cheannach a rinne sé.* - He bought them. (An emphatic form of *Cheannaigh sé iad*.)

### Expressing Emotions and States

Many emotions and physical states are described not with verbs or adjectives but with nouns. These nouns are typically assigned to a person using the verb `bí` with the preposition `ar` (on).

Common emotion and state nouns include: `áthas` (joy), `brón` (sorrow), `eagla` (fear), `fearg` (anger), `ocras` (hunger), and `tart` (thirst).

The pattern changes depending on whether someone has, is made to have, or develops an emotion.

| Concept               | Formula                                    | Example               | Translation (Literal) |
|:----------------------|:-------------------------------------------|:----------------------|:----------------------|
| Having an emotion     | `Tá` [emotion] `ar` [person]               | *Tá áthas orm.*       | Is joy on-me.         |
| Causing an emotion    | `Cuireann` [agent] [emotion] `ar` [person] | *Chuir sé áthas orm.* | Put it joy on-me.     |
| Developing an emotion | `Tagann` [emotion] `ar` [person]           | *Tháinig áthas orm.*  | Came joy on-me.       |

Here is the pattern with `eagla` (fear):

- *Tá eagla orm rompu.* - I am afraid of them.
- *Chuir sé eagla orm rompu.* - He made me afraid of them.
- *Tháinig eagla orm rompu.* - I became afraid of them.

### Further Examples

- *Bhain sé feidhm as an leabhar.* - He used the book. (Literally: "He took function out of the book.")
- *Bhain mé sult as an turas.* - I enjoyed the trip. (Literally: "I took pleasure out of the trip.")
- *Thug sé cúnamh dom.* - He helped me. (Literally: "He gave help to me.")
- *Thug sí aird air anois.* - Now she paid attention to him. (Literally: "She gave attention on him now.")
- *Ná déan dearmad air.* - Don't forget it! (Literally: "Make not forgetting on it.")
- *Lig sé fead.* - He whistled. (Literally: "He let a whistle.")

Some constructions are purely idiomatic and cannot be understood from their parts. A notable example is with `deara`:

- *Thug sé faoi deara é.* - He noticed it.
    - Dialectal variations include `fé ndeara`, `faoi ndeara`, and `fá deara`.
- *Chuir sé faoi deara dom é a dhéanamh.* - He/it caused me to do it.
