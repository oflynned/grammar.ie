In contrast to irregular verbs, which have a multitude of forms, defective verbs are verbs that cannot be fully conjugated. They only exist in a few forms, and some only occur in a single form.

Common defective verbs include:
- `feadair` (to know)
- `ar` / `arsa` (to say)
- `dar` (to seem)
- `dóbair` (to almost happen)
- `aitim` (I beseech)
- `tarlaigh` (to happen)
- `téana` (come)
- `ráinig` / `ráinigh` (reached)
- the copula `is` (is)

## Feadair (to know)

### Overview

`Feadair` is the only surviving deponent verb in Irish, meaning it is conjugated like a passive verb but has an active meaning. It is tenseless, so `feadramar` can mean "we know" or "we knew".

Today, `feadair` is used almost exclusively in questions, answers, and negative sentences.

- *An bhfeadair tú?* (Do you know?)
- *Ní fheadar.* (I don't know / I wonder.)

The verb often merges with the negative particle `ní` to form `n’fheadair`.

- *N’fheadar an bhfuil sé ann fós.* (I wonder if it is still there.)

When used with the preposition `do`, its meaning becomes "to be able to tell someone".

- *An bhfeadraís seo dom?* (Can you tell me this?)

### Conjugation

| Person | Verb form | Alternatives |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1st Sing. | `feadar` | `feadair mé` |
| 2nd Sing. | `feadraís` | `feadair tú` |
| 3rd Sing. | `feadair sé` | |
| 1st Plur. | `feadramar` | `feadramair`, `feadair muid` |
| 2nd Plur. | `feadrabhar` | `feadrabhair`, `feadair sibh` |
| 3rd Plur. | `feadradar` | `feadraid`, `feadair siad` |

### Historical and Dialectal Notes

The forms without an `-r-` in the plural (`feadamar`, `feadabhar`, `feadadar`) were common in Classical Irish. The `-r-` was later adopted from the singular forms.

`Feadair` is used mainly in Munster, where a vowel is often inserted between the `-d-` and `-r-` in pronunciation, so `feadramair` sounds like `feadaramair`.

In most dialects, the construction `tá a fhios ag duine` is far more common for "to know".

- *Tá a fhios agam.* (I know. Literally: 'is his knowledge at-me'.)

## Ar / Arsa (to say)

### Usage Rules

The verb `ar` or `arsa` is only used after direct speech and is tenseless. It must be used with the emphatic forms of pronouns (`mise`, `tusa`, `seisean`, etc.).

- `Ar` is used only before 3rd person pronouns: `ar seisean`, `ar sise`, `ar siadsan`.
- `Arsa` is used in all other cases: `arsa mise`, `arsa tusa`, `arsa an fear`.

### Examples

- *“Tuigim anois”, arsa Seán.* ("I understand now", said Seán.)
- *“Ní thuigeann tú ar chor ar bith”, ar sise.* ("You don't understand at all", she said.)

### Alternatives

For indirect speech, another verb like `abair` (to say) must be used.

- *Dúirt sé léi gur thuig sé.* (He told her that he understood.)
- *Dúirt sí leis nár thuig sé ar chor ar bith.* (She told him that he didn't understand at all.)

The verb `abair` can also be used instead of `ar`/`arsa` with direct speech, often in the present tense form `a deir`.

## Dar (to seem)

### Usage

This verb exists only in the single, tenseless form `dar`. It is always combined with the preposition `le` (with).

The construction `dar liom` means "it seems to me" or "I think". It can appear after a statement or before a `go`/`nach` clause.

### Examples

- *Dar leis an bhfear go bhfuil sé ann.* (The man thought that he was there.)
- *Sin a bhfuil le rá, dar liom.* (That is all there is to say, it seems to me.)

## Dóbair (it almost happened)

### Forms and Usage

This verb, which has variants like `d’fhóbair`, `fhobair`, and `hóbair`, only exists as a 3rd person singular preterite form. It now functions more like an adverb meaning "almost" or "nearly".

In Munster, it is often used with the copula: `ba dhóbair`. It can be followed by an infinitive construction (with the subject marked by `do`) or a `go`/`nach` subordinate clause.

### Examples

- *Dóbair dom titim.* (I almost fell.)
- *Dóbair go dtitfinn.* (I almost fell.)

## Aitim (I beseech)

This verb only occurs in two forms, both in the 1st person present indicative: `aitim` (I beseech) and `aitimid` (we beseech). Alternative forms include `aicim` and `aicimid`.

## Tarlaigh (to happen)

### Forms and Rules

`Tarlaigh` only occurs in the 3rd person. Its verbal noun is `tarlú` (happening, occurrence).

The preterite form is irregular: `tharla`. When followed by a clause, it does not use a dummy subject like `sé`. The person affected by the event can be introduced with the preposition `do`. The phrase `ó tharla (go)` generally means "since" or "because".

### Examples

- *Tharla timpiste.* (An accident happened.)
- *Má tharlaíonn go mbeidh tú ag caint leis.* (If it happens that you are talking to him.)
- *Tharla dom a bheith ann nuair a tháinig sé.* (I happened to be there when he came.)
- *Agus ó tharla muid ag fanacht le fada le freagra.* (And since we had to wait a long time for an answer.)

## Téana (come)

### Forms and Usage

`Téana` only exists in the imperative.

- *Téana ort!* (Come on!)
- *Téanaigí oraibh!* (Come on (plural)!)

The 1st person plural form `téanam` is common.

- *Téanam abhaile!* (Let's go home!)

The verbal noun is `téanachtaint`, used for the imperative in indirect speech.

- *Dúirt sé liom téanachtaint leis.* (He told me to come with him.)

## Ráinig / Ráinigh (reached)

### Overview and Forms

`Ráinig` is rare today but appears in older literature, primarily from Munster. It is related to `tháinig`, the preterite of `tar` (to come).

In Munster, `ráinig` evolved into `ráinigh`, which appears like a regular 2nd conjugation verb. From this form, other tenses are sometimes formed, such as `ráiníonn sé` (he reaches).

### Functions

This verb has several meanings, including to reach, to come, to happen, or to manage.

- *Conas mar a ráinig go raibh tú anseo?* (How did it happen that you were here?)
- *Ráinigh liom é a dhéanamh.* (I managed to do it.)

In most cases, it has been replaced by other verbs like `sroich` (to reach) or `tar` (to come). It is well-known from its use in the Irish national anthem: *buíon dár slua thar toinn do ráinig chughainn* ("some of our host came to us over the waves").

### Historical Remnants

The verbal noun `riachtain` survives in related words like `riachtanach` (necessary) and `riachtanas` (necessity). The subjunctive form survives in the rare preposition `go ruige` (until).
