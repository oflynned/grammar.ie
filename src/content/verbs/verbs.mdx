## Core Concepts of Irish Verbs

Understanding a few key principles is essential before learning how to conjugate Irish verbs. Unlike English, Irish verbs have some unique structural features.

### No Infinitive
Irish does not have a direct equivalent to the English infinitive, such as "to go" or "to see". Instead, this function is served by the **verbal noun** (an t-ainm briathartha).

### Verb Root and Stem
The **root** is the most basic form of a verb. The **stem** is the version of the root used for conjugation, to which endings are added. Often, the root and stem are the same. For example, the root of `cuir` (to put) is `cuir`.

### Citation Form
The form of the verb you will find in a dictionary is typically the 2nd person singular imperative. This is the command form you would use when speaking to one person, for example, `dún` (close!).

### Broad and Slender Stems
Verb stems, like nouns, end in either a broad vowel (`a`, `o`, `u`) or a slender vowel (`e`, `i`). The endings added to the verb must follow the "caol le caol agus leathan le leathan" rule, meaning slender endings attach to slender stems, and broad endings attach to broad stems.

### Initial Mutations
Verbs are subject to lenition and eclipsis. These changes occur at the beginning of the verb and are usually triggered by particles that precede them, such as those used for questions or negation.

### Synthetic and Analytic Forms
Irish verbs have two ways of expressing person and number.
*   **Synthetic** forms include the person in the verb ending itself. For example, `molaim` means "I praise".
*   **Analytic** forms use a separate pronoun after the verb. For example, `molann mé` also means "I praise".
In Modern Irish, analytic forms are more common, especially outside of the first person.

### Dependent and Independent Forms
For many tenses, there are two distinct forms of the verb.
*   The **independent** form is used in simple positive statements.
*   The **dependent** form is used after certain particles, most commonly in questions and negative statements. For example, `chonaic mé` (I saw) becomes `ní fhaca mé` (I did not see).

### The Relative Form
The relative form is a special verb form used in relative clauses to connect back to a noun. It is often used to mean "that" or "who". For example, *an fear a **cheannaíonn** an t-arán* (the man who **buys** the bread).

### The Autonomous Form
The autonomous form (an saorbhriathar) is an impersonal verb form. It is used when the agent performing the action is unknown, unimportant, or generalised. It can be translated as "one does", "they do", or with a passive voice construction like "it is done".

### Echo Forms
When answering a yes/no question, it is common to "echo" the verb from the question in your answer, rather than simply saying "yes" or "no". For example, *An ólann tú tae?* (Do you drink tea?) would be answered with `Ólaim` (I drink) or `Ní ólaim` (I don't drink).

## Conjugation Patterns

Irish verbs are grouped into two main conjugation classes based on their structure.

### First Conjugation
This class contains most verbs whose stem is a single syllable, such as `dún` (to close) and `ól` (to drink).

### Second Conjugation
This class contains verbs with two or more syllables. Most of these verbs end in `-aigh` or `-igh`, such as `ceannaigh` (to buy) or `imsigh` (to depart).

### Special Cases
*   **Monosyllabic Verbs in -igh**: A small group of single-syllable verbs ending in `-igh`, such as `nigh` (to wash) and `luigh` (to lie down), follow a specific pattern within the first conjugation.
*   **Verbs in -áil**: Verbs whose verbal noun ends in `-áil`, such as `siopadóireacht` (shopping), often derive from a second conjugation verb stem like `siopadóirigh`.

### Traditional Conjugation and Dialects
The conjugation patterns presented in the Official Standard (An Caighdeán Oifigiúil) are a simplification of more complex traditional systems. Dialectal usage often preserves a wider variety of synthetic forms and other older features.

## Building Verb Phrases

Verbs rarely stand alone and are often part of a larger phrase involving particles and other verb types.

### Tense, Mood, Voice, and Aspect
*   **Tense** indicates when an action occurs (past, present, future).
*   **Mood** indicates the speaker's attitude towards the action (indicative, imperative, subjunctive).
*   **Voice** indicates the relationship between the subject and the action (active, passive/autonomous).
*   **Aspect** describes the duration or completion of an action (e.g., the progressive aspect using `bí` + `ag` + verbal noun).

### Verbal Particles
These are small words like `an`, `ní`, `go`, and `nach` that come before a verb to form questions, negatives, and subordinate clauses. They are a primary trigger for initial mutations.

### Modal and Auxiliary Verbs
Concepts like "can", "must", or "should" (modal verbs) are not expressed with single verbs in Irish. Instead, they are formed using constructions, often involving a verb like `is` or `tá` and a verbal noun. An auxiliary verb, like `bí` in English, helps form tenses or aspects.

### Phrasal and Light Verbs
*   **Phrasal verbs** combine a verb with a preposition to create a new meaning, like `tar ar` (to come across/find).
*   **Light verbs** are common verbs like `déan` (do) or `tabhair` (give) that combine with a noun to express an action, where the noun carries most of the meaning. For example, `déan staidéar` (to do study / to study).

## Irregular and Defective Verbs

A small but very common group of verbs do not follow the standard conjugation patterns.

### The 11 Irregular Verbs
These verbs have unique forms across different tenses and must be memorised.
*   `abair` (to say)
*   `beir` (to bear, to catch)
*   `bí` (to be)
*   `clois` / `cluin` (to hear)
*   `déan` (to do/make)
*   `faigh` (to get)
*   `feic` (to see)
*   `ith` (to eat)
*   `tabhair` (to give)
*   `tar` (to come)
*   `téigh` (to go)

### Defective Verbs
Defective verbs are verbs that lack a full set of conjugations. They can only be used in a limited number of tenses or forms. An example is `arsa` (said), which is only used in the past tense to introduce direct speech.

## Related Verb Forms

Verbs can be used to create other types of words, namely verbal nouns and verbal adjectives.

### Verbal Noun
The verbal noun (an t-ainm briathartha) is the name of the action. It functions as both the "-ing" form (e.g., "running") and the infinitive form ("to run") in English.

### Verbal Adjective
The verbal adjective (an aidiacht bhriathartha) functions similarly to an English past participle. It describes a noun that has had the action of the verb done to it, such as `an fhuinneog **bhriste**` (the **broken** window).
