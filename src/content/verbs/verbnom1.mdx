## Overview

The Irish verbal noun has two main syntactic roles. It can be used in an "infinitival" sense, forming verb-like phrases, or in a "substantival" sense, acting as a regular noun.

This article focuses on the infinitival uses, which can be categorised as:
- **Periphrastic Constructions**: The verbal noun is part of the main predicate, often with the verb `bí`, to express different aspects like continuous or prospective actions.
- **Infinitive Constructions**: The verbal noun forms a non-finite clause that acts as a subject or object within a larger sentence.
- **Attributive Use**: The verbal noun acts as an attribute to a noun, similar to an English present participle.

## Periphrastic Constructions

Periphrastic constructions use the auxiliary verb `bí` with a preposition and a verbal noun to express aspects of an action that simple tenses cannot. The basic structure is:

**Form of `bí` + Subject + Preposition + Verbal Noun**

The preposition used determines the aspect, such as progressive (ongoing), prospective (intended), or terminative (completed).

### The Progressive Form

The progressive form describes actions that are in progress, much like the "-ing" forms in English. It is the most common way to express present actions in colloquial Irish. It is formed with the verb `bí`, the preposition `ag`, and a verbal noun.

#### Pronunciation of `ag`

In the progressive form, `ag` is often shortened in speech.
- **[əg]** or **[əg′]** after a consonant and before a vowel.
  - *Tá Seán ag ól.* (Seán is drinking.)
- **[g]** or **[g′]** between vowels.
  - *Tá tú ag ól.* (You are drinking.)
- **[ə]** between consonants.
  - *Tá Pádraig ag léamh.* (Pádraig is reading.)
- **Silent** after a vowel and before a consonant.
  - *Tá tú ag tuiscint.* (You understand.)

Sometimes, the spelling `a’` is used to reflect the pronunciation, as in *Tá Seán a’ tuiscint*.

#### Tenses of the Progressive

The verb `bí` can be in any tense or mood, creating a full range of progressive forms.

| Tense/Mood       | Example                 | Translation              |
|:-----------------|:------------------------|:-------------------------|
| Present          | *Tá sé ag léamh.*       | He is reading.           |
| Habitual Present | *Bíonn sé ag léamh.*    | He (usually) is reading. |
| Preterite        | *Bhí sé ag léamh.*      | He was reading.          |
| Imperfect        | *Bhíodh sé ag léamh.*   | He used to be reading.   |
| Future           | *Beidh sé ag léamh.*    | He will be reading.      |
| Conditional      | *Bheadh sé ag léamh.*   | He would be reading.     |
| Imperative       | *Bí ag léamh!*          | Be reading!              |
| Subjunctive      | *Go raibh sé ag léamh.* | May he be reading.       |
| Verbal Noun      | *bheith ag léamh*       | to be reading            |

#### Progressive Form without `bí` (Small Clauses)

In certain contexts, the form of `bí` is omitted, leaving a "small clause" structure. This is common with specific types of verbs.

- **Verbs of perception** (`feic`, `clois`, `éist le`):
  - *Feicim an fear ag foghlaim.* (I see the man learning.)
  - *Cloiseann tú Séan ag teacht.* (You hear Seán coming.)
  - *D’éist mé léi ag ceol.* (I listened to her singing.)

- **Verbs of motion** (`téigh`, `tar`, `imigh`): These can describe the manner of motion or an action of purpose.
  - *Tagann siad ag siúl agus ag léim.* (They come walking and jumping.)
  - *Tagann sé ag foghlaim Gaeilge.* (He comes to learn Irish.)

- **Verbs of beginning, ending, or continuing** (`tosaigh`, `stad`, `lean`):
  - *Thosaigh mé ag ól.* (I began to drink.)
  - *Choinnigh sé ag cur ceisteanna.* (He kept on asking questions.)
  - *Stad mé ag caitheamh tobac.* (I stopped smoking.)

- **Causative `cuir`** (to make someone do something):
  - *Cuirfidh sé ag gáire thú.* (It will make you laugh.)
  - *Chuir mé ag scríobh na litreach é.* (I made him write the letter.)

- **With `agus`**: Often used to describe a simultaneous action.
  - *Agus é ag gáire thit sé go talamh.* (While he was laughing, he fell to the ground.)

#### The Verbal Noun in the Progressive

Almost any verbal noun can be used, with the notable exception of `bheith`. A phrase like *\*Tá mé ag beith cúramach\* is incorrect; you simply say *Tá mé cúramach* (I am careful).

After `ag`, the verbal noun is unlenited. `Ag` takes the dative case, so some verbal nouns may use a dative form in certain dialects, though this is rare.
- *Tá an capall ag seitrigh.* (The horse is neighing.) (dative of `seitreach`)

#### The Subject in the Progressive

The subject can be a pronoun or noun. The autonomous form of `bí` (`táthar`) is used for an impersonal "one" or to imply a passive sense without an agent.

- *Tá Pól ag teacht.* (Paul is coming.)
- *Táthar ag teacht.* (One is coming / People are coming.)
- *Táthar ag múineadh na teanga.* (The language is being taught.)

Some unaccusative verbs appear without a grammatical subject.
- *Tá ag éirí go maith liom.* (I am doing well.)

#### Intransitive and Transitive Forms

**Intransitive Progressive**
This form has no direct object. The structure is `bí` + Subject + `ag` + Verbal Noun.
- *Tá mé ag léamh.* (I am reading.)
- *Tá muid ag féachaint ar an teilifís.* (We are watching television.)

**Transitive Progressive**
This form includes a direct object. The structure changes depending on whether the object is a noun or a pronoun.

- **With a Noun Object**: The object follows the verbal noun and is in the genitive case.
  - Formula: `bí` + Subject + `ag` + VN + Genitive Object
  - *Tá mé ag léamh an nuachtáin.* (I am reading the newspaper.)
  - *Tá sí ag scríobh na litreach.* (She is writing the letter.)

- **With a Pronoun Object**: The preposition changes from `ag` to `do`, and the object is represented by a possessive pronoun placed before the verbal noun.
  - Formula: `bí` + Subject + `do` + Possessive Pronoun + VN
  - The combination `do` + `a` (his/her/their) becomes `á`.

- **With a Separated Object**: If the object is moved to the front (e.g., in a relative clause or for emphasis), the preposition `ag` changes to `a` (which causes lenition).
  - *Céard atá Pól a dhéanamh?* (What is Paul doing?)
  - *Is é an obair atá Pól a dhéanamh.* (It is the work that Paul is doing.)

#### Reflexive and Passive Forms

**Reflexive Progressive**
This is formed like the transitive progressive with a pronoun object, but with the addition of `féin` (self). The possessive pronoun must refer back to the subject.
- *Tá sé á bhualadh féin.* (He is hitting himself.)
- *Tá mé do mo réiteach féin.* (I am preparing myself.)

**Progressive Passive**
The structure is identical to the transitive progressive with a pronoun object, but the possessive pronoun is understood to be reflexive (referring back to the subject).
- *Tá an teach á thógáil.* (The house is being built. Literally: The house is at its building.)
- *Tá mé do mo bhualadh.* (I am being hit.)

This can lead to ambiguity. *Tá sé á dhíol* could mean "He is selling him" (active) or "He is being sold" (passive). Context is key. An agent can be added with `ag` to clarify the passive meaning.
- *Tá an teach á thógáil agam.* (The house is being built by me.)

#### Stative Verbs

A small group of verbs describing a state rather than an action use the preposition `i` (in) plus a possessive pronoun instead of `ag`.
- Formula: `bí` + Subject + `i` + Possessive Pronoun + VN
- These include `seasamh` (standing), `suí` (sitting), `luí` (lying), `cónaí` (living/dwelling), and `codladh` (sleeping).
  - *Tá mé i mo sheasamh.* (I am standing.)
  - *Tá sí ina cónaí i mBéal Feirste.* (She lives in Belfast.)
  - *Bí i do thost!* (Be quiet!)

#### Dialectal Variations

In Ulster, verbs of motion, beginning, and ending often use the preposition `a` (from `do`) with lenition instead of `ag` to show purpose.
- *Tháinig mé a ól.* (I came to drink.) (Standard: *Tháinig mé ag ól*)
- *Thosaigh sé a dh’éirí lag.* (He began to grow weak.)

This allows for a distinction in meaning that is not possible in other dialects:
- *D’imigh sé ag damhsa.* (He went away dancing - simultaneous action).
- *D’imigh sé a dhamhsa.* (He went away to dance - purpose).

### The Prospective Form

The prospective form describes actions that are intended, planned, or expected to happen in the future.

- **General Intention**: For actions that are not immediate, use `le` (Connacht/Ulster) or `chun` (Munster).
  - *Tá sé chun carr nua a cheannach.* (He intends to buy a new car.)
  - *An bhfuil sí le mé a fhágáil?* (Does she intend to leave me?)

- **Immediate Future ("about to")**: For actions that are imminent, use `ar tí` (Munster), `ar thob` (Connacht), or `réidh le` (Ulster).
  - *Bhí sé ar tí imeacht.* (He was about to leave.)
  - *Bhí Liam ar thob dul a chodladh.* (Liam was about to go to sleep.)

- **"Be going to"**: The structure `ag dul ag` + VN (or `ag dul a` + VN in Ulster) is used like "be going to" in English to express a strong intention or a very likely future event.
  - *Tá mé ag dul ag foghlaim Gaeilge.* (I am going to learn Irish.)
  - *Tá an cheist seo ag dul á hathphlé.* (This question is going to be discussed again.)

### The Perfect Form

The perfect aspect describes a recently completed action. It is formed with `tar éis` (after), or dialectally `i ndiaidh`.
- Formula: `bí` + Subject + `tar éis` + Object + `a` + VN
- *Tá mé tar éis an balla a phéinteáil.* (I have just painted the wall.)

To describe an action that was in progress and has just finished (Progressive Perfect), the verbal noun `bheith` is added.
- *Tá mé tar éis bheith ag péinteáil an bhalla.* (I have just been painting the wall.)

### Other Periphrastic Constructions

- **With `le`**: This construction expresses necessity, possibility, or duty. With transitive verbs, it has a passive meaning. With intransitive verbs, it is active and prospective.
  - *Tá an balla seo le péinteáil.* (This wall is to be painted.) (Passive)
  - *Tá obair le déanamh agam.* (I have work to do.) (Passive with agent)
  - *Tá mé le teacht.* (I am expected to come.) (Active)

- **With `gan`**: This expresses that something has not been done, often with a resultative sense (like the prefix "un-").
  - *Tá an leabhar gan scríobh fós.* (This book is still unwritten.)
  - *Bhí sí fada gan teacht.* (She had not come for a long time.)

- **With `ar`**: This is used with certain verbal nouns to describe a continuous state.
  - *Tá an siopa ar oscailt.* (The shop is open.)
  - *Tá an leabhar ar fáil.* (The book is available.)

## Infinitive Constructions

The verbal noun is also used to create non-finite clauses that function like infinitives in English. They can act as the subject or object of a sentence.

### Simple Infinitive Phrases

- **Intransitive (no object)**: This is simply the bare verbal noun.
  - *Ba mhaith liom siúl.* (I would like to walk.)
  - Negation uses `gan`: *Tá áthas orm gan fanacht.* (I am glad not to wait.)

- **Transitive (with an object)**: The object comes before the verbal noun, separated by the particle `a` (which causes lenition).
  - Formula: Object + `a` + VN
  - *Ba mhaith liom an leabhar a léamh.* (I would like to read the book.)
  - Negation: `gan` + Object + `a` + VN. *Is fearr gan an scéal a insint.* (It is better not to tell the story.)

### Infinitive Phrases with a Subject

Unlike in English, an Irish infinitive phrase can have its own subject. This allows it to function like a full subordinate clause. Dialects handle this differently.

- **Intransitive with a Subject**:
  - **Southern (Standard)**: Subject + `a` + VN. *Ba mhaith liom tú a phósadh.* (I would like you to marry.)
  - **Northern (Ulster)**: Subject + VN. *Ba mhaith liom tú pósadh.* (I would like you to marry.)
  - The Ulster form avoids ambiguity, as *Ba mhaith liom tú a phósadh* can also mean "I would like to marry you" (with `tú` as the object).

- **Transitive with a Subject**:
  - **Northern (Ulster)**: Subject + Object + `a` + VN. *Ba mhaith liom an fear an obair a dhéanamh.* (I would like the man to do the work.)
  - **Southern (Munster)**: Subject + `a` + VN + Genitive Object. *Ba mhaith liom an fear a dhéanamh na hoibre.* (I would like the man to do the work.)

### The Infinitive of Purpose

To express purpose ("in order to"), an infinitive phrase is introduced by a preposition, most commonly `le` or `chun`.
- *Tháinig mé anseo chun gloine a ól.* (I came here to drink a glass.)
- *D’oibrigh sé go crua le Gaeilge a fhoghlaim.* (He worked hard to learn Irish.)

After verbs of motion, a construction with `ag` (or `a` in Ulster) can also express purpose.
- *Chuaigh sé ag iascach.* (He went fishing.)

### Temporal Infinitive Phrases

To describe simultaneous, preceding, or subsequent actions, prepositions are used with an infinitive phrase where the logical subject is marked with `do`.
- **Simultaneity (`le linn`)**: *le linn dom an leabhar a léamh* (while I was reading the book)
- **Anteriority (`tar éis`)**: *tar éis dó an obair a chríochnú* (after he finished the work)
- **Posteriority (`roimh`)**: *roimh di imeacht* (before she left)

## Attributive and Substantival Use

### The Verbal Noun as an Attribute

The verbal noun can modify another noun, much like a present participle in English. The noun being modified is the agent of the verbal noun's action.
- *fear inste scéil* (a story-telling man / a storyteller)
- *cailín deas crúite na mbó* (the nice, cow-milking girl)
- *lucht foghlamtha na Gaeilge* (the learners of Irish)

### The Verbal Noun as a Noun

The verbal noun can be used as a regular noun, referring to the action itself as a concept. In this role, it can take an article, be pluralised, and be modified by adjectives.
- *Is rud maith é an léamh.* (Reading is a good thing.)
- *Fuair mé dhá ordú uaidh.* (I received two orders from him.)
