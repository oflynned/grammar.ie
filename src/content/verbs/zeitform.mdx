## Overview of the Verbal System

The tense of a verb determines the time of an action. Irish makes several distinctions, such as between the simple and habitual present, and the preterite and imperfect in the past.

Verbs have **simple tenses** (`aimsirí simplí`), which are formed with a specific verb ending, and **compound tenses** (`aimsirí comhshuite`), which are formed with the auxiliary verb `bí` to express further aspects like the progressive or perfect.

Simple tenses are traditionally divided into:
*   **Primary tenses** (`aimsirí príomha`): Present, Future, Subjunctive Present, and Imperative.
*   **Historical tenses** (`aimsirí stairiúla`): Preterite, Imperfect, Subjunctive Preterite, and Conditional.

A key feature of historical tenses is the use of a `d'-` prefix before vowels and lenition on consonants, a remnant of the old past verbal particle *do*.

## Tense (An Aimsir)

The indicative mood is the most common and has the most tenses. These are often referred to simply as "the tenses".

### The Present Tenses

In the present, a distinction is made between non-habitual and habitual actions, resulting in two present tenses.

*   **Simple Present** (`an aimsir láithreach`): Describes a punctual action happening at the current moment.
*   **Habitual Present** (`an aimsir ghnáthláithreach`): Describes an action that happens regularly (e.g., usually, always, often).

The verb `bí` has separate forms for each.

| Simple Present | Habitual Present |
| :--- | :--- |
| *Tá sé fuar anocht.* (It is cold tonight.) | *Bíonn sé fuar istoíche.* (It is cold at night.) |

For all other verbs, the form is the same, but the usage differs between literary and colloquial Irish.

*   **Literary Usage**: The present tense form can be either non-habitual or habitual, depending on the context. This is common in sports commentary, news, and formal rituals.
    *   *Cromann sí síos, ardaíonn an sliotar agus buaileann, agus téann sé thar an trasnán.* = She bends down, lifts the sliotar, strikes it, and it goes over the crossbar.
    *   *Baistím thú in ainm an Athar agus an Mhic agus an Spioraid Naoimh.* = I baptise you in the name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit.
*   **Colloquial Usage**: The present tense form is generally understood in two ways:
    *   With verbs of thinking, feeling, and perception, it can be non-habitual or habitual.
        *   *Feiceann sé anois.* = He sees now.
        *   *Feiceann sé go minic.* = He sees often.
    *   With all other verbs, it implies the habitual present. The simple, non-habitual present is expressed using the progressive aspect.
        *   *Ólaim tae le bainne.* = I (usually) drink tea with milk.
        *   *Tá mé ag ól tae le bainne.* = I am (currently) drinking tea with milk.

#### Special Uses of the Present

**Relative Form**
In a direct relative clause, a special relative form ending in `-s` is used in the 3rd person.
*   *an fear a ghlana(nn)s an carr* = the man who cleans the car

The relative form of `tá` is `atá`, and the relative form of `bíonn` is `a bhíos`.

**Present instead of Future**
Using the present tense for a future action is not common, except for fixed future events with the verb `bí`.
*   *Táimid ag filleadh amárach.* = We will return tomorrow.

Additionally, the future tense is generally not used after `má` (if) and `cha` (not); the habitual present is used instead.

**Tá instead of bíonn**
When an action or state is assumed to be ongoing, the simple present `tá` is used instead of the habitual `bíonn`.
*   *Tá mé i mo chónaí i nGaillimh.* = I live in Galway.

**The Gnomic Present**
The habitual present is used for general, timeless statements.
*   *Itheann na beithígh féar.* = Cows eat grass.

The simple present `tá` is used for circumstances that are always true.
*   *Tá an fharraige goirt.* = The sea is salty.

For punctual events that occur on specific occasions, the future is often used (gnomic future).
*   *Nuair atá an duilleog feoite, is beag an t-aithleá a scoiltfidh den chraobh í.* = When the leaf is withered, a breath of wind is enough to separate it from the branch.

**The Historical Present**
Used as a stylistic device in narratives to make past actions more vivid. In Ulster, the old ending `-(a)idh` is used instead of `-(e)ann`.
*   *Le sin preabaidh Sergeant Leland chun tosaigh, tarraingidh air scian is gearraidh sé suas an t-arán.* = With that, Sergeant Leland jumps forward, reaches for a knife and cuts the bread.

### The Past Tenses

#### The Preterite (An Aimsir Chaite)

The preterite describes a single, punctual action in the past and is the standard narrative tense. It often translates to the English simple past, present perfect, or past perfect.
*   *Thug sé dom é.* = He gave it to me. / He has given it to me. / He had given it to me.

Independent verb forms are lenited, a remnant of the past particle *do*. Before a vowel, a `d'-` prefix is used.
*   *Bhuail mé é.* = I struck him.
*   *D’ól mé é.* = I drank it.

#### The Imperfect (An Aimsir Neamhfhoirfe)

Also known as the habitual preterite, the imperfect describes a recurring or durative action in the past (e.g., "used to").
*   *Dhéanadh sé an obair go maith.* = He used to do the work well.
*   *D’óladh sé gach lá.* = He drank every day.

Compare the preterite and imperfect:
*   **Preterite**: *Bhuail sé é.* = He struck him (a single blow).
*   **Imperfect**: *Bhuaileadh sé é.* = He used to beat him (repeatedly or over time).

In Ulster, a periphrasis with `ba ghnách le` is often used instead.
*   *Ba ghnách leat an obair a dhéanamh go maith.* = You usually did the work well.

### The Future (An Aimsir Fháistineach)

The future tense describes actions that will happen.
*   *Rachaidh mé go Gaillimh.* = I will go to Galway.

It can also be used to:
*   **Express desire**: *Beidh deoch agam.* = I'll have a drink.
*   **Give instructions**: *Rachaidh tú síos go dtí an droichead.* = Go down to the bridge.
*   **Ask for instructions**: *An osclóidh mé an fhuinneog?* = Shall I open the window?
*   **Express ability**: *Ní inseofar an ghlóir fhlaithiúnais.* = The heavenly glory cannot be described.

As with the present, the future tense is replaced by the habitual present after `má` (if) and `cha` (not).
*   *Glanfaidh tú* (you will clean) becomes *má ghlanann tú* (if you clean).

## Mood (An Modh)

Mood indicates whether an action is real, wished for, conditional, or commanded.

### The Indicative (An Modh Táscach)

The indicative is the realis mood, used for factual statements and normal narration. It is the most frequent mood. In indirect speech, the indicative future changes to the conditional if the main clause is in a past tense.

#### The Indicative of Certainty

In conditional sentences, the indicative can be used in the result clause to emphasise the certainty of the outcome.
*   **Present instead of Future**: *Má théann tú ann tá deireadh leat.* = If you go there, it's the end of you.
*   **Perfect instead of Future**: *Má chloistear thú tá tú gafa.* = If you are heard, you are caught.
*   **Preterite instead of Conditional**: *Muna mbeadh tusa, do bhíos marbh acu.* = If it hadn't been for you, they would certainly have killed me.

### The Conditional (An Modh Coinníollach)

The conditional mood describes actions dependent on certain conditions, translating closely to "would". It is tense-independent and can refer to the past, present, or future.
*   *Rachainn inniu.* = I would go today.
*   *Rachainn inné.* = I would have gone yesterday.

The conditional is used for:
*   Actions dependent on unreal conditions: *Thiocfadh sé dá mbeadh an t-am aige.* = He would come if he had the time.
*   Future-in-the-past (e.g., in indirect speech): *Dúirt sé go mbeadh sé ann.* = He said he would be there.
*   Polite requests: *An dtabharfá dom an t-im, le do thoil?* = Would you please give me the butter?
*   Comparisons with `mar` (as if): *Bhog sé a cheann mar a bheadh sé ag seinm.* = He moved his head as if he were playing music.
*   Expressing anger or impatience: *Bhrisfinn a mhuineáil.* = I could break his neck.
*   After `dóbair` (almost): *Dóbair go dtitfinn.* = I almost fell.
*   Rhetorical questions: *Cé a chreidfeadh sin?* = Who would believe that?

### The Imperative (An Modh Ordaitheach)

The imperative is the command form, used for orders and requests. It has one tense.

*   **Primary Imperative**: The distinct form for the 2nd person.
    *   *Glan!* = Clean!
    *   *Glanaigí!* = Clean (pl.)!
*   **Secondary Imperative**: Forms from other moods are used for other persons.
    *   **1st Person Sg.**: *Glanaim!* = Let me clean! (Form is identical to the habitual present).
    *   **3rd Person (Jussive)**: *Glanadh sé!* = He should clean! / Let him clean! (Form is identical to the subjunctive preterite).
    *   **1st Person Pl. (Hortative)**: *Glanaimis!* = Let's clean! (Form is identical to the subjunctive preterite).

The negative imperative uses the particle `ná`.
*   *Ná glan é!* = Don't clean it!
*   *Ná habair é!* = Don't say it! / You're welcome.

#### Usage of the Imperative

Besides commands, the imperative is used to:
*   **Express indifference**: *Bíodh sé sásta nó ná bíodh.* = Whether he is satisfied or not.
*   **Form a condition**: *Déarfainnse an rud céanna le Seán agus bíodh sé ann.* = I would say the same thing to Seán if he were there.
*   **Act as a concessive conjunction**: *Bíodh nach dtuigim é, tuigim as.* = Although I don't understand it exactly, I get the gist of it.
*   **Form interjections**: *Abair é!* = You're right!
*   **Serve as the citation form of a verb**: *an briathar "bí"* = the verb "to be".

### The Subjunctive (An Foshuiteach)

The subjunctive is the potential mood, for actions that are possible or impossible, not real. It appears only in dependent clauses after conjunctions. It has two tenses:

*   **Subjunctive Present**: Expresses a future meaning.
*   **Subjunctive Preterite**: Is temporally indefinite, like the conditional.

The subjunctive is used for:
*   **Wishes and Curses (Optative)**: The subjunctive present with `go` (positive) or `nár` (negative).
    *   *Go raibh maith agat!* = Thank you! (May there be good at you).
    *   *Nár éirí sin leat!* = May you not succeed!
*   **After temporal conjunctions** (until, before): *Fan go dtaga mé.* = Wait until I come.
*   **To express purpose** (so that): *Rachaidh mé chun an aonaigh go gceannaí mé bó.* = I will go to the fair to buy a cow.
*   **In unreal conditional clauses** (after `dá`/`mura`): *Ní íosfainn an fheoil sin dá bhfaighinn bás den ocras.* = I would not eat that meat even if I were dying of hunger.
*   **In exclamatory wishes of regret**: *Dá dtéadh sé go Gaillimh!* = If only he had gone to Galway!

## Voice (An Fhaí)

Voice describes the relationship between the action and the participants (subject, object).

### The Active (An Fhaí Ghníomhach)

In the active voice, the subject of the sentence performs the action. This is the standard and most preferred form in Irish.
*   *Glanann sé an bord.* = He cleans the table.

### The Passive (An Fhaí Chéasta)

In the passive voice, the subject of the sentence receives the action. Irish uses several periphrastic (compound) constructions for the passive, as there is no simple passive verb ending. The agent (the one doing the action) can be added with the preposition `ag`.

*   **Resultative Perfect Passive**: `bí` + Subject + Verbal Adjective (+ `ag` + Agent)
    *   *Tá an bord briste agam.* = The table has been broken by me. / I have broken the table.
*   **Progressive Passive**: `bí` + Subject + `á` (`do` + possessive pronoun) + Verbal Noun (+ `ag` + Agent)
    *   *Tá an teach á thógáil agam.* = The house is being built by me.
*   **Prospective Passive**: `bí` + Subject + `le` + Verbal Noun (+ `ag` + Agent)
    *   *Tá an balla le péinteáil agam.* = The wall is to be painted by me.
*   **Passive with `faigh`**: This is a calque from English ("get done").
    *   *Fuair sé gortaithe.* = He got injured.

### The Autonomous Form (An Saorbhriathar)

The autonomous form is grammatically active but has an impersonal meaning. It is often translated with a passive sense, or with an impersonal subject like "one" or "they". The agent cannot be specified.
*   *Briseadh an bord.* = The table was broken. / One broke the table.
*   *Déantar é.* = It is done. / One does it.

## Aspect (An Ghné)

Aspect describes how an action extends over time (e.g., ongoing, completed, habitual). In Irish, aspects are mainly expressed through compound tenses using the verb `bí`.

### The Perfect Tenses (Na hAimsirí Foirfe)

Perfect tenses are used less frequently than in English; the preterite is often preferred. Irish distinguishes between two main perfect aspects:

*   **Terminative Aspect**: Emphasises the recent completion of an action, formed with `tar éis`.
*   **Resultative Aspect**: Emphasises the state resulting from an action, formed with the verbal adjective.

These can be formed in any tense or mood by conjugating the auxiliary verb `bí`.

| Tense/Mood | Resultative | Terminative | Translation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Present Perfect** | *Tá sé déanta agam.* | *Tá mé tar éis é a dhéanamh.* | I have done it. |
| **Preterite Perfect** | *Bhí sé déanta agam.* | *Bhí mé tar éis é a dhéanamh.* | I had done it. |
| **Future Perfect** | *Beidh sé déanta agam.* | *Beidh mé tar éis é a dhéanamh.* | I will have done it. |
| **Conditional Perfect** | *Bheadh sé déanta agam.* | *Bheinn tar éis é a dhéanamh.* | I would have done it. |
| **Imperative Perfect** | *Bíodh sé déanta agat.* | *Bí tar éis é a dhéanamh.* | Have it done! |

#### Formation with the Verbal Adjective

This form is resultative, focusing on the state after an action. With transitive verbs, it is formally a passive construction but is used and translated as an active perfect.
*   **Transitive**: *Tá an litir scríofa aici.* = She has written the letter.
*   **Intransitive**: *Tá siad imithe go hÉirinn.* = They have gone to Ireland.

#### Formation with tar éis

This form is terminative, emphasising that an action has just been completed. The preposition `i ndiadh` is used in Ulster.
*   *Tá mé tar éis snámh.* = I have just been swimming.
*   *Tá sé tar éis an litir a scríobh.* = He has just written the letter.

### The Progressive Aspect (An Fhoirm Leanúnach)

The progressive aspect describes an action in progress. It is formed with a conjugated form of `bí` + `ag` + verbal noun.
*   *Tá mé ag féachaint ar an teilifís.* = I am watching television.

In colloquial speech, the progressive present is the standard way to express a non-habitual present action.
*   **Progressive**: *Tá sé ag glanadh.* = He is cleaning (right now).
*   **Simple Present**: *Glanann sé.* = He cleans (habitually).

### The Prospective Aspect (An Fhoirm Aidhmeach)

The prospective aspect describes an action that is about to happen.
*   **Immediate Action**: Formed with `ar tí`.
    *   *Bhí mé ar tí an carr a dhíol.* = I was about to sell the car.
*   **General Prospect/Obligation**: Formed with `le`. This form is passive with transitive verbs.
    *   *Tá an obair le déanamh agam.* = The work is to be done by me.
