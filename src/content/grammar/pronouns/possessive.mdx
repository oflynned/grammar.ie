In Irish, the words for "my", "your", "his", and so on are technically considered possessive adjectives (`aidiachtaí sealbhacha`) rather than pronouns. This is because they must always be used with a noun and cannot stand alone. To express concepts like "mine" or "yours" independently, helper words are required.

## Forms of Possessive Pronouns

Possessive pronouns come before the noun they modify and often cause a sound change, known as a mutation, at the beginning of that noun. The specific pronoun and the mutation it causes are essential for distinguishing meaning, especially between "his", "her", and "their".

| Person | Pronoun | Mutation | Before a Vowel | Emphatic Suffix |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1st Sg. (my) | `mo` | Lenition | `m'` | `-sa`/`-se` |
| 2nd Sg. (your) | `do` | Lenition | `d'` | `-sa`/`-se` |
| 3rd Sg. (his) | `a` | Lenition | `a` | `-san`/`-sean` |
| 3rd Sg. (her) | `a` | None | `a h-` | `-sa`/`-se` |
| 1st Pl. (our) | `ár` | Eclipsis | `ár n-` | `-na`/`-ne` |
| 2nd Pl. (your) | `bhur` | Eclipsis | `bhur n-` | `-sa`/`-se` |
| 3rd Pl. (their) | `a` | Eclipsis | `a n-` | `-san`/`-sean` |

The forms `m'` (for `mo`) and `d'` (for `do`) are used before nouns starting with a vowel or `fh-`.
* *m'athair* - my father
* *d'uncail* - your uncle
* *m'fhocal* - my word

However, the full forms `mo` and `do` are typically used before `fhl-` or `fhr-`.
* *do fhreagra* - your answer

### Emphatic Forms

To add emphasis, a suffix is attached to the noun (or a following adjective), not the pronoun itself.
* *mo charrsa* - **my** car
* *mo charr mórsa* - **my** big car

## Dialectal Differences

<Dialect region="Connacht/Munster">
In many dialects outside Ulster, `t'` is used instead of `d'` for "your" before a vowel or `fh-`.
* *t'uncail* - your uncle
* *t'fhocal* - your word
</Dialect>

<Dialect region="Connacht">
In Connacht, `ár` (our) and `bhur` (your, pl.) are often pronounced the same as `a` [ə]. Because of this, stronger emphatic forms are needed to avoid ambiguity. Instead of the simple suffixes, the full emphatic pronouns are often placed after the noun.
* *a charr seisean* - his car (instead of `a charrsan`)
* *a carr sise* - her car (instead of `a carrsa`)
* *ár gcarr muide* - our car (instead of `ár gcarrna`)
* *bhur gcarr sibhse* - your car (instead of `bhur gcarrsa`)
* *a gcarr siadsan* - their car (instead of `a gcarrsan`)
</Dialect>

<Dialect region="Ulster">
In Ulster, a similar pattern occurs in the 3rd person when lenition or eclipsis isn't enough to distinguish meaning.
* *a lámh eisean* - his hand
* *a lámh ise* - her hand
* *a lámh iadsan* - their hand

Also in Ulster, `mur` is used for `bhur` (your, pl.).
</Dialect>

<Dialect region="Munster">
In Munster, `úr` is used for `bhur` (your, pl.).
</Dialect>

<Note>
You may occasionally see `mh'` instead of `m'` for "my", for example, *mh'athair*.
</Note>

## Using cuid with Possessive Pronouns

The word `cuid` (meaning "share" or "part") is frequently used with possessive pronouns, especially for plural or uncountable nouns. Its use is more common in northern dialects.

### Grammatical Form

When `cuid` is used, the noun that follows it is put into the genitive case.
* *mo chuid oibre* - my work (literally "my share of work")

Alternatively, the preposition `de` ("of") can be used.
* *mo chuid den obair* - my share of the work

<Note>
Although `cuid` is a feminine noun, it does not cause lenition on the following noun because the noun is in the partitive genitive, which is not lenited.
* *a cuid gruaige* - her hair
</Note>

When adding emphasis, the suffix is usually attached to the noun following `cuid`, not to `cuid` itself.
* *Seo mo chuid bainnese!* - This is **my** milk!
* *mo chuid airgidse* - **my** money

However, it is also possible to attach it to `cuid`.
* *mo chuidse airgid* - **my** money

### When to Use cuid

The use of `cuid` is mandatory in all dialects for certain categories:
*   **Food**: *mo chuid ime* - my butter
*   **Property**: *mo chuid talaimh* - my land
*   **Language skills**: *mo chuid Gaeilge* - my Irish
*   **Activities**: *mo chuid oibre* - my work

In other cases, `cuid` is used for:
1.  **Plural nouns**: *mo chuid leabhar* - my books.
2.  **Uncountable nouns**: *ár gcuid airgid* - our money.

<Warning>
`Cuid` is generally not used for inalienable possessions like body parts, except in Ulster.
*   Standard: *mo chosa* - my feet
*   Ulster: *mo chuid cos* - my feet
</Warning>

For some nouns, usage varies. Both `m'eolas` and `mo chuid eolais` are correct for "my knowledge".

## Expressing 'Mine', 'Yours', 'Hers'

Irish has no single words for "mine", "yours", etc. Instead, it uses a possessive pronoun with a helper noun like `ceann` (one/head), `cuid` (share), or `muintir` (people).

*   `ceann` is used for singular, countable items.
*   `cuid` is used for plural items or uncountable singular items.
*   `muintir` is used for people.

| Meaning | Singular (Countable) | Plural / Uncountable | For People |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| mine | `mo cheannsa` | `mo chuidse` | `mo mhuintirse` |
| yours (sg) | `do cheannsa` | `do chuidse` | `do mhuintirse` |
| his | `a cheannsan` | `a chuidsean` | `a mhuintirsean` |
| hers | `a ceannsa` | `a cuidse` | `a muintirse` |
| ours | `ár gceannna` | `ár gcuidne` | `ár muintirne` |
| yours (pl) | `bhur gceannsa` | `bhur gcuidse` | `bhur muintirse` |
| theirs | `a gceannsan` | `a gcuidsean` | `a muintirsan` |

* *Sin é mo cheannsa* - That is mine. (referring to a single item)
* *Sin é mo chuidse* - Those are mine. (referring to multiple items or an amount)

To contrast two items, you can simply repeat the noun.
* *mo leabharsa agus do leabharsa* - my book and yours
* *mo chuid leabharsa agus do chuidse* - my books and yours

<Warning>
To state that something **belongs** to someone, use the preposition `le` (with), not a possessive pronoun. This is a very common point of confusion.
* *Is liomsa an leabhar.* - The book is mine. (literally "Is with-me the book.")
* *An leatsa an carr?* - Is the car yours?
* *Is linne na bróga.* - The shoes are ours.
</Warning>

## Combinations with Prepositions

Several prepositions that end in a vowel merge with a following possessive pronoun.

| | `de` (of) | `do` (to) | `faoi` (under) | `i` (in) | `le` (with) | `ó` (from) | `trí` (through) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`mo`** (my) | `de mo` | `do mo` | `faoi mo` | `i mo` | `le mo` | `ó mo` | `trí mo` |
| **`do`** (your) | `de do` | `do do` | `faoi do` | `i do` | `le do` | `ó do` | `trí do` |
| **`a`** (his/her) | **`dá`** | **`dá`, `á`** | **`faoina`** | **`ina`** | **`lena`** | **`óna`** | **`trína`** |
| **`ár`** (our) | **`dár`** | **`dár`** | **`faoinár`** | **`inár`** | **`lenár`** | **`ónár`** | **`trínár`** |
| **`bhur`** (your) | `de bhur` | `do bhur` | `faoi bhur` | **`in bhur`** | `le bhur` | `ó bhur` | `trí bhur` |
| **`a`** (their) | **`dá`** | **`dá`, `á`** | **`faoina`** | **`ina`** | **`lena`** | **`óna`** | **`trína`** |

<Dialect region="Munster">
In Munster, combined forms are common in the 1st and 2nd person singular.
*   `im leabhar` (instead of `i mo leabhar`) - in my book
*   `lem` (for `le mo`), `led` (for `le do`)
*   `óm` (for `ó mo`), `ód` (for `ó do`)
</Dialect>

<Dialect region="Connacht/Ulster">
In Connacht and Ulster, you may see `in mo` and `in do` instead of `i mo` and `i do`.
</Dialect>

<Note>
The forms `ina` and `inár` are often shortened in speech and writing to `'na` and `'nár`.
* *Tá sé ’na chónaí i gCorcaigh.* - He lives in Cork.
</Note>

### Special Cases for Prepositions

**The Preposition `do` (to)**
When used with a verbal noun to show the object of an action, `do + a` becomes `á`.
* *Tá mé á mholadh.* - I am praising him. (literally "Am I to-his praising.")

In other contexts, the standard form `dá` is used.
* *Thug mé dá chara é.* - I gave it to his friend.

**The Preposition `ar` (on)**
The combined form `arna` is only used before verbal nouns to mean "after". In all other cases, `ar a` is used.
* *arna chur in eagar ag...* - edited by... (literally "after its putting in order by...")

<Details summary="Historical note on the linking -n-">
The linking consonant `-n-` in forms like `ina`, `lena`, and `óna` originally came from prepositions that caused eclipsis, such as `i` (`i n-a` > `ina`). Its use spread by analogy to other vowel-ending prepositions like `le` and `ó`. In some older or fixed phrases, especially with `a chéile` (each other), this `-n-` is absent: *le chéile* (together) and *ó chéile* (from each other).
</Details>

## Grammatical Roles of Possessive Pronouns

Possessive pronouns have several important grammatical functions beyond simple ownership.

*   **As the genitive of a personal pronoun**:
    *   Object of a verbal noun: *Tá tú do mo bhualadh.* - You are hitting me.
    *   Passive meaning: *Tá an teach á thógáil.* - The house is being built. (literally "Is the house to-its building.")
    *   Reflexive meaning (with `féin`): *Tá tú do do bhualadh féin.* - You are hitting yourself.
    *   With two-part prepositions: *os mo chionn* - above me (literally "above my head.")

*   **With verbs of state**: With verbs like `bí` (to be), `suí` (sit), `codladh` (sleep), the structure `i + possessive pronoun` is used.
    *   *Tá sé ina shuí.* - He is sitting. (literally "He is in his sitting.")
    *   *Tá sé ina chónaí i mBaile Átha Cliath.* - He lives in Dublin.

*   **For classification**: To state a person's profession or role (a temporary state), this structure is also used.
    *   *Tá mé i mo dhochtúir.* - I am a doctor.

*   **To anticipate a clause**: The pronoun `a` (his) can be used to refer to a following clause, especially with verbs of knowing or seeing.
    *   *Tá a fhios agam go bhfuil tú anseo.* - I know that you are here. (literally "Is its knowledge at-me that...")
    *   *Ba mhaith liom a fheiceáil go bhfuil tú sásta.* - I would like to see that you are happy.

### When Not to Use Possessive Pronouns

There are specific situations where a possessive pronoun is not used, even though it might seem logical.

*   **"One of my friends"**: Use a prepositional pronoun of `do` or `le`, or the phrase `de chuid`.
    *   *cara liom* - a friend of mine
    *   *cara dá chuid* - a friend of his

*   **In direct address (vocative)**: Use the vocative particle `a`, which causes lenition.
    *   *A chara!* - My friend!

*   **With demonstratives, comparatives, or ordinals**: A construction with the preposition `ag` is used instead.
    *   *an teach seo agamsa* - this house of mine
    *   *an mac is sine agam* - my eldest son
    *   *an chéad mhac aige* - his first son

## Alternative Construction for Possession

Instead of a simple possessive pronoun, you can use a structure with the definite article `an`, the noun, a demonstrative (`seo`, `sin`, or `úd`), and a form of the preposition `ag`. This is often used for emphasis or with certain nouns.

| Structure | Example | Translation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `an` + noun + `seo` + `agamsa` | *an teach seo agamsa* | this house of mine |
| `an` + noun + `seo` + `agam féin` | *an teach seo agam féin* | my own house |

This construction is particularly common with nouns like `tír` (country), `baile` (town/home), and `teach` (house).
* *an tír seo againne* - our country

It is also used with personal names to show a relationship.
* *Seán seo againne* - our Seán
