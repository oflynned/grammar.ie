In Irish, prepositions are relational words placed before a noun to show its relationship to another word in the sentence. They function much like prepositions in English.

## Types of Prepositions

Irish prepositions can be divided into two main categories: simple and compound. They are also classified by whether they combine with pronouns.

### Simple and Compound Prepositions

**Simple prepositions** consist of a single, short word. Many are ancient words with roots in other Indo-European languages (e.g., Irish `i` is related to English "in").

**Compound prepositions** are formed from a simple preposition combined with a noun, but this guide focuses on the more common simple prepositions.

### Conjugated and Unconjugated Prepositions

A key feature of Irish grammar is that many simple prepositions merge with personal pronouns to create a single word. These are known as **prepositional pronouns** or **conjugated prepositions** (`réamhfhocail réimníthe`).

-   **Example:** The preposition `le` (with) combines with pronouns to form `liom` (with me), `leat` (with you), `leis` (with him), etc.

Some prepositions do not merge with pronouns. These are called **unconjugated prepositions** (`réamhfhocail neamhréimníthe`). The preposition and the personal pronoun remain separate words.

-   **Example:** The preposition `gan` (without) is followed by the standard pronouns: `gan mé` (without me), `gan tú` (without you), `gan é` (without him), etc.

## Grammar and Usage

### Syntax

Prepositions always come directly before the noun they modify. If the noun has an article or another determiner, it stands between the preposition and the noun.

In a list, the preposition is usually repeated before each noun.
-   *i nGaillimh agus i gCorcaigh* (in Galway and in Cork)

An exception is `idir` (between), which is used only once to connect two nouns.
-   *idir Gaillimh agus Corcaigh* (between Galway and Cork)

### Meaning

Like in English, Irish prepositions have both literal meanings (often indicating location or direction) and more abstract or idiomatic uses. Many prepositions are essential parts of common phrases and are used to express concepts like possession or desire, often in combination with the verb `bí`.

### Initial Mutations and Case

Many prepositions cause an initial mutation (lenition or eclipsis) on the following noun. These rules are specific to each preposition.

Historically, prepositions governed different grammatical cases (dative or accusative). Today, this distinction is mostly lost, and the dative is considered the default case after most prepositions. However, the use of eclipsis after a preposition and the definite article (e.g., `ar an mbord`, on the table) is a remnant of the old accusative case.

A few simple prepositions, which evolved from nouns, require the genitive case.

## Notes on Forms and Dialects

### Formation Patterns

You can see clear patterns in the endings of prepositional pronouns:
-   **1st person singular:** always ends in `-m` (e.g., `agam`, `orm`, `liom`).
-   **2nd person singular:** always ends in `-t` (e.g., `agat`, `ort`, `leat`).
-   **1st person plural:** usually ends in `-inn` (e.g., `againn`, `orainn`, `linn`).
-   **2nd person plural:** usually ends in `-ibh` (e.g., `agaibh`, `oraibh`, `libh`).

### Historical and Obsolete Forms

-   The preposition `idir` (between) is now mostly unconjugated in the singular (`idir mé`, `idir thú`). Older conjugated forms like `eadram` (between me) and `eadrat` (between you) are no longer in common use.
-   In older texts, you may encounter conjugated forms of prepositions that are now rare, such as `seach` (e.g., `seacham`) or `os` (e.g., `uasam`).

### Spelling and Pronunciation

-   **3rd Person Plural:** The ending `-u` (e.g., `acu`, `orthu`) was historically often spelt with `-a` (`aca`, `ortha`). The `-u` spelling is now standard.
-   **Connacht Pronunciation:** In Connacht (especially Connemara), the 3rd person plural ending `-u` is often pronounced /əb/, as if spelt `-ab`. For example, `acu` is pronounced [akəb] and `orthu` is pronounced [orhəb]. The 2nd person plural ending `-ibh` may also be pronounced this way.
