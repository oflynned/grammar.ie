Irish personal pronouns change form depending on their grammatical role, such as whether they are the subject or object of a verb. They also have distinct emphatic forms used for contrast or emphasis.

## Forms of Personal Pronouns

This table shows the main forms of personal pronouns. The distinction between subject, object, and copula forms is most important in the 3rd person.

| Person | Subject Form | Object / Copula Form | Emphatic Subject | Emphatic Object / Copula |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1st sg (I) | `mé` | `mé` | `mise` | `mise` |
| 2nd sg (you) | `tú` | `thú` | `tusa` | `thusa` |
| 3rd sg (he) | `sé` | `é` | `seisean` | `eisean` |
| 3rd sg (she) | `sí` | `í` | `sise` | `ise` |
| 3rd sg (it) | — | `ea` | — | (`eadhon`) |
| 1st pl (we) | `sinn` / `muid` | `sinn` / `muid` | `sinne` / `muidne` | `sinne` / `muidne` |
| 2nd pl (you) | `sibh` | `sibh` | `sibhse` | `sibhse` |
| 3rd pl (they) | `siad` | `iad` | `siadsan` | `iadsan` |

**Dialectal variants for `muidne`:**
*   Ulster: `muidinne`, `muid inne`
*   Connacht: `muide`

### Conjunct and Disjunct Forms

While often called subject and object forms, a more precise linguistic distinction is between **conjunct** and **disjunct** forms.
*   **Conjunct forms** (`sé`, `sí`, `siad`) are used when the pronoun is the subject and immediately follows a finite verb.
*   **Disjunct forms** (`é`, `í`, `iad`) are used in all other positions, including as an object, with the copula, or as a subject that does not immediately follow the verb.

### Genitive and Dative Cases

Irish personal pronouns do not have distinct genitive (possessive) or dative forms.
*   To show possession, **possessive pronouns** are used (e.g., `mo`, `do`, `a`).
*   To express the dative case, **prepositional pronouns** are used, which are combinations of a preposition and a pronoun (e.g., `dom` for `do + mé`, meaning "to me").

## Grammatical Roles and Usage

### The Subject Form (Conjunct)

The subject forms (`sé`, `sí`, `siad`, and `tú`) are used when the pronoun is the subject of the sentence and comes **immediately after the verb**.

*   *Ólann sé bainne.* - He drinks milk.

This form is **not** used:
*   With synthetic verb forms, where the subject is part of the verb ending (e.g., *ólaim* - I drink). An exception is the 3rd person plural, where the pronoun can be added for clarity: *tuigid siad* or *tuigeann siad* (they understand).
*   When the pronoun subject does not directly follow the verb: *Ólann seisean agus ise.* (He and she drink).

### The Object and Copula Form (Disjunct)

The object forms (`é`, `í`, `iad`, and `thú`) are used in all other contexts. This includes when the pronoun is:
*   The **direct object** of a verb: *Chonaic mé é.* (I saw him.)
*   The **subject in an infinitive construction**: *Ba mhaith liom é a fheiceáil.* (I would like to see him.)
*   The **subject or predicate in a copula sentence**: *Is é mo mhúinteoir é.* (He is my teacher.)
*   Used with the **autonomous verb form**: *Glantar é.* (It is cleaned.)
*   Used after **non-conjugable prepositions**: *gan é* (without him), *idir mé agus tú* (between me and you).

Note that with the copula `is`, the forms `é`, `í`, and `iad` are often pronounced as if they start with 's': `is é` sounds like `sé`. This is sometimes reflected in non-standard spellings like `'sé`.

## Pronunciation and Stress

### Stressed and Unstressed Forms

The simple pronouns `mé`, `tú`, `sé`, `sí`, and `siad` have both stressed and unstressed pronunciations.
*   **Stressed:** The vowel is long, as the spelling suggests: `mé` [m′eː], `tú` [tuː], `sé` [s′eː]. This is used after conjunctions like `agus` or `ach`, in infinitive constructions, and in copula sentences.
*   **Unstressed:** The vowel is shortened, sometimes to a schwa [ə]. This is the more common pronunciation in everyday speech when the pronoun is not the focus.

Despite the change in pronunciation, the standard spelling with the fada (`mé`, `tú`) is always retained.

### Double Stress (`an béimniú dúbáilte`)

In some cases, a simple personal pronoun subject is stressed to emphasise the **action or the truth of the statement**, not the person performing it. This creates a "double stress" pattern where both the verb and the pronoun receive equal emphasis.

*   *Bhí sé.* [ˈv′iː ˈs′eː] - He **was** (indeed). (In response to "An raibh sé anseo?")
*   *Rachaidh sí.* [ˈraxə ˈs′iː] - She **will** go.

To emphasise the **person**, the emphatic forms (`seisean`, `sise`, etc.) are used instead.
*   *Rinne sé sin.* (unstressed `sé`) - He did that. (Neutral statement)
*   *Rinne sé sin.* (stressed `sé`) - He **did** do that. (Emphasis on the action)
*   *Rinne seisean sin.* - **He** did that. (Emphasis on the person: he, and no one else)

## Emphatic Forms and Suffixes

### Using Emphatic Pronouns

Emphatic forms like `mise`, `tusa`, and `seisean` are used to contrast or highlight a person. They are required in certain structures:
*   In lists of people: *mise agus tusa* (me and you).
*   As the predicate in identification sentences with the copula: *Is mise Pól.* (I am Paul.)
*   When calling out to someone: *Haigh, tusa!* (Hey, you!)

These forms are generally used for people, not for inanimate objects. For objects, alternatives like `é féin` (itself) or `é sin` (that one) are used.

### Emphatic Suffixes (`na hiarmhíreanna tréise`)

The emphatic pronouns are formed by adding suffixes to the simple pronouns. These suffixes can also attach to other words to add emphasis to the person associated with them.

| Person | Suffix (after broad vowel) | Suffix (after slender vowel) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1st sg (I) | `-sa` | `-se` |
| 2nd sg (you) | `-sa` | `-se` |
| 3rd sg (he) | `-san` | `-sean` |
| 3rd sg (she) | `-sa` | `-se` |
| 1st pl (we) | `-na` | `-ne` / `-e` |
| 2nd pl (you) | `-sa` | `-se` |
| 3rd pl (they) | `-san` | `-sean` |

The suffix is chosen based on the quality (broad or slender) of the final vowel of the word it attaches to.
*   Broad ending: `a bhean**sa**` (his wife)
*   Slender ending: `táim**se**` (I am)

### Attaching Emphatic Suffixes

These suffixes can be attached to:
*   **Personal pronouns:** `sé` + `-sean` → `seisean`
*   **Possessive pronouns (via the noun):** `a theach**san**` (his house)
*   **Prepositional pronouns:** `aige` + `-sean` → `aigesean` (with him)
*   **Synthetic verb forms:** `tuigim` + `-se` → `tuigimse` (I understand)

If a person is mentioned multiple times in a clause, the suffix is only used on the first mention: *Nímse mé féin.* (I wash myself.)

## Special Pronouns and Cases

### The Impersonal Pronoun `ea`

`ea` is a remnant of the neuter gender and translates as "it". It refers to indefinite nouns, phrases, or entire statements, but its use is restricted to predicative positions with the copula.

*   **In classification sentences:** *Múinteoir is ea í.* (She is a teacher. Lit: "A teacher, is it she.")
*   **In answers:** *An dochtúir thú? - Ní hea.* (Are you a doctor? - No.)
*   **In questions:** *Seán a rinne é, an ea? - Is ea.* (Seán did it, is that so? - Yes.)
*   **To connect adverbials:** *Thall ansiúd is ea atá sí.* (Over there is where she is.)
*   **In phrases:** `más ea` (if so), `ní hea` (no, not so).

For the general, expletive "it" with other verbs (e.g., weather, time), `sé` is used: *Tá sé fuar.* (It is cold.)

### The Polite Form (`foirm an ómóis`)

Irish does not have a formal "you" like French *vous* or German *Sie*. The 2nd person singular `tú` is used for everyone, including strangers and superiors.
*   *Conas atá tú, a Uachtaráin?* - How are you, Mr. President?

The only exception is when addressing clergy, where the 2nd person plural `sibh` can be used as a sign of respect (the *plural of honour*).
*   *Conas atá sibh, a Athair?* - How are you, Father?

### `muid` vs. `sinn`

Both `muid` and `sinn` mean "we".
*   `sinn` is the older, traditional form, strongly associated with Munster.
*   `muid` originated from a verb ending (`-imid`) in Ulster and is now more common in many regions.

In the Official Standard, `muid` is fully accepted. Some speakers differentiate, using `muid` as a subject form after a verb (*Tuigeann muid*) and `sinn` as an object form (*Tuigeann tú sinn*), but this is not a universal rule.

### `tú` vs. `thú`

The lenited form `thú` is primarily the object form of "you" (sg.).
*   **Use `tú` (unlenited):**
    *   As the subject of a verb: *Rinne tú go maith.* (You did well.)
    *   Immediately after the copula: *Is tusa mo ghrá.* (You are my love.)
    *   After `agus` and non-conjugated prepositions: *idir mise agus tusa* (between me and you).
*   **Use `thú` (lenited):**
    *   As the object of a verb: *Chonaic mé thú.* (I saw you.)
    *   In copula sentences where it does not immediately follow the copula: *Is dochtúir thú.* (You are a doctor.)

## Syntax and Agreement

### Order of Persons (`ord na bpearsan`)

When listing people, Irish follows a strict order: 1st person, then 2nd person, then 3rd person. This is the opposite of the polite convention in English.
*   *Bhí mise, tusa agus eisean ann.* - I, you, and he were there.
*   *Bhí mise agus mo chairde in Éirinn.* - My friends and I were in Ireland.

### Agreement in Gender and Number

Pronouns must agree with the noun they refer to.
*   **Gender:** For people, the pronoun matches their natural gender, regardless of the noun's grammatical gender (*an cailín* is masculine, but the pronoun is `sí`). For animals and objects, the pronoun usually matches the grammatical gender of the noun (*stail*, "stallion", is feminine, so the pronoun is `sí`).
*   **Number:** Collective nouns like `muintir` (people) or `beirt` (two people) are grammatically singular but can be referred to by a plural pronoun (`iad`, `leo`) when the individual members of the group are being considered.

## Pronouns in Other Constructions

### With Demonstratives and `féin`

Personal pronouns combine with other words to create more specific meanings.
*   **With demonstratives:** `é` + `seo` → `é seo` (this one, masc.); `í` + `sin` → `í sin` (that one, fem.).
*   **With the reflexive pronoun `féin`:** `mé` + `féin` → `mé féin` (myself); `é` + `féin` → `é féin` (himself).

### The Determinative Pronoun `an té`

The pronoun `an té` means "the one who" or "he who". It functions like a substantivised pronoun but is gender-neutral and can refer to men or women.
