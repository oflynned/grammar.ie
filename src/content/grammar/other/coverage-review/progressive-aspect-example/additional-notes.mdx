## Forms with a Pronominal Object

The construction for the progressive aspect with a pronominal object (e.g., "He is hitting **me**") varies significantly across Irish dialects and the other Goidelic languages.

### Standard Irish

The standard forms use the preposition `do` combined with a possessive pronoun, which causes lenition.

- **striking me:** `do mo bhualadh` (pronounced [də mə])
- **striking you (sg):** `do do bhualadh` (pronounced [də də])
- **striking him:** `á bhualadh` (from `do + a`, pronounced [aː])
- **striking her:** `á bualadh` (from `do + a`, pronounced [aː], no lenition)
- **striking us:** `dár mbualadh` (from `do + ár`, pronounced [daːr])
- **striking you (pl):** `do bhur mbualadh` (pronounced [də vur])
- **striking them:** `á mbualadh` (from `do + a`, pronounced [aː])

### Munster Irish

Munster forms are shortened versions of the standard.

- **striking me:** `am bualadh` (pronounced [əm])
- **striking you (sg):** `ad bhualadh` (pronounced [əd])
- **striking him:** `á bhualadh` (pronounced [ɑː])
- **striking her:** `á bualadh` (pronounced [ɑː])
- **striking us:** `ár mbualadh` (pronounced [ɑːr])
- **striking you (pl):** `bhur mbualadh` (pronounced [uːr])
- **striking them:** `á mbualadh` (pronounced [ɑː])

### Connacht Irish

Connacht forms are similar to the standard, but with distinct pronunciation. The preposition `do` is pronounced [gə].

- **striking me:** `do mo bhualadh` (pronounced [gə mə])
- **striking you (sg):** `do do bhualadh` (pronounced [gə də])
- **striking him:** `dhá bhualadh` (pronounced [γɑː])
- **striking her:** `dhá bualadh` (pronounced [γɑː])
- **striking us:** `dhár mbualadh` (pronounced [γɑː])
- **striking you (pl):** `dho’ur mbualadh` (pronounced [γɑː])
- **striking them:** `dhá mbualadh` (pronounced [γɑː])

The forms `dhá`, `dhár`, and `dho’ur` can also be pronounced [dɑː], [gɑː], or [ɑː].

### Ulster Irish

Ulster forms use the preposition `ag` combined with a possessive pronoun.

- **striking me:** `ag mo bhualadh` (pronounced [(ə) mə])
- **striking you (sg):** `ag do bhualadh` (pronounced [(ə) də])
- **striking him:** `á bhualadh` (pronounced [æː])
- **striking her:** `á bualadh` (pronounced [æː])
- **striking us:** `ag ár mbualadh` (pronounced [(əg) æːr])
- **striking you (pl):** `ag mur mbualadh` (pronounced [(ə) mər])
- **striking them:** `á mbualadh` (pronounced [æː])

Note: The 'g' in `ag` is often not pronounced before a consonant, and sometimes the entire preposition `ag` is omitted.

### Scottish Gaelic

- **striking me:** `gam bhualadh`
- **striking you (sg):** `gad bhualadh`
- **striking him:** `ga bhualadh`
- **striking her:** `ga bualadh`
- **striking us:** `gar bualadh`
- **striking you (pl):** `gur bualadh`
- **striking them:** `gam bualadh`

### Manx

Manx has older forms similar to Irish and Scottish Gaelic, and newer forms with a different structure.

#### Older Forms

- **striking me:** `dy my vuilley`
- **striking you (sg):** `dy dty vuilley`
- **striking him:** `dy vuilley (eh)`
- **striking her:** `dy builley`
- **striking us:** `dyn muilley (shin)`
- **striking you (pl):** `dyn muilley (shiu)`
- **striking them:** `dyn muilley (ad)`

The older version, especially for the 3rd person singular masculine, is still used when preceded by an object, such as in relative clauses.

#### Newer Forms

In modern Manx, the personal pronoun is simply placed after the verbal noun.

- *Ta eh builley mee.* (He is striking me.)
- *Ta eh builley oo.* (He is striking you.)
- *Ta eh builley eh.* (He is striking him.)
- *Ta eh builley ee.* (He is striking her.)
- *Ta eh builley shin.* (He is striking us.)
- *Ta eh builley shiu.* (He is striking you all.)
- *Ta eh builley ad.* (He is striking them.)

## Historical and Dialectal Notes

### Phonetic Convergence of `do` and `ag`

Historically, both `do` and `ag` have been used in this construction. The reason for this blending is the common pronunciation of the preposition `do` as [gə] in many dialects. This led to a phonetic convergence with `ag`, especially in forms followed by a possessive pronoun.

This was also influenced by the similar construction of the infinitive of purpose, which originally always used `do` (later `a`). In Connacht and Munster, `ag` is now used in that construction as well, except before possessive pronouns. For a long period, both `do` and `ag` were used in the progressive aspect in written language.

### Older Forms

In older texts, the form `agá` (also written `'ghá`) can be found for `ag` + `a`, which corresponds to modern `á`.

### Debunked Active vs. Passive Distinction

Some older grammatical descriptions claimed an original distinction between active progressive forms (with `ag`) and passive progressive forms (with `do`). This assumption is now considered incorrect.

A prescriptive recommendation based on this was to use `ghá` (or `'ghá`) for the active and `dhá` (or `dh’á`) for the passive. For example:
- *Tá sé ghá dhéanamh.* (He is doing it. Lit: "is he at-his doing")
- *Tá sé dhá dhéanamh.* (It is being done. Lit: "is he/it to-his/its doing")

This distinction is no longer maintained or recommended.
