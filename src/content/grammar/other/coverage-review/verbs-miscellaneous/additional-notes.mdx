## Phrasal Verbs and Light Verbs (*Na Briathra Frásacha agus na Briathra Éadroma*)

### Phrasal Verbs (`briathra frásacha`)

Phrasal verbs are verbs in a fixed combination with prepositions and adverbs, which together carry a specific meaning.

While Old Irish used prefixes to modify verb meanings, this is no longer a productive feature in Modern Irish, with a few exceptions like `ath-` (re-), `frith-` (anti-), and `neamh-` (un-/non-). Some modern verbs, however, originated from these older prefixed forms, such as `abair` (from `ad` + `beir`) and `tabhair` (from `to` + `beir`).

In Modern Irish, prepositions (with their objects) or directional adverbs are placed *after* the verb to form a semantic unit. This combination, a "phrasal verb", can significantly change the verb's original meaning, often in ways that are highly context-dependent. This allows a small number of common verbs, such as `cuir`, `déan`, and `bain`, to be used in a multitude of expressions.

**Examples with `bain` (to pull out, extract)**

*   **`bain`**: *Bhain mé na prátaí.* (I harvested the potatoes.)
*   **`bain amach`** (to pull out, obtain, reach): *Bhain mé an teach amach.* (I reached the house.)
*   **`bain ar`** (to make someone do something): *Bhain mé air a theacht liom.* (I made him come with me.)
*   **`bain as`** (to take out of, get out of): *Bhain sé seachtain asam é a dhéanamh.* (It took me a week to do it.)
*   **`bain chuig/chun`** (to start, begin with): *Bhain mé chun reatha.* (I started running.)
*   **`bain de`** (to take away, remove from, reduce): *Bhain mé an siúl den bhád.* (I reduced the speed of the boat.)
*   **`bain do`** (to touch, concern): *Ná bain dó!* (Don't touch it!)
*   **`bain le`** (to touch, concern): *Tá costas ag baint leis.* (Costs are associated with it.)
*   **`bain faoi`** (to settle down; to calm someone): *Bhain mé fúm san áit seo.* (I settled down in this place.) *Bhain mé fúithi.* (I calmed her.)
*   **`bain ó`** (to control, restrain; to sharpen): *Bain uait!* (Restrain yourself!) *Bhain mé ón scian.* (I sharpened the knife.)

**Examples with `cuir` (to put, place, sow)**

*   **`cuir síos ar`** (to describe, report): *Chuir sé síos ar an gcuairt a thug sé uirthi.* (He reported on his visit to her.)
*   **`cuir isteach ar`** (to interrupt, disturb; to apply): *Chuir sé isteach ar an phost.* (He applied for the post.)
*   **`cuir siar`** (to postpone, defer, put off)
*   **`cuir siar ar`** (to impose, force upon)
*   **`cuir suas`** (to set up, erect): *Chuir sí suas an scáth fearthainne.* (She put up the umbrella.)
*   **`cuir suas le`** (to put up with, tolerate): *Chuir sé suas leis ar feadh deich nóiméad.* (He put up with it for 10 minutes.)

Verbal nouns formed from phrasal verbs retain the accompanying prepositional phrase or adverb.

*   `éirigh` (to rise) → `éirigh amach` (to rebel, revolt) → `an t-éirí amach` (the uprising). *Éirí Amach na Cásca* (The Easter Rising).
*   `cuir` (to put) → `cuir as` (to dislodge) → `cur as seilbh` (expropriation, lit. "placing out of possession").
*   `bain` (to extract) → `bain le` (to concern) → `baint le` (connection with). *Níl baint aige leis.* (He has nothing to do with it.)

### Light Verbs (`briathra éadroma`)

Irish often expresses states and actions using a general verb—a "light verb"—in combination with a noun and prepositions. This is a common feature of the language, which can be described as noun-oriented.

Common light verbs include `bain` (to take), `bí` (to be), `cuir` (to put), `déan` (to do/make), `faigh` (to get), `lig` (to let), `tabhair` (to give), and `tar` (to come).

These combinations, known as **light verb constructions**, are used in several ways.

#### Idiomatic Expressions

Many concepts are primarily or exclusively expressed with a light verb construction.

*   *Rinne sé gáire.* (He laughed. lit: "He made laughter.")
*   *Chuir sé áthas orm.* (It made me happy. lit: "It put joy on me.")
*   *Bhain mé sult as an lá.* (I enjoyed the day. lit: "I drew pleasure out of the day.")
*   *Tá a fhios agam.* (I know it. lit: "Is his knowledge at me.")
*   *Tá grá agam duit.* (I love you. lit: "Is love at me to you.")
*   *Tá suim agam ann.* (I am interested in it. lit: "Is interest at me in it.")

#### Describing a Singular Event

A light verb can be used with another verbal noun to describe a single, punctual event.

*   *Rinne/Thug sé léim.* (He made a jump.) (cf. *Léim sé* - He jumped.)
*   *Déan do mhachnamh arís air.* (Think about it again. lit: "Make your thinking again on it.")
*   *Bhain sé croitheadh as an mbuidéal.* (He shook the bottle once. lit: "He drew a shaking out of the bottle.")

#### Expressing Causation

Light verbs, especially `bain as`, can show that one person or thing causes another to perform an action.

*   *Bainfidh mé léim aisti.* (I will make her jump / I will startle her.)
*   *Baineadh titim asam.* (I was made to fall. lit: "One drew falling out of me.")
*   *Baineann sé allas asam.* (It makes me sweat. lit: "It draws sweat out of me.")
*   *Bhain sé ceol as an gcláirseach.* (He played the harp. lit: "He drew music out of the harp.")

#### Auxiliary Functions

The verb `déan` can act as an auxiliary verb for circumlocution or emphasis.

*   *Iad a cheannach a rinne sé.* (He bought them.) (cf. *Cheannaigh sé iad.*)

#### Feelings and States

Many feelings and physical states are described using nouns rather than dedicated verbs or adjectives. These nouns include `áthas` (joy), `brón` (sadness), `eagla` (fear), `fearg` (anger), `ocras` (hunger), and `tart` (thirst).

These states are typically expressed with the following patterns:

*   **To have a feeling**: `bí` + noun + `ar` (on)
    *   *Tá áthas orm.* (I am happy. lit: "Joy is on me.")
    *   *Tá eagla orm rompu.* (I am afraid of them.)
*   **To cause a feeling**: `cuir` + noun + `ar` (on)
    *   *Chuir sé áthas orm.* (It made me happy. lit: "It put joy on me.")
    *   *Chuir sé eagla orm rompu.* (He made me afraid of them.)
*   **To be overcome by a feeling**: `tar` + noun + `ar` (on)
    *   *Tháinig áthas orm.* (I became happy. lit: "Joy came on me.")
    *   *Tháinig eagla orm rompu.* (I became afraid of them.)
*   **To take a feeling away**: `bain` + noun + `de` (from)
    *   *Bhain sé an t-áthas díom.* (It took my joy away. lit: "It drew the joy from me.")

#### Further Examples of Light Verb Constructions

*   *Bhain sé feidhm as an leabhar.* (He used the book. lit: "He drew function out of the book.")
*   *Bhain mé sult as an turas.* (I enjoyed the journey. lit: "I drew pleasure out of the journey.")
*   *Thug sé cúnamh dom.* (He helped me. lit: "He gave help to me.")
*   *Thug sí aird air anois.* (Now she paid attention to him. lit: "She gave attention on him now.")
*   *Chuir sé ó chodladh í.* (It robbed her of sleep. lit: "It put her from sleep.")
*   *Cad é an rath a chuir tú ar an airgead?* (What did you use the money for? lit: "What is the benefit that you put on the money?")
*   *Chuir sé an abairt ó thuiscint.* (It made the sentence incomprehensible. lit: "It put the sentence from understanding.")
*   *Rinne mé iontas de.* (I wondered about it. lit: "I made wonder of it.")
*   *Ná déan dearmad air.* (Don't forget it! lit: "Don't make forgetting on it.")
*   *Lig sé fead.* (He whistled. lit: "He let a whistle.")

Some constructions are purely idiomatic. For example, the noun `deara` appears almost exclusively in the following phrases for "to notice" or "to cause":

*   *Thug sé faoi deara é.* (He noticed it.) (Dialect forms include `fé ndeara` in Munster, `faoi deara` or `ndeara` in Connacht, and `fá deara` in Ulster.)
*   *Chuir sé faoi deara dom é a dhéanamh.* (He/it made me do it.) (Munster: `fé ndear`/`ndeár`.)
