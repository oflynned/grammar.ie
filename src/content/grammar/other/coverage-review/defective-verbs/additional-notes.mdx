Defective verbs, known in Irish as `na briathra uireasacha`, are verbs that cannot be fully conjugated. Unlike irregular verbs, which have a wide variety of forms, defective verbs exist only in a few specific forms, with some appearing in only a single form.

## `feadair` (to know)

The verb `feadair` is the only surviving deponent verb in Irish. It is conjugated using old passive forms but has an active meaning. It is timeless, meaning a form like `feadramar` can mean "we know" or "we knew".

Today, `feadair` is used almost exclusively in questions, answers to questions, and negative sentences. It is most common in Munster Irish.

-   *An bhfeadair tú?* - Do you know?
-   *Ní fheadar.* - I don't know / I wonder.

The negative particle `ní` often merges with the verb to form `n’fheadair`. In speech, the first person form is often shortened further to `n’fhead’` or `n’fhea’`.

-   *N’fheadar an bhfuil sé ann fós.* - I wonder if it's still there.

With the preposition `do` (to), it means "to be able to tell someone".

-   *An bhfeadraís seo dom?* - Can you tell me this?

### Conjugation

| Person | Verb form | Alternatives |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1st sg. | `feadar` | `feadair mé` |
| 2nd sg. | `feadraís` | `feadair tú` |
| 3rd sg. | `feadair sé` | |
| 1st pl. | `feadramar` | `feadramair`, `feadair muid` |
| 2nd pl. | `feadrabhar` | `feadrabhair`, `feadair sibh` |
| 3rd pl. | `feadradar` | `feadraid`, `feadair siad` |

### Historical and Dialectal Notes

-   **Plural Forms**: Forms without `-r-` before the ending (e.g., `feadamar`) were common in Classical Irish. The `-r-` was later adopted from the singular forms, where it was originally a passive ending but came to be seen as part of the stem.
-   **Pronunciation**: In Munster, an epenthetic vowel is inserted between `d` and `r`, so `feadramair` is pronounced as if it were written `feadaramair`.
-   **Alternative Expressions**: The idiom `tá a fhios ag duine` (literally "his knowledge is at someone") is far more common for "to know". In Munster, it is also used in positive sentences where `feadair` is not used.
    -   *Tá a fhios agam.* - I know.
-   **Connemara Usage**: In Connemara, a verb `ní` can mean "to wonder" (e.g., *Ní mé* - I wonder). This is likely a form of the verb `déan`.

## `ar`, `arsa` (to say)

These forms are used only *after* direct speech to attribute the quote. They are timeless, so `arsa mise` can mean "I say" or "I said".

Only the emphatic forms of pronouns are used with `ar` and `arsa` (e.g., `mise`, `tusa`, `seisean`).

-   **`ar`** is used only before 3rd person pronouns: `ar seisean` (said he), `ar sise` (said she), `ar siadsan` (said they).
-   **`arsa`** is used in all other cases: `arsa mise` (said I), `arsa tusa` (said you), `arsa an fear` (said the man). An older, rarer form `ars` is sometimes found before the article.

### Examples

-   *"Tuigim anois", arsa Seán.* - "Now I understand," said Seán.
-   *"Ní thuigeann tú ar chor ar bith", ar sise.* - "You don't understand at all," she said.

For indirect speech, a different verb like `abair` must be used.

-   *Dúirt sé léi gur thuig sé.* - He told her that he understood.
-   *Dúirt sí leis nár thuig sé ar chor ar bith.* - She told him that he didn't understand at all.

The verb `abair` (often in the relative present form `a deir`) can also be used instead of `ar/arsa` with direct speech.

## `dar` (to seem)

This verb exists only in the single, timeless form `dar`. It is always combined with the preposition `le` (with) to mean "it seems to me" or "I think/thought".

It can appear after a declarative sentence or before a `go/nach` clause.

-   *Dar leis an bhfear go bhfuil sé ann.* - It seems/seemed to the man that he was there. / The man thought that he was there.
-   *Sin a bhfuil le rá, dar liom.* - That's all there is to say, it seems to me.

## `dóbair` (it almost happened)

This verb exists only as a 3rd person singular preterite form. It has several variants: `d’fhóbair`, `fhobair`, `hóbair`.

Though originally a verb, it now functions more like an adverb meaning "almost" or "nearly". In Munster, it is often used with the copula: `ba dhóbair`.

It can be followed by a verbal noun construction (with the subject marked by `do`) or a `go/nach` clause.

-   *Dóbair dom titim.* - I almost fell.
-   *Dóbair go dtitfinn.* - I almost fell.

## `aitim` (I implore)

This verb appears in only two forms, both in the 1st person present indicative:

-   `aitim` - I implore
-   `aitimid` - we implore

Alternative spellings `aicim` and `aicimid` also exist. The old orthography was `aithchim` and `aithchimid`.

## `tarlaigh` (to happen)

This verb is used only in the 3rd person. Forms for the 1st and 2nd person, as well as the autonomous form, do not exist. The verbal noun is `tarlú` (happening, event).

The preterite is irregular: **`tharla`**. (The form `tharlaigh` is also found in some dialects).

When followed by a `go/nach` clause or a verbal noun phrase, it does not usually take a dummy subject `sé` (it).

-   *Tharla timpiste.* - An accident happened.
-   *Má tharlaíonn go mbeidh tú ag caint leis.* - If it happens that you speak with him.

The person affected by the event can be introduced with `do` when a verbal noun follows.

-   *Tharla dom a bheith ann nuair a tháinig sé.* - I happened to be there when he came.

The phrase `ó tharla (go)` means "since" or "because".

-   *Agus ó tharla muid ag fanacht le fada le freagra...* - And since we had been waiting a long time for an answer...
    (Note: `muid` is not the subject of `tharla`; the entire clause `muid ag fanacht...` is.)

## `téana` (come)

This verb is used only in the imperative.

-   *Téana ort!* - Come along!
-   *Téanaigí oraibh!* - Come along! (plural)

The 1st person plural form `téanam` is very common.

-   *Téanam abhaile!* - Let's go home!

`Téanam` is also used as a general command addressed to a 2nd person.

-   *Téanam ort!* - Come along!
-   *Téanam oraibh!* - Come along! (plural)

A verbal noun, `téanachtaint`, exists for use in indirect commands.

-   *Dúirt sé liom téanachtaint leis.* - He told me to come with him.

## `ráinig`, `ráinigh` (to reach)

`Ráinig` is rare today but common in older literature, especially from Munster. It is etymologically related to `tháinig`, the preterite of `tar` (to come). While other forms once existed (e.g., present `rigeann sé`), only the preterite stem `ráinig` survived. The 1st person singular synthetic form was `rángas` (`ráinig mé`).

In Munster, `ráinig` evolved into `ráinigh`, which looks like a regular 2nd conjugation verb. This led to the creation of other "regular" forms like `ráiníonn sé` (he reaches) and `ráineoidh sé` (he will reach). It is often used impersonally in the 3rd person.

**Usage:**
`Ráinig` can mean "to reach", "to come", "to happen", or "to achieve".

-   *Conas mar a ráinig go raibh tú anseo?* - How did it happen that you were here?
-   To achieve a goal (logical subject with `le`): *Ráinigh liom é a dhéanamh.* - I managed to do it.
-   Impersonally with `le` to mean "to be able to": *sara ráinig linn-ne dul abhaile* - before we could go home.

The verb is well-known from its use in the Irish national anthem: *buíon dár slua thar toinn do ráinig chughainn* ("a group of our host came to us over the waves").

Its verbal noun, `riachtain`, survived in the words `riachtanach` (necessary) and `riachtanas` (necessity). The subjunctive form survived in the rare preposition `go ruige` (until).
