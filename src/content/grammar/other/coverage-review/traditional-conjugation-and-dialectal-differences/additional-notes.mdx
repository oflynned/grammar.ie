## Traditional Conjugation Forms

The following notes describe synthetic and analytic verb forms from older stages of Irish, regardless of whether they are still used in modern Irish or have been replaced by analytic endings. Modern orthography has been applied throughout. As many of these forms appear primarily in older texts, the older orthography is also sometimes provided.

It should be noted that spelling in older orthography often varied, and different spellings were used to represent the same sounds. For example, the ending of the autonomous form in the imperfect, pronounced [tiː], could be written as `-t(h)aí`, `-t(h)aoi`, `-t(h)aidhe`, `-t(h)aighe`, `-t(h)ithe`, or `-tithi`.

In the past tenses, the particle `do` is always used here for illustrative purposes. Today, this is usually omitted (except partly in Munster), but it always appears as a `d'` prefix before a vowel and is the historical cause of the consistent lenition in these tenses.

Parentheses indicate additions corresponding to slender or broad stem endings. For example, `-f(a)idh` means either `-faidh` (e.g., `dúnfaidh`) or `-fidh` (e.g., `buailfidh`). In the future and conditional of the second conjugation, reformed spelling uses `-eo-` for slender stems instead of `-ó-`, while older spelling always used an accent: `-eó-`. For example, `salóidh sé` (he will pollute) vs `éireoidh sé` (he will rise).

These conjugation forms are no longer used in this complete manner anywhere. Different forms have been preserved in individual dialects, with a constant trend towards analytic forms, which is more pronounced in the north than in the south. Especially in Ulster and Connacht, synthetic verb endings are primarily used in echo forms.

## Dialectal Differences

In the dialects, the traditional forms have developed differently. The Official Standard, in turn, established forms that are partly no longer used in the dialects but can be explained by the older forms (e.g., `-amar` as a preterite form instead of the more common dialectal `-amair`).

### Endings with -t- or -th-

The conjugation endings with `-t-` (i.e., `-t(e)á`, `-t(e)ar`, `-t(a)í`) originally only occurred where `-th-` was not possible due to delinition.
-   `-t-` occurred after `d, l, n, s, t, th`.
-   `-th-` occurred in all other cases.

The voiced consonants `b` and `g` became voiceless [p] and [k] before `-th-`.
-   *scuab* (to sweep): `scuabthaí` [scuapiː] (one swept), `scuabthar` [scuapər] (one sweeps), `scuabthá` [scuapaː] (you swept).

In the Standard today, `-t-` is used in almost all cases: `scuabtaí`, `scuabtar`, `scuabtá`. In many dialects, the old forms with `-th-` are still alive. In Munster, `-th-` can even occur after `-l`. In the Standard, `-th-` now only appears in the verbal adjective after `b, bh, c, f, g, m, mh, p, r` and after a vowel (e.g., `scuabtha` [scuapə] = swept). After `ch, dh, gh, d, l, n, s, t, th`, `-t-` is used.

### Differences between 1st and 2nd Conjugation

The main difference between the two conjugations is the future marker: `-f-` for the first conjugation and `-ó-` for the second. This distinction exists in all dialects.

In Ulster, forms of the future and conditional in the 2nd conjugation have been preserved that are closer in pronunciation to the old orthography, as the consonant [x] has been retained (though often weakened to [h], and `ó` weakened to [a]):
-   Future: `-óchaidh` [axi, ahi, ahə], e.g., *sáróchaidh sé* (he will surpass).
-   Conditional: `-óchadh` [axu, ahu].

In other dialects, these endings were shortened to `-óidh` (future) [oː] or [oːiː] and `-ódh` (conditional) [oːx], e.g., *sáróidh sé*. The pronunciation of the future ending as [oː] in Connacht occasionally leads to the spelling `-ó`, e.g., *sáró sé*.

In northern Donegal, the stem ending `-igh` of the 2nd conjugation is often dropped when other endings are added, making the forms resemble the 1st conjugation.
-   *ceannaigh* (to buy): *ceannann sé* (he buys), not *ceannaíonn sé* as in other dialects and the Standard.
-   The preterite, which has no ending, remains *cheannaigh sé*.

For monosyllabic verbs of the 1st conjugation ending in `-igh`, this `-igh` is omitted before endings in the Standard. However:
-   In Munster (Muskerry), it often remains as a depalatalised [x], e.g., *feoigh* (to wither) -> *feochann sí* (Standard: *feonn sí*).
-   In northern Donegal, `-igh` remains as [j], e.g., *léigh* (to read) -> *léigheann sé* (Standard: *léann sé*).

For 2nd conjugation verbs ending in `-il, -ir, -in`, the `-ó-` originally replaced the vowel of the last syllable. These older forms have survived as alternatives in some Donegal dialects and on Cape Clear Island.
-   *osclóidh* (will open) has an older alternative *oscólaidh*.
-   *imreoidh* (will play) has an older alternative *imeoraidh*.

### Indicative Present and Habitual Present

#### Analytic Forms
The ending `-(e)ann` was originally only a secondary, dependent form. In Classical Irish, the independent form used `-idh` while dependent forms were endingless or used `-ann`.
-   *buailidh sé* (he strikes) vs *ní bhuail sé* or *ní bhuaileann sé* (he does not strike).

The older `-(a)idh` form is still found in Ulster, especially as a "historical present" in narratives and in positive answers.

Dependent forms without an ending are common with a few verbs:
-   *An meas tú?* (Do you think?)
-   *An gclois tú?* (Do you hear?)

Endingless forms are still standard with some irregular verbs, especially in Ulster:
-   *tig sé* (he comes)
-   *tchí tú* (you see)

In Munster, it is common to use `-ann` in the 2nd person even for verbs that are endingless in the 3rd person: *tánn tú*, *deireann tú*.

The relative form `-(e)as` is no longer found in Munster but is common in Ulster and Connacht, often as `-(e)anns`. In the Standard, it is optional (e.g., *a leanas* = that follows). In Dingle (Munster), an ending `-(e)as` often appears in questions before `tú`, though it is likely not related to the relative form:
-   *An dtuigeas tú?* (Do you understand?)
-   *an bhfuileas tú?* (are you?)

#### Synthetic Forms
-   **2nd person plural (`-taoi`):** Hardly used anymore.
-   **1st person singular (`-(a)im`):** Found regularly and often obligatorily in all dialects, e.g., `buailim` (not `buaileann mé`). With `tá`, both `táim` and `tá mé` are possible. In Ulster, a broad ending `-(e)am` also occurs.
-   **1st person plural (`-(a)imid`):** Obligatory in West Munster and the Standard. In many other dialects (especially Ulster and Connacht), the analytic form `buaileann muid` is more common. In Munster, a long `i` is usually heard: `-(a)imíd`.
-   **2nd person singular (`-(a)ir`):** Rare in Munster, replaced by analytic forms. Occasionally found with irregular verbs: *táir* (you are), *Cad deirir?* (What do you say?). In Connacht, `-(a)inns` is used as an echo form instead (`buailinns`).
-   **3rd person plural (`-(a)id`):** Common in Munster and Connemara. In Connemara, the preterite ending `-adar` is sometimes used in the present instead (`tádar` = they are). The ending `-(a)id` can be used synthetically or analytically: *buailid* or *buailid siad* (they strike).

### Indicative Preterite

#### Analytic Forms
Analytic forms are always endingless. The stem ending `-igh` of the 2nd conjugation is pronounced [ig′] in Munster.

In West Cork (Munster), some verbs of the 2nd conjugation whose verbal noun ends in `-(e)amh` take the ending `-imh` [iv′] in the preterite. This form appears to be derived from the verbal noun.
-   *do sheasaimh sé* (he stood) (Standard: *sheas sé*, Ulster: *sheasaigh sé*).

#### Synthetic Forms
-   **Plural forms:** In spoken Munster Irish, the 1st and 2nd person plural now have a slender `r`: `-(e)amair`, `-(e)abhair`. The 3rd person plural retains a broad `r`: `-(e)adar`. These forms are obligatory in West Munster. The 1st and 3rd person plural also occur in Connacht.
-   **Singular forms:** The 1st and 2nd person singular forms occur regularly in Munster, but mostly only as echo forms in Connacht and Ulster.
-   **Endingless 1st person singular:** For some irregular verbs in Munster: *chonac* (I saw), *chuala* (I heard), *thána* / *thánag* (I came).

### Indicative Imperfect

The imperfect forms are largely preserved in the Standard.
-   Instead of `-(a)imis`, variants like `-(e)amais`, `-(e)amaois`, or `-(a)imís` exist. A long `í` is common in Munster.
-   In Ulster and parts of Munster, an additional `-t` is found in the 1st and 3rd person plural: `-(a)imist`, `-a(i)dist` (e.g., `bhuailimist`).
-   In Connacht, the 1st person plural is uncommon; the analytic form `bhuaileadh muid` is used instead.
-   In West Cork, after the analytic ending `-(e)adh`, the pronouns `sé` and `sí` are pronounced with a broad `s`.

### Indicative Future

Synthetic future forms are now only in regular use in Munster. Elsewhere, they appear mainly as echo forms.
-   In Connacht, `-f(a)is` sometimes occurs as an echo form for the 2nd person singular instead of `-f(a)ir` (`buailfis`).
-   The 2nd person plural `-f(a)idhe` is obsolete.
-   The 1st person plural has variants like `-f(e)amuid` and `-f(e)am` (without `-d`), which continue older dependent forms.
-   The relative form `-f(e)as` (2nd conj. `-óchas`) is not found in Munster but is common in Ulster and Connacht (Ulster `-óchas`, Connacht `-ós`).
-   The original autonomous form was `-f(a)idhear` (2nd conj. `-óchthaidhear`), which is why dialectal forms with a long [iːr] sound still occur.

### Conditional

The synthetic forms are preserved in the Standard, except for the obsolete 2nd person plural.
-   Variants for `-f(a)imis` are similar to the imperfect `-(a)imis`.
-   In Ulster, a form `-fas muid` / `-feas muid` also occurs, alongside the analytic form.
-   In older spelling, the 2nd conjugation did not have `-f-`. Instead of `-ófá`, the form was `-óchthá`. The consonant cluster `-chth-` became `-f-`, assimilating to the 1st conjugation. In Ulster, the older pronunciation is still found: `-óchá` instead of `-ófá`.
-   In West Cork, after the analytic ending `-f(e)adh`, the pronouns `sé` and `sí` are pronounced with a broad `s`.

### Imperative

-   The 2nd person plural in the Standard is now `-igí` instead of the original `-(a)idh`. The older form persisted longer in Munster (pronounced [ig′] or [iːg′]).
-   In Munster, the forms `-ighis` and `-ighís` also occur.
-   The 1st person plural can be `-(e)am` (the original Classical Irish form) or `-(a)imid` instead of `-(a)imis`.
-   In Cork, after the analytic ending `-(e)adh` of the 3rd person singular, `sé` and `sí` are pronounced with a broad `s`.
-   In Classical Irish, the 1st person singular was `-ar`.

### Present Subjunctive

#### Analytic Forms
The ending `-(a)idh` is now written `-a` or `-e` in the Standard, partly reflecting pronunciation and the endingless dependent form of Classical Irish. The 2nd conjugation `-(a)ídh` is now written `-(a)í`.

In some dialects (Ulster and West Muskerry), the older forms with an audible `-(a)idh` have been preserved (pronounced [i(ː)] in Ulster, [ig′] in West Muskerry).
-   *go dtagaidh do ríocht* / *go dtaga do ríocht* (thy kingdom come).

#### Synthetic Forms
Only the 1st person plural is obligatory in the Standard. The other synthetic endings (`-(e)ad`, `-(a)ir`, `-(a)id`) are used optionally in Munster. The forms in Classical Irish were different: 1st sg `-ar`, 2nd sg `-a`, 1st pl `-am`.

### Past Subjunctive

With the verb `bí`, the past subjunctive and conditional are similar: always `dá mbeadh`, never `*dá mbíodh`. However, the 2nd person singular and autonomous forms use `-t-` instead of the conditional's `-f-`:
-   `dá mbeit(h)eá` (if you were)
-   `dá mbeit(h)í` (if one were)

## The Conjugation Endings

The endings listed in traditional grammar largely determine the tense and person. However, some endings repeat across different tenses (e.g., `-imid` for the 1st person plural). Based on their forms, three "series" of endings can be identified.

### 1st Series

This series appears in the **Indicative Present, Indicative Future, and Present Subjunctive**.
-   **Basic forms (1st conj.):** `-im/-ad`, `-ir`, `-idh/-ann`, `-imid`, `-tidh`, `-id`, `-tar`.
-   In direct relative clauses, a relative form `-as` is added in the 3rd person (but not in the subjunctive).
-   In the future, the marker `-f-` precedes the ending.

**Notes on origin:**
-   **1st sg `-im`:** Similar to the Latin subjunctive (`amem`). The Indo-European indicative ending (Latin `-o`, German `-e`) is no longer found in Modern Irish. The form `-ad` (in future `-fad`, subjunctive `-ad`) originally contained a vocalic ending plus an appended pronoun.
-   **2nd sg `-ir`:** Derives from an old 1st person subjunctive/imperative ending `-ar`. In Classical Irish, the present tense 2nd person had a vocalic ending `-a` or `-e`, which is still rarely found in Munster (`Cad deire?`).
-   **3rd sg `-idh`:** Related to the `-t` in German and Latin (`amat`). This form was supplanted by `-ann` in the present indicative but is preserved in the future (`-fidh`) and subjunctive (`-a/-e`).
-   **1st pl `-imid`:** Contains the `-m-` typical of Indo-European languages (Latin `amamus`). The `-d` is an appended pronoun.
-   **2nd pl `-tidhe, -tí`:** Obsolete today, but related to Latin `-tis`.
-   **3rd pl `-id`:** Related to Latin `-ant` (`amant`).
-   **Autonomous `-tar`:** Has passive roots, related to Latin `-tur` (`amatur`).

### 2nd Series

This series appears in the **Indicative Imperfect, Past Subjunctive, and Conditional**, and partly in the **Imperative**.
-   **Basic forms (1st conj.):** `-inn`, `-tá`, `-adh`, `-imis`, `-ídís`, `-tí`.
-   In the conditional, the future marker `-f-` precedes the ending.
-   The etymological origin of these endings is complex.

### 3rd Series

This series appears only in the **Indicative Preterite**.
-   **Basic forms:** `-as`, `-is`, `-`, `-amar`, `-abhar`, `-adar`, `-adh`.
-   The singular endings (`-as`, `-is`) derive from the Old Irish s-preterite.
-   The plural endings (`-amar`, `-abhar`, `-adar`) derive from the formerly deponent (passive) conjugation, hence the `r`.
-   In Connemara, the 2nd person singular present (`-ir`) and preterite (`-is`) can be interchanged in echo forms.
-   Classical Irish had a second past tense form used without the particle `do` (and thus unlenited), whose synthetic ending was `-is`.
