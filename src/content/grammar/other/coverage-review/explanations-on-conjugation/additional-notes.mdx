## Core Concepts

### The Verbal Noun (No Infinitive)

Irish verbs do not have an infinitive form (`infinideach`). Instead, Irish uses the verbal noun to express concepts that would require an infinitive in other languages.

### Verb Root vs. Verb Stem

The **verb root** (`fréamh briathartha`) is the base form of the verb, identical to the 2nd person singular of the imperative (e.g., `mol` - praise!, `beannaigh` - bless!). It is a regular, endingless form.

The **verb stem** (`stoc briathartha`) is the form to which conjugation endings are attached.

-   In the **1st conjugation**, the stem and root are often identical. However, some changes can occur:
    -   Verbs ending in `-áil`, like `sabháil`, undergo depalatalisation: the verb root is `sabháil`, but the stem is `sabhál-` (e.g., `sabhálann`).
    -   Monosyllabic verbs ending in `-igh` with a long vowel, like `dóigh` or `léigh`, drop the `-igh` before endings: the verb roots are `dóigh` and `léigh`, but the stems are `dó-` and `lé-` (e.g., `dónn sé`, `léann sé`).
-   In the **2nd conjugation**, the differences can be greater, often for orthographical reasons. Verbs ending in `-il`, `-ir`, or `-in` often experience syncope (the loss of a vowel). For example, the verb root of `imir` (to play) becomes the stem `imr(igh)-`. The stem is also extended by `-igh` (or orthographically `-í-`).

### Citation Form

The citation form is how a word is listed in a dictionary or grammar book. In languages like English or German, this is typically the infinitive. Since Irish lacks an infinitive, other forms are used:

-   **Verb Root**: The 2nd person singular imperative (e.g., `mol`, `beannaigh`) is the most common citation form used in dictionaries and on these grammar pages. This applies even to irregular verbs where the imperative may differ from other forms (e.g., `tar` - come, `bí` - be).
-   **1st Person Singular Present**: Some dictionaries use this form (e.g., `molaim` - I praise, `beannaím` - I bless).
-   **Verbal Noun**: This is typically only used as a citation form within a larger phrase, e.g., *teach a thógáil* (to build a house).

## Conjugation Classes

### First and Second Conjugation

Irish verbs are divided into two main classes, the 1st and 2nd conjugations (or Type I and Type II).

The primary difference lies in the future and conditional markers:
-   **1st Conjugation**: Uses `-f-` (e.g., *molfaidh sé* - he will praise).
-   **2nd Conjugation**: Uses `-ó-` (e.g., *beannóidh sé* - he will bless).

Other differences are largely due to orthographical rules related to the `-igh` ending of 2nd conjugation verbs. In older spelling, a verb like `beannaigh` would form the present tense as `beannaigheann`. In modern, reformed orthography, this becomes `beannaíonn`. The `-igh` is reflected in the `-í-` of the ending.

-   **2nd Conjugation verbs in `-il`, `-in`, `-ir`, `-is`**: Verbs like `imir` (to play) have an `-igh` in their stem (`imrigh-`) but not their root (`imir`). The present tense `imríonn` reflects the older spelling `imrigheann`.
-   **1st Conjugation verbs in `-igh`**: These are typically monosyllabic and are identified by their use of the `-f-` future marker.
    -   With **long vowels** (e.g., `dóigh`, `léigh`), the `-igh` is dropped in the stem (`dó-`, `lé-`), e.g., *dónn sé* (he burns).
    -   With **short vowels** (e.g., `nigh`, `suigh`), the `-igh` is not dropped, and they behave orthographically much like 2nd conjugation verbs, e.g., `nigheann` > *níonn sé* (he washes). Before the future `-f-`, a long `í` appears: `nighfidh` > *nífidh sé* (he will wash).

### Broad and Slender Stem Endings

The quality (broad or slender) of the final consonant of a verb stem affects the pronunciation of the following ending.

-   **Vowel-initial endings**: The pronunciation is generally the same regardless of the stem.
    -   Broad: `glanaim` [-əm′]
    -   Slender: `tuigim` [-əm′]
-   **Consonant-initial endings** (e.g., with `-t-` or `-f-`): The consonant of the ending matches the quality of the stem.
    -   Broad: `glantar` [-tər], `glanfá` [-fɑː]
    -   Slender: `tuigtear` [-t′ər], `tuigfeá` [-f′ɑː]

**Dialect Note**: In some dialects (e.g., Dingle), consonant-initial endings tend to be consistently broad (`-tar`, `-far`, etc.), even after a slender stem. Conversely, in Muskerry, endings like `-tí` and `-fí` are often slender, even after a broad stem.

## Verb Forms and Functions

### Initial Mutations of Verbs

Verbs with a consonant at the beginning are lenited in the **past tense (preterite), imperfect,** and **conditional**. This is a remnant of the historical past particle `do`, which caused lenition. While `do` is now usually omitted in the standard language (except before vowels), the lenition remains.
- *thuig mé* < *do thuig mé* (I understood)

Before verbs starting with a vowel (or `fh` + vowel), a `d'` prefix is used in these tenses.
- *d’ól mé* (I drank)
- *d’fhoghlaim mé* (I learned)

When other particles or conjunctions are used, they determine the mutation (lenition or eclipsis), and the `d'` is omitted.
- *ar ól tú?* (did you drink?)

The **autonomous form in the past tense** is never lenited. Historically, it would take an h-prefix after `do` (*do hóladh* - one drank). In some dialects, this h-prefix appears even without `do`. In other tenses, the autonomous form is now usually lenited after leniting particles in the standard language.

### Synthetic and Analytic Forms (`foirmeacha táite agus scartha`)

Irish verb conjugation involves two types of forms: synthetic and analytic.

#### Synthetic Forms (`foirm tháite`)

In a synthetic form, the verb and the personal pronoun subject are combined into a single word (`táite` means "connected").

-   A separate personal pronoun subject **must not** follow the verb.
    -   *tuigim* (I understand)
    -   *thuigeamar* (we understood)
-   Emphatic suffixes can be attached.
    -   *tuigimse* (I understand)
    -   *thuigeamarna* (we understood)
-   The 3rd person plural form can exceptionally be followed by a pronoun or noun.
    -   *tuigid* or *tuigid siad* (they understand)
    -   *tuigid na páistí* (the children understand)
-   With multiple subjects, the verb agrees only with the first one.
    -   *Bhíos féin agus Seán ag caint le chéile.* (Seán and I were talking to each other.)

#### Analytic Forms (`foirm scartha`)

In an analytic form, the verb and subject are separate words.

-   A subject (pronoun or noun) **must** follow the verb.
    -   *tuigeann tú* (you understand)
    -   *tuigeann an fear* (the man understands)
-   In writing, the analytic form is the same for all persons (e.g., `tuigeann`). However, in many dialects, the pronunciation of endings like `-idh`, `-adh`, and `-igh` varies depending on whether the following subject is a pronoun (allegro form) or a noun (pausa form).
-   Historically limited to the 3rd person singular, analytic forms are now widely used.
-   Special analytic forms with an `-s` ending, called **relative forms**, are used in direct relative clauses.
    -   *Cé a thuigeas sin?* (Who understands that?)

**Standard vs. Dialectal Usage**

-   **Munster** dialects tend to favour synthetic forms ("traditional conjugation").
-   **Northern** dialects (Connacht and Ulster) use analytic forms more frequently.
-   The **Official Standard** includes a mix of both. The modern standard is more flexible than older versions, allowing for regional variations. For example, both `tuigimid` (synthetic) and `tuigeann muid` (analytic) are now acceptable for "we understand".

Some synthetic forms remain obligatory in the standard (e.g., `tuigim` for "I understand"; `thuigfinn` for "I would understand"), while others that are common in dialects are not included (e.g., `tuigir` for "you understand").

### The Relative Form (`an fhoirm choibhneasta`)

Relative forms are special analytic endings used in the present and future tenses of positive, direct relative clauses. They typically end in `-s`.

| Tense | Dialect | 1st Conjugation (broad/slender) | 2nd Conjugation (broad/slender) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Present** | Standard | `-as` / `-eas` | `-aíos` / `-íos` |
| | Conamara | `-anns` / `-eanns` | `-aíonns` / `-íonns` |
| **Future** | Standard | `-fas` / `-feas` | `-ós` / `-eos` |
| | Ulster | `-fas` / `-feas` | `-óchas` / `-eochas` |

**Usage Notes**:
-   In the **Official Standard**, the relative form is now optional, though it was not included in older versions. The form `a leanas` (that follows) is prescribed, and the relative forms of `bí` (`atá`) and the copula are always used.
-   In **Munster**, relative forms are not used; standard analytic forms are used instead.
-   In **Connacht** and **Ulster**, they are widespread.

Relative forms are used in several contexts, including:
-   When the antecedent is the **subject** of the relative clause (e.g., *an fear a ghlanas an carr* - the man who cleans the car).
-   In temporal clauses after `nuair` (when) and `sul` (before).
-   In comparative clauses after `agus` (as) and `mar` (as/how).
-   In **cleft sentences**, where the verb is always 3rd person and takes the relative form in the present/future.
    -   *Is mise a chreideann do scéal.* (It is I who believes your story.)
    -   *Céard a dhéanann tú?* (What are you doing?)

### Dependent and Independent Forms (`na foirmeacha spléacha agus neamhspléacha`)

Historically, Irish verbs had distinct forms depending on their position in a sentence.

-   **Independent (Absolute) Forms**: Used when the verb is in the initial position or follows particles like the direct relative `a` or the conjunction `má`.
-   **Dependent Forms**: Used after particles like `an`, `ní`, `nach`, `go`, `mura`, etc. These particles often cause mutation (eclipsis or lenition).

For regular verbs, the distinction has mostly disappeared, except for the relative form (which is independent) and the presence of the past particle `d'` (also independent). However, the distinction is still very prominent for five of the irregular verbs, which use different stems for dependent and independent forms.

| | **Absolute / Independent** | **Dependent** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Regular** | *ólaim* (I drink)<br/>*d’ól mé* (I drank) | *ní ólaim* (I do not drink)<br/>*níor ól mé* (I did not drink) |
| **Irregular (`bí`)** | *tá sé ann* (he is there)<br/>*bhí sé ann* (he was there) | *níl sé ann* (he is not there)<br/>*ní raibh sé ann* (he was not there) |

### The Autonomous Form (`an saorbhriathar`)

Also known as the impersonal form, the autonomous form (`saor` means "free") refers to an indefinite, unspecified agent. It is comparable to using "one" or "they" in English. The agent may be unknown, unimportant, or deliberately unstated.

#### Translation

The autonomous form is best translated in one of the following ways:
-   With an impersonal pronoun like **"one"** or **"they"**: *Tuigtear é.* (One understands it. / They understand it.)
-   With a **passive** construction: *Tuigtear é.* (It is understood.)
-   With an **active** verb if an agent is impossible or the action is accidental: *Báitheadh anuraidh é.* (He drowned last year.)

It is not a true passive. Personal pronouns that follow it are in the object form (*Glantar é* - One cleans him), not the subject form.

#### Lenition of the Autonomous Form

-   **Past Tense**: Never lenited (e.g., `cuireadh`). Before a vowel, it takes an h-prefix (`hóladh`).
-   **Other Tenses**: Lenited after leniting particles in the modern standard (`ní chuirtear`). Before a vowel, an h-prefix is used (`ní hóltar`).
-   **Conditional**: Usually lenited.

#### Tenses

The autonomous form exists for all simple tenses and can be used in compound tenses with `bí`.
-   *Táthar ag feitheamh leat.* (One is waiting for you. / You are awaited.)
-   *Bhíothas i ndiaidh airgead a bhailiú.* (Money had been collected.)

#### Idiomatic Uses

The autonomous form is common in idioms, especially those involving fateful events or perception.

**Verbs of fateful events:**
-   `cailleadh`: died (lit. "one lost"), e.g., *Cailleadh go hóg é.* (He died young.)
-   `castar ar`: meets, e.g., *Casadh cailín orm.* (I met a girl, lit. "a girl was turned on me".)
-   `rugadh`: was born, e.g., *Rugadh mac di.* (She had a son, lit. "a son was born to her".)

**Verbs of perception/conjecture:**
The perceiver is indicated with the preposition `do`.
-   `feictear do`: it seems to, e.g., *Feictear dom go bhfuil sí tinn.* (It seems to me that she is ill.)
-   `samhlaítear do`: to imagine, e.g., *Ní hé a mhalairt a samhlaíodh dom.* (I had imagined nothing else.)
-   `taibhrítear do`: to dream, e.g., *Is minic a taibhríodh dom...* (I often dreamed that...)

## The Irregular Verbs (`na briathra neamhrialta`)

There are 11 irregular verbs in Irish. Their irregularity stems from several features:

1.  **Multiple Stems**: They use different verb stems in different tenses (e.g., `tá`, `bhí`, `beidh` for the verb `bí`).
2.  **Special Dependent Forms**: Five verbs have distinct dependent forms that use a different stem (e.g., `tá` vs. `fuil`, `bhí` vs. `raibh`).
3.  **No `-r` on Preterite Particles**: Six verbs use particles like `an`, `ní`, `go` (instead of `ar`, `níor`, `gur`) in the past tense.
4.  **Autonomous Preterite in `-thas`**: Six verbs use this ending instead of `-adh`/`-eadh` in the standard.

## Linguistic and Historical Notes

### Specifying the Agent with the Autonomous Form

In Classical Irish, the agent of an autonomous verb could be specified with prepositions like `le` or `ó`. This is no longer common in modern Irish, where such a construction would imply an instrument (`le`) rather than an agent. However, it can still be found in formal legal texts (often mirroring English passive constructions) and in some dialects (e.g., in Connemara, using `ó`).

### Reflexive Pronouns with the Autonomous Form

Because the autonomous form has no defined subject, a reflexive pronoun (which must refer back to a subject) is logically not possible. If a reflexive meaning is needed, another construction must be used. In colloquial speech, however, sentences sometimes begin with an autonomous form and later use a reflexive pronoun, typically in the 2nd person singular (e.g., *idir do dhá láimh* - between one's [lit. "your"] two hands).

### Etymology of Irregular Verbs

The multiple stems of irregular verbs are not random; they are the result of historical sound changes. Many of these verbs were originally compounds, formed from a preposition and a verb root.

-   **Prototonic vs. Deuterotonic Stress**: In Old Irish, stress could fall on the first syllable (prototonic) or the second (deuterotonic). This led to different sound developments. Today's **dependent forms** often derive from prototonic forms, while **independent forms** derive from deuterotonic forms.
    -   Example (`déan`): The independent `do-ghním` (stress on `ghním`) and dependent `déanaim` (stress on `dé-`) evolved differently.
-   **Future Stems**: Some irregularities trace back to old future formations.
    -   **s-future**: Preserved in `ith` -> `íosfaidh`.
    -   **é-future**: Led to future stems in `beir` -> `béarfaidh` and `deir` -> `déarfaidh`. An additional `-f-` marker was later added.
