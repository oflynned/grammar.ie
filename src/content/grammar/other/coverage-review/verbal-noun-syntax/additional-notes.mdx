## Syntax of the Verbal Noun (`Comhréir an Ainm Bhriathartha`)

The Irish verbal noun can be used in two main ways:

-   The **infinitive use**, corresponding to the infinitive in other languages.
-   The **substantival use**, where it functions as a regular noun.

Within the "infinitive" uses, a further distinction can be made:

-   **Periphrastic constructions**, where the verbal noun is part of the predicate and helps to express certain aspects or compound tenses (`comhaimsirí`).
-   **Infinitive constructions**, which are equivalent to subordinate clauses and function as sentence constituents (e.g., subject or object).
-   **Verbal nouns as attributes**, comparable to present participle constructions.

This separation is not absolute; certain constructions can be adapted for periphrastic, infinitive, or attributive use.

## Periphrastic Constructions (`struchtúir thimchainteacha`)

These are also known as circumscribing constructions, verbal periphrases, or compound tenses. In Irish, certain aspects of an action that cannot be expressed by simple tenses are formed using periphrastic constructions.

With the verbal noun, these are primarily:
-   the **progressive** aspect
-   the **prospective** aspect (the intention form)
-   the **terminative** aspect (a perfect form)

Another perfect form, the resultative aspect, is formed with the verbal adjective.

The basic structure of periphrastic constructions consists of an inflected form of the auxiliary verb `bí` and the verbal noun of another verb, introduced by a preposition. The choice of preposition determines the aspect (progressive, prospective, perfect, etc.).

The preposition and verbal noun together form the predicate of the sentence as a predicative with the verb `bí`. The auxiliary verb `bí` can sometimes be omitted in what is known as a Small Clause.

Direct objects are handled differently depending on the aspect. In the progressive aspect, objects typically appear as genitive objects after the verbal noun. In perfect and prospective constructions, they appear before the verbal noun, connected with `a`.

The progressive aspect describes actions that are in progress, similar to English "continuous" forms. Colloquially, the progressive present is the usual way to express present actions, while the simple present (with a few exceptions) expresses habitual actions.

## The Progressive Aspect (`an fhoirm leanúnach`)

### General Information

The Irish progressive aspect is formed with the auxiliary verb `bí` (to be) and a preposition followed by a verbal noun. The most common preposition is `ag` (at, on), but `do` (to) or `a` (to) are used in certain cases.

### The Preposition `ag` in the Progressive Aspect

In the progressive aspect, the preposition `ag` is often treated more like a "progressive aspect particle," which affects its pronunciation. While the full form is usually pronounced [eg′], at normal speaking speed it is often shortened:

-   **[əg]** or **[əg′]** after a consonant and before a vowel.
    -   *Tá Seán ag ól.* (Seán is drinking.) [taː s′aːn əg oːl]
    -   *Tá Cáit ag éisteacht.* (Cáit is listening.) [taː kaːt′ əg′ eːs′t′axt]
-   **[g]** or **[g′]** between vowels, as the `a`- [ə] becomes silent.
    -   *Tá tú ag ól.* (You are drinking.) [taː tuː g oːl]
    -   *Tá sí ag éisteacht.* (She is listening.) [taː s′iː g′ eːs′t′axt]
-   **[ə]** between consonants, as the `-g` [g]/[g′] becomes silent.
    -   *Tá Seán ag tuiscint.* (Seán understands.) [taː s′aːn ə tis′k′ən′t′]
    -   *Tá Pádraig ag léamh.* (Pádraig is reading.) [taː paːrig′ ə l′eːv]
-   **[Ø]** (completely silent) after a vowel and before a consonant.
    -   *Tá tú ag tuiscint.* (You understand.) [taː tuː tis′k′ən′t′]
    -   *Tá Síle ag léamh.* (Síle is reading.) [taː s′iːl′ə l′eːv]

Occasionally, when the `-g` is silent, the spelling `a’` is used, e.g., *Tá Seán a’ tuiscint.*

### Tense of the Verb `bí`

The verb `bí` can appear in any tense and mood, creating a progressive present, past, future, etc.

| Tense             | Example                               | Meaning                                                              |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Present           | *Tá sé ag léamh.*                     | He is reading (right now).                                           |
| Habitual Present  | *Bíonn sé ag léamh.*                  | He reads (usually, for a certain duration).                          |
| Preterite         | *Bhí sé ag léamh.*                    | He was reading (at a specific time).                                 |
| Imperfect         | *Bhíodh sé ag léamh.*                 | He used to read (usually, for a certain duration).                   |
| Future            | *Beidh sé ag léamh.*                  | He will be reading (then).                                           |
| Conditional       | *Bheadh sé ag léamh.*                 | He would be reading; He would have been reading.                     |
| Imperative        | *Bí ag léamh!*                        | Read (now)!                                                          |
| Subjunctive       | *Go raibh sé ag léamh*<br/>*go mbé sé ag léamh* | May he be reading (right now)<br/>until he reads (then)              |
| Verbal Noun       | *bheith ag léamh*                     | to be reading                                                        |

### Progressive Aspect without the Verb `bí`

A form of the verb `bí` is omitted in certain situations, resulting in a "Small Clause". This can be the object or subject of other verbs or appear without an introductory verb. This applies to other uses of `bí`, not just the progressive.

The following types of verbs can introduce a Small Clause:

-   A **verb of perception** (e.g., `clois` = to hear, `feic` = to see, `tabhair faoi deara` = to notice). This form expresses perceiving someone in the middle of an action. The subject of the progressive is the object of the main sentence.
    -   *Feicim an fear ag foghlaim.* (I see the man learning.)
    -   *Cloiseann tú Séan ag teacht.* (You hear Seán coming.)
    -   A pronoun object often stands after the verbal noun: *Nuair a chonaic mé ag imeacht thú …* (When I saw you leaving …)
    -   Some verbs of perception are used with a preposition (`éist le`, `breathnaigh ar`), which comes before the Small Clause.
        -   *D’éist mé le Síle ag ceol.* (I listened to Síle singing.)
        -   With a pronoun, a prepositional pronoun is used: *D’éist mé léi ag ceol.* (I listened to her singing.)

-   A **verb of movement** (e.g., `téigh` = to go, `tar` = to come, `imigh` = to go away). This often describes the manner of movement.
    -   *Tagann siad ag siúl agus ag léim.* (They come walking and jumping.)
    -   Sometimes it implies purpose: *Tagann sé ag foghlaim Gaeilge.* (He comes to learn Irish.)

-   A **verb of beginning/ending/continuing** (e.g., `tosaigh` = to begin, `stad` = to stop, `lean` = to continue).
    -   *Thosaigh mé ag ól.* (I began to drink.)
    -   *Choinnigh sé ag cur ceisteanna.* (He kept asking questions.)
    -   *Stad mé ag caitheamh tobac.* (I stopped smoking.)
    -   Some of these verbs can be followed by `ar`, with a prepositional pronoun referring back to the subject: *Ach leanann siad orthu ag caitheamh.* (But they continue smoking.)

-   The **verb `cuir`** (to put, place) in a causative sense: to make someone do something.
    -   *Cuirfidh sé ag gáire thú.* (It will make you laugh.)
    -   *Chuir mé ag scríobh na litreach é.* (I made him write the letter.)
    -   *Chuir mé an litir á scríobh aige.* (I arranged for the letter to be written by him.)

-   The **verb `tarlaigh`** (to happen) and similar verbs.
    -   *Tharla é ag dul abhaile nuair …* (It happened that he was going home when …)

-   **Multiple progressive forms** can appear in succession.
    -   *Bhí mé ag dul ag iascach.* (I went fishing.)
    -   *Chonaic mé ag tosú ag foghlaim thú.* (I saw you begin to learn.)

Such Small Clauses can also appear **without an introductory verb**, especially with the conjunction `agus` in a temporal sense.
-   *Agus é ag gáire thit sé go talamh.* (While he was laughing, he fell to the ground.)

**Dialect Note (Ulster):** In Ulster, with verbs of movement and verbs of beginning/ending, the preposition `a` (from `do`) with lenition is common instead of `ag` to express purpose. This is sometimes called the "infinitive of purpose".
-   *Tháinig mé a ól.* (I came to drink.)
-   *Stad sé a chur báistí.* (It stopped raining.)
-   If the purpose is not implied, `ag` is used: *D’imigh sé ag damhsa* (He went dancing) vs. *D’imigh sé a dhamhsa* (He went in order to dance).

**Usage Notes:**
-   `Stad` is often used with `de`: *Stad sé den chaint.* (He stopped talking.)
-   In Munster, `tosnaigh ar` and `crom ar` require a full infinitive construction, not just a progressive Small Clause. *Thosnaigh sé ar an bpáirc a threabhadh.* (He began to plough the field.)
-   In Ulster, the idiom `bí ar obair ag` is used for "to begin": *Tá sé ar obair ag foghlaim Gaeilig.* (He has begun to learn Irish.)

### Verbal Noun in the Progressive Aspect

#### Possible Verbal Nouns

Any verbal noun can be used in the progressive aspect except for `bheith`, the verbal noun of `bí`. A form like *`*Tá mé ag beith cúramach`* is incorrect; one simply says *Tá mé cúramach* (I am careful).

Verbs of thinking, feeling, and speaking are often used in the progressive to emphasise the ongoing nature of the action.
-   *Tá mé ag ceapadh go bhfuil sé tinn.* (I am thinking/considering that he is ill.)
-   *Ceapaim go bhfuil sé tinn.* (I think that he is ill.)

#### Form of the Verbal Noun

After the preposition `ag`, the verbal noun is always unlenited.
-   *Tá mé ag teacht.* (I am coming.)

The preposition `ag` requires the dative, so in some dialects, dative forms of the verbal noun may appear.
-   *Tá an capall ag seitrigh.* (The horse is neighing.) (Dative of `seitreach`)

### Subject of the Progressive Aspect

The subject can be any person, singular or plural.
-   *Tá mé / Táim ag teacht.* (I am coming.)
-   *Tá siad / Táid ag teacht.* (They are coming.)

A noun can also be the subject.
-   *Tá Pól ag teacht.* (Paul is coming.)

The autonomous form of `bí` (`táthar`) is used for an impersonal "one".
-   *Táthar ag teacht.* (One is coming.)
-   *Táthar ag múineadh na teanga.* (The language is being taught.)

This has a similar meaning to the progressive passive but does not allow an agent to be specified.

Some unaccusative verbs have no grammatical subject.
-   *Tá ag éirí go maith liom san fhisic.* (I am doing well in physics.)
-   *Tá ag briseadh ar m’fhoighne.* (My patience is running out.)

### Intransitive Progressive Aspect (`an fhoirm leanúnach neamhaistreach`)

This is the progressive aspect without a direct object.

| `Form of bí` + Subject + `ag` + VN |
| :--- |
| *Tá mé ag léamh.* (I am reading.) |
| *Tá tú ag teacht.* (You are coming.) |

Indirect objects with prepositions follow the verbal noun.
-   *Tá muid ag féachaint ar an teilifís.* (We are watching television.)

### Transitive Progressive Aspect (`an fhoirm leanúnach aistreach`)

This is the progressive aspect with a direct object. The structure depends on whether the object is a noun, pronoun, or clause.

#### Progressive Aspect with a Noun as Object

A noun object usually follows the verbal noun in the genitive case. The preposition `ag` is used.

| `Form of bí` + Subject + `ag` + VN + Genitive-Object |
| :--- |
| *Tá mé ag léamh an nuachtáin.* (I am reading the newspaper.) |
| *Tá sí ag scríobh na litreach.* (She is writing the letter.) |

**Lenition of the Object:**
Indefinite objects are mostly unlenited today, except in some common expressions.
-   *Tá siad ag baint phrátaí.* (They are harvesting potatoes.)
-   *Tá sé ag fáil bháis.* (He is dying.)

Definite objects (like names) are usually lenited.
-   *Tá sé ag moladh Mháire.* (He is praising Máire.)

#### Progressive Aspect with a Pronoun as Object

A possessive pronoun is used instead of a personal pronoun. The preposition is `do` (which combines with `a` to form `á`).

| `Form of bí` + Subject + `do` + Possessive Pronoun + VN |
| :--- |
| *Tá tú do mo bhualadh.* (You are hitting me.) |
| *Tá siad á chur.* (They are sowing it.) |

In Ulster and Munster, forms with `ag` are also possible for the 1st and 2nd person.

If the possessive pronoun refers back to the subject, it forms the **progressive passive**.
-   *Tá an teach á thógáil.* (The house is being built.)

Sentences can be ambiguous if the pronoun could refer to the subject or another entity.
-   *Tá sé á dhíol.* can mean "He is selling it" (active) or "He is being sold" (passive).

An emphatic suffix (`-sa`, `-san`) on the verbal noun clarifies the active meaning by emphasising the object.
-   *Tá tú do mo mholadhsa.* (You are praising me.)

The reflexive pronoun `féin` creates the **reflexive progressive**.
-   *Tá sí á bualadh féin.* (She is hitting herself.)

A demonstrative pronoun (`seo`, `sin`, `siúd`) is added after the verbal noun.
-   *Tá tú á dhéanamh seo.* (You are doing this [masc.].)
-   *Tá tú á déanamh seo.* (You are doing this [fem.].)
-   *Tá tú á ndéanamh seo.* (You are doing these [pl.].)

The reciprocal pronoun `a chéile` (each other) is treated like a noun object.
-   *Tá na buachaillí ag bualadh a chéile.* (The boys are hitting each other.)

#### Progressive Aspect with a `go/nach`-Clause as Object

In Munster especially, a proleptic pronoun `á` (`ag/do` + `a`) is used before the verbal noun, anticipating the clause. Elsewhere, `ag` is common.

| Structure                                                |
| :------------------------------------------------------- |
| `Form of bí` + Subject + `á` / `ag` + VN + `go/nach`-Clause |
| *Tá mé á rá go bhfuil tú sásta.* (I am saying that you are satisfied.) |
| *Tá mé ag rá go bhfuil tú sásta.* (I am saying that you are satisfied.) |

This also applies to indirect questions.
-   *Tá mé á fhiafraí díot an bhfuil tú sásta.* (I am asking you if you are satisfied.)

#### Progressive Aspect with an Infinitive Construction as Object

A bare verbal noun or a transitive infinitive construction follows the first verbal noun.

| `Form of bí` + Subject + `ag` + VN + VN-Construction |
| :--- |
| *Tá mé ag iarraidh é a fhiafraí de.* (I am trying to ask him it.) |

In Munster, `á` is sometimes used instead of `ag`. With the verbal noun `iarraidh` (to try), Munster uses `a d’` instead of `ag`.
-   *Tá sé a d’iarraidh an cheist a chur air.* (He is trying to ask him the question.)

#### Progressive Aspect with a Separated Noun as Object

If the object is separated from the verbal noun (e.g., fronted for emphasis or in a relative clause), it is in the nominative/accusative, and the particle `a` (with lenition) is used instead of `ag`.

This occurs in:
1.  **Direct relative clauses where the object is the antecedent.**
    -   *an obair atá Pól a dhéanamh* (the work that Paul is doing)
    -   *Céard atá Pól a dhéanamh?* (What is Paul doing?)
2.  **Narrative fronting in negative sentences.** The object is moved to the front for emphasis.
    -   *Pingin ní raibh mé a shaorú.* (I did not earn a penny.)
3.  **Semi-negated sentences** with `ach` (only).
    -   See below.

### Reflexive Progressive Aspect (`an fhoirm leanúnach athfhillteach`)

This is formed like the progressive with a pronominal object, but the possessive pronoun refers back to the subject, and the reflexive pronoun `féin` (self) is required.

| `Form of bí` + Subject + `do` + Possessive Pronoun + VN + `féin` |
| :--- |
| *Tá sé á bhualadh féin.* (He is hitting himself.) |

-   *Tá mé do mo bhualadh féin.* (I am hitting myself.)
-   *Tá siad á mbualadh féin.* (They are hitting themselves.)

Without `féin`, the sentence would be passive or a non-reflexive active progressive.

### Progressive Passive Aspect (`an fhoirm leanúnach chéasta`)

This form is identical to the progressive with a pronominal object, but the possessive pronoun is always reflexive, referring back to the subject.

| `Form of bí` + Subject + `do` + Possessive Pronoun + VN |
| :--- |
| *Tá an teach á thógáil.* (The house is being built.) |

-   *Tá mé do mo bhualadh.* (I am being beaten.)
-   *Tá siad á mbualadh.* (They are being beaten.)

In the 3rd person, this can be ambiguous without context.
-   *Tá sé á dhíol.* can mean "He is selling it" (active) or "He is being sold" (passive).

An agent can be added with the preposition `ag`.
-   *Tá an teach á thógáil agam.* (The house is being built by me.)

In Munster, the agent can optionally stand before the verbal noun.
-   *Tá an teach agam á thógáil.* (The house is being built by me.)

#### Special Form: Progressive Passive with Auxiliary `déan`

A verbal noun can be the grammatical subject of a progressive passive construction with `déanamh`. This allows passive forms for both transitive and intransitive verbs.

| `Form of bí` + VN + `á` + `d(h)éanamh` + `ag` + Agent |
| :--- |
| *Tá rith á dhéanamh againn.* (Running is being done by us.) |

-   *Tá cur á dhéanamh ag na mná.* (The sowing is being done by the women.)
-   *Tá uasghrádú á dhéanamh ar an suíomh seo.* (This site is currently being updated.)

### Progressive Aspect in Relative Clauses

#### Subject as Antecedent

A direct relative clause is used. The progressive construction is unchanged, using `ag`.

| Subject + `a` + Relative Form of `bí` + `ag` + VN + Genitive-Object |
| :--- |
| *Nach maith an fear atá ag déanamh na hoibre?* (Isn't the man good who is doing the work?) |
| *Cé atá ag damhsa ansin?* (Who is dancing there?) |

Often, the relative particle and verb are omitted, leaving `ag` + VN as an attribute.
-   *Casadh fear dóibh ag dul aníos an bóthar.* (They met a man going up the road.)

#### Direct Object as Antecedent

##### Direct Relative Clause

The particle `a` (with lenition) is used instead of `ag`. This is common in questions about the object. In Munster, the progressive passive is often preferred.

| Object + `a` + Relative Form of `bí` + Subject + `a` + VN |
| :--- |
| *an obair atá an fear a dhéanamh* (the work that the man is doing) |
| *Cad é atá tú a dhéanamh?* (What are you doing?) |

If multiple verbal nouns are involved, `a` replaces `ag` only before the one whose object is the antecedent.
-   *an leabhar a bhí sé ag tosú a léamh* (the book he began to read)

##### Indirect Relative Clause

Alternatively, an indirect relative clause can be used with a reflexive pronoun `á`. This is less common.

| Object + `a` + Abbreviated Form of `bí` + Subject + `do` + Possessive Pronoun + VN |
| :--- |
| *an obair a bhfuil an fear á déanamh* (the work that the man is doing) |
| *Cad é a bhfuil tú á dhéanamh?* (What are you doing?) |

#### Conjunction `mar` instead of an Antecedent

After the conjunction `mar` (as), `a` with lenition is used instead of `ag`.

| `mar` + `a` + Relative Form of `bí` + Subject + `a` + VN |
| :--- |
| *... ná mar a bhí siad a fháil roimhe sin* (... than they were getting before) |

#### Cleft Sentences with Fronted Adverbs

When adverbs like `fada`, `annamh`, `minic` are fronted in a cleft sentence, the relative particle and `bí` can be omitted, leaving a Small Clause.
-   *Ba mhinic Síle ag léamh.* (Síle often read.)
-   *B’annamh gan Síle ag léamh.* (Síle rarely did not read.)

### Progressive Passive Aspect in Relative Clauses

#### Grammatical Subject as Antecedent

A direct relative clause is used.

| Subject + `a` + Relative Form of `bí` + `do` + Possessive Pronoun + VN + `ag` + Agent |
| :--- |
| *an obair atá á déanamh ag an fear* (the work that is being done by the man) |
| *Cad atá á dhéanamh agat?* (What is being done by you?) |

In Munster, this passive form is often preferred over the active relative clause when the object is fronted.

#### The Agent as Antecedent

An indirect relative clause is used because of the required reflexive prepositional pronoun (`aige`, `aici`, `acu`).

| Agent + `a` + Dependent Form of `bí` + Subject + `do` + Possessive Pronoun + VN + `ag` + Reflexive Pronoun |
| :--- |
| *an fear a bhfuil an obair á déanamh aige* (the man by whom the work is being done) |

Alternatively, the preposition `ag` can be moved before the relative particle.
-   *an fear ag a bhfuil an obair á déanamh* (the man by whom the work is being done)

### Progressive Aspect in Semi-Negated Sentences

To express "only", Irish often uses a negated sentence with `ach` (but). If the object follows `ach`, it is separated from the verbal noun, and `a` is used instead of `ag`.

| Negative Form of `bí` + Subject + `a` + VN + `ach` + Object |
| :--- |
| *Níl mé a léamh ach an nuachtán.* (I am only reading the newspaper.) |

### Perfect Progressive Aspect (`an fhoirm leanúnach fhoirfe`)

This is for actions that have just been completed after being in progress for some time. It is formed with `tar éis` (after).

| `Form of bí` + Subject + `tar éis` + `bheith` + `ag` + VN + Genitive-Object |
| :--- |
| *Tá mé tar éis bheith ag léamh an nuachtáin le tamall.* (I have just been reading the newspaper for a while.) |

A perfect progressive passive is not used. Instead, a mixed perfect with the verbal adjective is possible: *Tá sé tar éis bheith déanta agam.* (It has just been done by me.)

## Further Periphrastic Constructions

### With the Preposition `le`

This construction is used prospectively or modally in Connacht and Ulster to express purpose, duty, necessity, or possibility. In Munster, `chun` is used for intention.

-   With **intransitive verbs**, the meaning is **active**. It expresses an intended or expected future action.
    -   *Tá mé le teacht.* (I intend to come / am expected to come.)
    -   *an t-am atá le teacht* (the time that is to come)
-   With **transitive verbs**, the meaning is **passive**. It corresponds to a "to be done" construction.
    -   *Tá sé le feiceáil.* (It is to be seen.)
    -   *Tá sé le déanamh.* (It is to be done.)
    -   An agent can be added with `ag`: *Tá obair le déanamh agam.* (Work is to be done by me.)

This construction can also be used attributively: *obair le déanamh* (work to be done).

### With the Preposition `chun`

This form prospectively expresses that something will happen, either intentionally or simply in the future. It is always **active**.
-   *Tá na páistí chun dul abhaile.* (The children intend to/will go home.)

It can also describe being ready or fit for an action. Here, it is passive with transitive verbs and active with intransitive verbs, and the verbal noun is in the genitive.
-   *Tá an féar chun gearrtha.* (The grass is ripe to be cut.)
-   *Tá Pól chun iascaigh.* (Paul is ready for fishing.)

### With the preposition `gan`

This form expresses that something has not been done. It is resultative. With intransitive verbal nouns, it is active; with transitive, it is passive.
-   *Tá an leabhar seo gan scríobh fós.* (This book has not yet been written.)
-   *Bhí an bád gan imeacht fós.* (The boat had not yet departed.)

With transitive verbal nouns in the active, it expresses that someone is not doing something, focusing on the resulting state.
-   *Bhí mé gan aon bhlúire bia a ithe.* (I had not eaten a bite.)

### With the preposition `ar`

1.  **The genuine preposition `ar`**
    This form expresses a continuous passive state and is restricted to certain verbal nouns. `Ar` does not cause lenition here.
    -   *Tá sé ar oscailt.* (It is open.)
    -   *Tá an leabhar ar fáil anseo.* (The book is available here.)
    -   *an obair atá ar siúl* (the work that is underway)

2.  **The preposition `iar` > `ar`**
    `Ar` (from older `iar` = after) with the possessive pronoun `a` forms `arna` to express a **perfect passive**. It is common in official texts and publishing credits.
    -   *arna chur in eagar ag Niall Ó Dónaill* (edited by Niall Ó Dónaill)
    -   *arna fhoilsiú ag Oifig an tSoláthair* (published by the Stationery Office)
    -   In a full sentence, it is rare today: *Treoir X mar atá arna leasú...* (Directive X, as amended...)

### With other prepositions

-   **`a`**: With adjectives like `furasta` (easy) and `deacair` (difficult).
    -   *Tá sin chomh deacair a dhéanamh.* (That is so difficult to do.)
-   **`thar`**: "more than".
    -   *Tá sé thar a bheith maith.* (It is more than good.)

## Infinitive Constructions (`frásaí ainm bhriathartha`)

Irish has no true infinitive. The verbal noun is used in "infinitive constructions" that function like subordinate clauses. Unlike in English, these constructions can have their own subject.

## Simple Infinitive Constructions

### Infinitive Construction with and without Object

**Intransitive (without object):**
This consists of the bare verbal noun. It is negated with `gan`.

| Positive | `VN` | *Tá mé sásta siúl.* (I am ready to go.) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Negative | `gan` + `VN` | *Tá mé sásta gan siúl.* (I agree not to go.) |

Some verbal nouns of motion and `bheith` often take the preposition `a` (to).
-   *Ba mhaith liom a dhul chun na farraige.* (I would like to go to the sea.)
-   *Ba mhaith liom (a) bheith in Éirinn.* (I would like to be in Ireland.)

**Transitive (with direct object):**
The object stands before the verbal noun, separated by the preposition `a` (to), which causes lenition.

| Positive | Object + `a` + VN | *Tá mé sásta an leabhar a léamh.* (I am ready to read the book.) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Negative | `gan` + Object + `a` + VN | *Tá mé sásta gan an leabhar a léamh.* (I am content not to read the book.) |

Indirect objects and other complements always stand after the verbal noun.

**With a pronoun as object:**
The pronoun simply stands before `a`.

| Positive | Pronoun + `a` + VN | *Tá mé sásta iad a dhíol.* (I agree to sell them.) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Negative | `gan` + Pronoun + `a` + VN | *Tá mé sásta gan iad a dhíol.* (I agree not to sell them.) |

Less commonly, a possessive pronoun can be used instead, without `a`.
-   *Tá mé sásta a ndíol.* (I agree to sell them.)

**Semi-negated:**
To express "only", `gan ... ach` is used.

| Structure | Example |
| :--- | :--- |
| `gan` + `a` + VN + `ach` + Object | *Tá mé sásta gan a shiúl ach míle amháin.* (I agree to walk only one mile.) |

**With indirect object:**
The indirect object follows the verbal noun. `a` is not used unless there is also a direct object.

| Structure | Example |
| :--- | :--- |
| `VN` + Prep. + indir. Object | *Ba mhaith liom féachaint ar an teilifís.* (I want to watch television.) |

**With clauses as object:**
A `go/nach`-clause or indirect question follows the verbal noun. It is anticipated by a proleptic pronoun `a` (his).

| Structure | Example |
| :--- | :--- |
| `a` + VN + `go/nach`-clause | *Ba mhaith liom a fheiceáil go bhfuil tú sásta.* (I want to see that you are happy.) |

**With another infinitive construction as object:**
One infinitive construction can be the object of another.
-   *Thiocfadh leis iarraidh ort dul chun na farraige.* (He could ask you to go to the sea.)

**In a relative clause:**
If the object is the antecedent of a relative clause, the construction is split.
-   *Sin rud is furasta a dhéanamh.* (That is something that is easy to do.)
-   *Cá bhfuil an capall a dúirt tú liom a cheannach?* (Where is the horse you told me to buy?)

### Use of Infinitive Constructions

Infinitive constructions are used:
-   **As the object of a finite verb:** *Bhain mé triall as é a dhíol.* (I tried to sell it.)
    -   With `déan` as an auxiliary verb: *Rinne sé an capall a dhíol.* (He sold the horse.)
    -   With `abair` to form an indirect command: *Dúirt sé liom an capall a dhíol.* (He told me to sell the horse.)
-   **As the subject of a copula sentence:** *Ba mhaith liom iad a cheannach.* (I would like to buy them.)
-   **After modal constructions:** *Ba mhaith liom an carr seo a cheannach.* (I would like to buy this car.)
-   **After nouns:** *tráth é a rá* (time to say it).
-   **As a short sentence** (e.g., in headings, instructions): *eascanna a fhriochadh* (to fry eels).
-   **After `agus`** to link clauses: *...mura bhfeicfinn an fear agus é a chloisteáil* (...if I had not seen the man and heard him).
-   **After some conjunctions** like `murach`, `ach`, `agus` in specific senses.

### Infinitive Construction with Subject

Unlike in English, an Irish infinitive construction can have its own subject.

#### Intransitive Infinitive Construction with Subject

There is a dialectal split.

| Region | Structure | Example | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **North (Ulster)** | Subject + VN | *tú pósadh* | that you marry |
| **South (Connacht/Munster)** | Subject + `a` + VN | *tú a phósadh* | that you marry |

The Ulster form avoids ambiguity. *Ba mhaith liom tú pósadh* (I want you to marry) is distinct from *Ba mhaith liom tú a phósadh* (I want to marry you). In the South, the latter sentence is ambiguous.

#### Transitive Infinitive Construction with Subject

There is also a North/South split here.

| Region | Structure | Example | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **North (Ulster)** | Subject + Object + `a` + VN | *an fear an obair a dhéanamh* | that the man does the work |
| **South (Munster)** | Subject + `a` + VN + Genitive-Object | *an fear a dhéanamh na hoibre* | that the man does the work |
| **South (Munster)** | Subject + `do` + Object-Possessive Pronoun + VN | *an fear á dhéanamh* | that the man does it |

The standard form for the last example uses `á` (`do` + `a`).

#### Special Form: Logical Subjects with Prepositions

In sentences with a logical subject expressed by a preposition (e.g., *Tá airgead agat*), this logical subject can be fronted in an infinitive construction with `bheith`. This is typical of Ulster.
-   *Ba mhaith liom tú airgead a bheith agat.* (I want you to have money.)
In other dialects, the fronted subject is omitted: *Ba mhaith liom airgead a bheith agat.*

#### Special Form: Omission of `bheith`

The verbal noun `bheith` is often omitted, creating a Small Clause.
-   *Is mór an trua gan é anseo.* (It is a pity that he is not here.) (from *...gan é a bheith anseo*)
-   *Is annamh báisteach ann.* (There is rarely rain.) (from *Is annamh a bhíonn báisteach ann.*)

### Passive Infinitive Construction

Infinitive constructions can have a passive meaning depending on context. More explicit passive forms are derived from finite passive constructions and use `bheith`.

-   **Progressive Passive:** *Is féidir rud éigin a bheith á dhéanamh aige.* (It is possible that something is being done by him.)
-   **Perfect Passive:** *Is féidir rud éigin a bheith déanta aige.* (It is possible that something has been done by him.)
-   **Prospective Passive:** *Is féidir rud éigin a bheith le déanamh aige.* (It is possible that something is to be done by him.)

## Infinitive Construction of Purpose

These forms correspond to "in order to".

### With `le` / `chun`

| Type | Structure | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Intransitive** | `le`/`chun` + VN | *le féachaint air* (in order to look at it) |
| **Transitive** | `le`/`chun` + Object + `a` + VN | *le gloine a ól* (in order to drink a glass) |

-   In Connacht and Munster, `le` becomes `le n-` before a vowel. In Standard and Ulster, it becomes `le h-`.
-   In Munster, `le` is common with intransitive verbs and `chun` with transitive verbs.

### With Other Prepositions

Other prepositions can express purpose, such as `d’fhonn`, `ar mhaithe le`, `fá choinne` (Ulster), and `le haghaidh` (Connacht).
-   *d’fhonn fearg a chur air* (in order to make him angry)
-   *leisce bréag a inseacht* (in order not to tell a lie)

### Without Prepositions

After verbs of thinking and saying, a simple infinitive construction can express purpose.
-   *Shíl sé é a dhéanamh.* (He intended to do it.)

### Construction of Purpose with `ag`

After verbs of motion, a progressive-style construction can indicate purpose.
-   *Tháinig sé ag ól gloine.* (He came to drink a glass.)

### Construction of Purpose with `a`

In Ulster, `a` (`< do`) is used instead of `ag` after verbs of motion for this purpose. This is often called the "infinitive of purpose".
-   *Tháinig sé a ól gloine.* (He came to drink a glass.)

## Infinitive Constructions of Simultaneity / Anteriority / Posteriority

These constructions function as temporal adverbials, with a logical subject introduced by `do` + agent.

-   **Simultaneity ("while"):**
    -   *le linn gloine a ól dom* (while I drink a glass)
    -   *ag ól na gloine duit* (while you drink the glass)
-   **Anteriority ("after"):**
    -   *tar éis gloine a ól dom* (after I have drunk a glass)
    -   *arna ól dom* (after I have drunk it)
-   **Posteriority ("before"):**
    -   *roimh gloine a ól dom* (before I drink a glass)

## Further Infinitive Constructions

### With Further Prepositions and Conjunctions

Many prepositions and conjunctions can be followed by an infinitive construction.
-   `gan`: without doing something
-   `in áit`: instead of doing something
-   `toisc`: because
-   `d’ainneoin`: despite
-   `trí`: by means of

### After Phrasal Verbs

Verbs with fixed prepositions (`tabhair faoi`, `staon ó`) can take an infinitive construction as their object.
-   *Thug mé faoi é a dhéanamh.* (I tried to do it.)
-   *Staon mé óna dhéanamh.* (I refrained from doing it.)

### With `agus` and `ach`

These conjunctions can introduce infinitive constructions or Small Clauses with special circumstantial meanings (temporal, conditional).
-   *Agus é ag gáire thit sé go talamh.* (While he was laughing, he fell to the ground.)
-   *Gheobhaidh muid é ach fanacht leis.* (We will get it, provided that we wait for it.)

## Verbal Noun as Attribute

The verbal noun can act as an attribute of a noun, which is its logical subject. This often corresponds to a present participle.

| Form | Example |
| :--- | :--- |
| Noun + Genitive-Object + `a` + VN | *fear scéil a insint* (a man telling a story) |
| Noun + Genitive-VN + Genitive-Object | *fear inste scéil* (a man telling a story) |
| Noun + Possessive Pronoun + Genitive-VN | *fear a inste* (a man telling it) |

The genitive form used can be the substantival genitive (for intransitive use) or the verbal adjective (for transitive use).
-   *lucht óil* (drinkers)
-   *lucht ólta poitín* (poteen drinkers)

## General Notes

### Verbal Noun Constructions after Prepositions

Verbal noun constructions are generally treated as "enclosed". Prepositions preceding them usually do not cause internal changes like dative or genitive case marking, or initial mutations (unless an article is present).
-   *chun an bhean a phósadh* (in order to marry the woman)
-   *leis an bhfear a phósadh* (in order to marry the man)

### Genitive in Verbal Noun Constructions

The use of the genitive is complex:
-   **In the progressive**, a noun object is in the genitive if it is definite or indefinite without an attribute. It is in the nominative if it is indefinite with an attribute.
    -   *ag léamh an leabhair* (reading the book)
    -   *ag léamh leabhair* (reading a book)
    -   *ag léamh leabhar maith* (reading a good book)
-   **In infinitive constructions**, the object is in the nominative/accusative.
    -   *an bhean a phósadh* (to marry the woman)
-   **After genitive prepositions** (`chun`, `i ndiaidh`), the verbal noun construction generally remains unchanged (no genitive). However, if a possessive pronoun is the object, the verbal noun itself takes the genitive form.
    -   *chun é a fháil* (in order to get it)
    -   *chun a fhála* (in order to get it)

## Substantival Use

### Use as an Ordinary Noun

The verbal noun can be used as a regular noun, without reference to an agent or object.
-   *Tá an léamh go suimiúil.* (Reading is interesting.)
-   *Fuair mé orduithe uaidh.* (I received orders from him.)

It can be identified as a regular noun when it appears with an article, an adjective, a number, or in the plural.
-   `an léamh` (the reading)
-   `léamh maith` (good reading)
-   `dhá ordú` (two orders)
-   `orduithe` (orders)
