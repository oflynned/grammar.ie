## Tense, Mood, Voice and Aspect

In Irish, verbs are described using four main categories:
*   **Tense** (`an aimsir`): When an action happens (present, past, future).
*   **Mood** (`an modh`): The speaker's attitude towards the action (e.g., a fact, a command, a possibility).
*   **Voice** (`an fhaí`): Whether the subject performs the action (active) or receives it (passive).
*   **Aspect** (`an ghné`): The duration or completion of an action (e.g., ongoing, completed, habitual).

## Tense (`an aimsir`)

Tense places an action in time. The Irish indicative mood distinguishes between the simple present and habitual present, the preterite and the imperfect (past), and the future.

These combine to form eight **simple tenses** (`aimsirí simplí`), which are created by adding specific endings to the verb. Simple tenses are traditionally divided into two groups:

*   **Primary tenses** (`aimsirí príomha`): Indicative present and habitual present, indicative future, subjunctive present, and imperative.
*   **Past tenses** (`aimsirí stairiúla`) or **secondary tenses** (`aimsirí tánaisteacha`): Indicative preterite, indicative imperfect, subjunctive preterite, and conditional.

A key feature of past tenses is the use of the prefix `d’` and lenition, which are remnants of the old past verbal particle `do`.

In addition to simple tenses, Irish uses **compound tenses** (`aimsirí comhshuite`) to express other aspects, such as the progressive (ongoing), perfect (completed), and prospective (expected) aspects. These are formed with the auxiliary verb `bí`, which is conjugated in one of the simple tenses.

### The Present (`an aimsir láithreach`) and Habitual Present (`an aimsir ghnáthláithreach`)

The present tense distinguishes between two aspects: non-habitual and habitual actions. This results in two present tenses: the simple present and the habitual present.

*   **Simple Present** (`an aimsir láithreach`) describes a specific action happening at the current moment.
*   **Habitual Present** (`an aimsir ghnáthláithreach`) describes an action that happens regularly (e.g., usually, always, often, sometimes, rarely, never).

The verb `bí` has separate forms for each:

| Simple Present | Habitual Present |
| :--- | :--- |
| `Tá sé` = he is | `Bíonn sé` = he is (usually) |
| *Tá sé fuar anocht.* = It is cold tonight. | *Bíonn sé fuar istoíche.* = It is cold at night. |

For all other verbs, the simple and habitual present share a common form. However, their usage differs:

*   **In written language**, this common form is used for both non-habitual and habitual actions, as well as for ongoing actions. Context determines the meaning. This style is common in sports commentaries, news reports, and formal rituals.
    *   *Cromann sí síos, ardaíonn an sliotar agus buaileann, agus téann sé thar an trasnán.* = She bends down, lifts the sliotar and strikes it, and it goes over the crossbar.
    *   *Baistím thú in ainm an Athar agus an Mhic agus an Spioraid Naoimh.* = I baptise you in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit.
*   **In colloquial language**, the usage depends on the verb type:
    *   For verbs of thinking, feeling, and perception, the present form can be either non-habitual or habitual, depending on context.
        *   Non-habitual: *Feiceann sé anois.* = He sees now.
        *   Habitual: *Feiceann sé go minic.* = He sees often.
    *   For all other verbs, the present form is understood only as the **habitual** present.
        *   *Glanann sé go minic.* = He cleans often.
        *   *Ólaim tae le bainne.* = I (usually) drink tea with milk.
    *   To express a simple, non-habitual present action colloquially, the progressive aspect is used instead.
        *   *Tá sé ag glanadh anois.* = He is cleaning now.
        *   *Tá mé ag ól tae le bainne.* = I am (currently) drinking tea with milk.

#### Relative Form in the Present

In a direct relative clause, the 3rd person present tense has a special relative form ending in `-s`.
*   *an fear a ghlana(nn)s an carr* = the man who cleans the car.
*   The relative form of `tá` is `atá`.
*   The relative form of `bíonn` is `a bhíos`.

#### Present instead of Future

Using the present tense to refer to the future is generally not common in Irish; the future tense is preferred.
*   *Tiocfaimid amárach.* = We will come tomorrow.

An exception is the verb `bí`. The simple present `tá` is often used with a future meaning for a planned event. The progressive aspect can also be used this way for other verbs, usually with a time marker.
*   *Táimid ag filleadh amárach.* = We will return tomorrow.

In real conditional clauses, `tá` can be used instead of the future to emphasise the certainty of the outcome. For other verbs, the perfect (`tá` + verbal adjective) is used.

After `má` (if) and `cha` (not), the habitual present is used instead of the future.

#### `Tá` instead of `bíonn`

When an action is assumed to be ongoing, the simple present `tá` is used instead of the habitual `bíonn`, as the habitual nature is already understood.
*   *Tá mé i mo chónaí i nGaillimh.* = I live in Galway. (...and I continue to do so).

#### The Gnomic Present (`an láithreach ginearálta`)

The **habitual present** is used for general, timeless statements or observations.
*   *Itheann na beithígh féar.* = Cows eat grass.
*   *Fithisíonn an Domhan an ghrian.* = The Earth orbits the sun.

The **simple present of `bí` (`tá`)** is used for states that are always true.
*   *Tá an fharraige goirt.* = The sea is salty.

The **habitual present (`bíonn`)** is used for states that recur but are not constant.
*   *Bíonn sé fuar istoíche.* = It is cold at night.

For general but punctual events, especially in clauses introduced by `nuair` (when) or `má` (if), the **future tense** is often used (a "gnomic future").
*   *Nuair atá an duilleog feoite, is beag an t-aithleá a scoiltfidh den chraobh í.* = When the leaf is withered, a mere breath of wind will separate it from the branch.
*   *Ní íosfaidh aon bhó é.* = No cow eats it (if it encounters it).

#### The Historical Present (`an láithreach stairiúil`)

The historical present is a stylistic device used in narratives to make past events more vivid.

*   In Ulster, the old present ending `-(a)idh` is used instead of `-(e)ann` in the 3rd person.
    *   *Glanaidh sé é.* = He cleans/cleaned it.
    *   *Le sin preabaidh Sergeant Leland chun tosaigh...* = With that Sergeant Leland springs/sprang forward...
*   In literature, an analytic form with the ending `-(e)as` is sometimes used.
    *   *Glanas Seán é.* = Seán cleans/cleaned it.
    *   *Ritheas Oilill isteach.* = Oilill runs/ran in.

The simple present `tá` cannot be used as a historical present. Instead, `bíonn` is used. In Ulster, the preterite `bhí` (was) is used.

### The Preterite (`an aimsir chaite`)

The preterite describes a single, completed action in the past. It is the standard narrative tense and is often used where English would use the perfect, pluperfect, or even future perfect.
*   *Thug sé dom é.* = He gave it to me. / He has given it to me. / He had given it to me.
*   *Rinne sé é.* = He did it. / He has done it. / He had done it.

For non-punctual (longer-lasting or recurring) past actions, the imperfect is used.
*   *Thugadh sé dom é.* = He gave it to me (regularly).

Independent verb forms in the preterite are lenited. This is caused by the historical past particle `do`. This particle is sometimes still written as `do` before consonants in Munster, and always appears as a `d'` prefix before a vowel or `fh-`.
*   *(Do) bhuail mé é.* = I hit him.
*   *D’ól mé é.* = I drank it.

The autonomous form is not lenited (except for some irregular verbs) and does not take the `d'` prefix.

### The Imperfect (`an aimsir neamhfhoirfe`) or Habitual Preterite (`an aimsir ghnáthchaite`)

The imperfect is the habitual form of the past. It describes a recurring or longer-lasting action, similar to the English "used to...".
*   *(Do) dhéanadh sé an obair go maith.* = He used to do the work well.
*   *D’oladh sé gach lá.* = He drank every day.

The form is identical to the subjunctive preterite, but the imperfect is lenited and takes the `d'` prefix in independent forms.

**Preterite vs. Imperfect:**
*   Preterite: *Bhuail sé é.* = He hit him (a single blow).
*   Imperfect: *Bhuaileadh sé é.* = He used to hit him / He was hitting him (repeatedly or for a duration).

In Ulster, a common alternative is the construction `ba ghnách le` + subject + verbal noun.
*   *Ba ghnách leat an obair a dhéanamh go maith.* = You usually did the work well.

### The Future (`an aimsir fháistineach`)

The future tense is used for future events, but also to express:
*   **Desire:** *Beidh deoch agam.* = I want to have a drink.
*   **Instructions:** *Rachaidh tú síos go dtí an droichead.* = Go down to the bridge.
*   **Offers/Suggestions:** *An osclóidh mé an fhuinneog?* = Shall I open the window?
*   **Ability:** *Ní inseofar an ghlóir fhlaithiúnais.* = One cannot describe heavenly glory.

After the conjunction `má` (if) and the negative particle `cha` (not), the habitual present is used instead of the future.
*   *Glanfaidh tú* (you will clean) becomes *má ghlanann tú* (if you clean).

In a direct relative clause, the future has a special relative form ending in `-s`.
*   *an fear a ghlanfas an carr* = the man who will clean the car.

## The Mood (`an modh`)

Mood indicates the speaker's attitude towards an action—whether it is real, desired, conditional, or merely possible.

### The Indicative (`an modh táscach`)

The indicative is the mood of reality, used for stating facts. It is the most common mood and is used in all the tenses described above. Unlike some languages, Irish does not typically change to the subjunctive in indirect speech, though the future indicative does change to the conditional if the main clause is in a past tense.

#### The Indicative of Certainty (`táscach na cinnteachta`)

In conditional sentences, certain indicative forms can be used in the result clause (apodosis) to emphasise the certainty of the outcome.

*   **Present instead of Future (with `bí`)**: In real conditional clauses.
    *   *Má théann tú ann tá deireadh leat.* = If you go there, it is your end.
*   **Perfect instead of Future**: In real conditional clauses.
    *   *Má chloistear thú tá tú gafa.* = If you are heard, then you are already caught.
*   **Pluperfect instead of Conditional**: In unreal conditional clauses.
    *   *Murach í bhí sé imithe.* = If it weren't for her, he would certainly have gone.
*   **Preterite instead of Conditional**: In unreal conditional clauses.
    *   *Muna mbeadh tusa, do bhíos marbh acu.* = If you had not been there, they would certainly have killed me.

### The Conditional (`an modh coinníollach`)

The conditional is the mood of possibility, used for actions that depend on a condition. It is equivalent to "would..." in English. The Irish conditional has only one form and can refer to the past, present, or future depending on context.
*   *Rachainn inniu.* = I would go today.
*   *Rachainn amárach.* = I would go tomorrow.
*   *Rachainn inné.* = I would have gone yesterday.

Independent forms are lenited and take the `d'` prefix.
*   *(Do) dhéanfadh sé an obair.* = He would do the work.

The conditional is used:
*   For actions dependent on an unreal condition: *Thiocfadh sé dá…* = He would come if...
*   In the condition clause itself (with `dá`/`mura`): *…dá mbeadh an t-am aige.* = ...if he had the time.
*   As a future-in-the-past (e.g., in indirect speech): *Dúirt sé go mbeadh sé ann.* = He said he would be there.
*   For polite questions and requests: *An dtabharfá dom an t-im, le do thoil?* = Would you please give me the butter?
*   In comparisons with `mar` (as if): *Bhog sé a cheann mar a bheadh sé ag seinm.* = He moved his head as if he were playing music.
*   To express anger or impatience: *Bhrisfinn a mhuineáil!* = I could break his neck!
*   After `dóbair` (almost): *Dóbair go dtitfinn.* = I almost fell.
*   After `b’fhéidir` (perhaps) instead of the future: *B’fhéidir go mbeadh sí ann.* = Perhaps she will be there.
*   In rhetorical questions: *Cad a dhéanfadh mac an chait ach luch a mharú?* = What else does the cat's son do but kill mice? (Like father, like son).
*   Instead of the imperfect (similar to English "would" for "used to").

### The Imperative (`an modh ordaitheach`)

The imperative is the command form. It has one tense, which is future-oriented.

*   **Primary Imperative**: The direct command form for the 2nd person.
    *   *Glan!* = Clean! (singular)
    *   *Glanaigí!* = Clean! (plural)
*   **Secondary Imperative**: Forms from other moods are used for other persons.
    *   **1st person singular** ("let me..."): Same form as the indicative present. *Glanaim!* = Let me clean!
    *   **3rd person** ("he shall..."): Same form as the subjunctive preterite, but without mutation. *Glanadh sé!* = Let him clean!
    *   **1st person plural** ("let us..."): Same form as the subjunctive preterite. *Glanaimis!* = Let's clean!

The negative imperative (prohibitive) uses the special particle `ná`.
*   *Ná glan é!* = Don't clean it!
*   *Ná habair é!* = Don't say it! / You're welcome.

#### Usage of the Imperative

*   **Commands and Requests**: Primarily the 2nd person. *Déan é!* = Do it!
*   **General Prohibitions**: The autonomous form is used, especially on signs. *Ná caitear tobac!* = No smoking!
*   **3rd Person Commands**: Often directed at a 2nd person to allow or prevent an action by a third party. *Ná bíodh eagla ort!* = Don't be afraid! (lit. "Let no fear be on you!").
*   **1st Person Singular**: Often a request directed at the listener. *Ná feicim sin arís!* = Let me not see that again!
*   **1st Person Plural**: For requests including the speaker. *Téimis!* = Let's go!
*   **Indifference**: To mean "whether or not". *bíodh sé sásta nó ná bíodh* = whether he is satisfied or not.
*   **Concession**: `bíodh go/nach` can mean "although". *Bíodh nach dtuigim é...* = Although I don't understand it...
*   **Interjections**: *Abair é!* = You're right!
*   **Citation Form**: The 2nd person singular imperative is the dictionary form of a verb, e.g., `déan`.

### The Subjunctive (`an modh foshuiteach`)

The subjunctive is the mood of possibility, for actions that are not real but possible or desired. It only appears in dependent clauses, usually after conjunctions that cause eclipsis.

*   **Subjunctive Present** (`an foshuiteach láithreach`): Refers to a future possibility. It is often replaced by the indicative future in modern Irish.
*   **Subjunctive Preterite** (`an foshuiteach caite`): Can refer to past, present, or future. Its form is identical to the indicative imperfect. It is often replaced by the conditional.

The subjunctive is used:
*   **Wishes (Optative)**: With `go` (positive) or `nár` (negative).
    *   *Go raibh maith agat!* = Thank you! (lit. "May there be good at you").
    *   *Nár éirí sin leat!* = May that not succeed for you!
*   **After temporal conjunctions** like `go` (until) and `sula` (before).
    *   *Fan go dtaga mé.* = Wait until I come.
*   **To express purpose** with `go` (so that).
    *   *Rachaidh mé chun an aonaigh go gceannaí mé bó.* = I will go to the fair to buy a cow.
*   **In unreal conditional clauses** after `dá`/`mura` (if/if not).
    *   *Ní íosfainn an fheoil sin dá bhfaighinn bás den ocras.* = I would not eat that meat even if I were to die of hunger.
*   **In exclamations of wish or regret** with `dá`.
    *   *Dá dtéadh sé go Gaillimh!* = If only he had gone to Galway!

## Voice (`an fhaí`)

### The Active Voice (`an fhaí ghníomhach`)

In the active voice, the subject of the sentence performs the action. This is the standard form for verbs.
*   *Glanann sé an carr.* = He cleans the car.

### The Passive Voice (`an fhaí chéasta`)

In the passive voice, the subject receives the action. The direct object of the active sentence becomes the subject of the passive sentence. The agent (the one performing the action) can be mentioned using the preposition `ag`.

Irish does not have a simple passive formed with verb endings. Instead, it uses several periphrastic (multi-word) constructions.

#### Resultative Perfect Passive

This construction uses `bí` + object + verbal adjective + `ag` + agent. It emphasises the state resulting from an action.
*   *Tá an bord briste agam.* = The table has been broken by me. / I have broken the table.

#### Terminative Perfect Passive

This form uses `bí` + subject + `tar éis` + possessive pronoun + verbal noun. It is less common and primarily found in Connacht.
*   *Tá an bhó tar éis a bléan.* = The cow has just been milked.

#### Progressive Passive

This construction uses `bí` + subject + `á` (`ag` + possessive pronoun) + verbal noun + `ag` + agent. It describes an action in progress.
*   *Tá an teach á thóigeáil agam.* = The house is being built by me.

#### Prospective Passive

This construction uses `bí` + subject + `le` + verbal noun + `ag` + agent. It indicates that an action is to be done.
*   *Tá an balla le péinteáil agam.* = The wall is to be painted by me.

#### Mixed Passive

A combination of perfective and progressive aspects uses `tar éis bheith` + verbal adjective.
*   *Tá an teach tar éis bheith tóigeáilte agam.* = The house has just been built by me.

#### Passive with `faigh`

The verb `faigh` (to get) can be used with a verbal adjective to form a passive, influenced by English "get done".
*   *Fuair sé gortaithe.* = He was injured. (He got hurt).

#### Autonomous Form instead of Passive

The autonomous form is grammatically active, but it can be translated with a passive sense. It describes an action without specifying an agent.
*   *Briseadh an bord.* = The table was broken. (or: One broke the table).
*   *Déantar é.* = It is done. (or: One does it).

## Aspect (`an ghné`)

Aspect describes the nature of an action's flow in time. Irish can express several aspects grammatically:
*   **Habitual**: Customary, recurring actions.
*   **Progressive**: The ongoing nature of an action.
*   **Resultative**: The result of a completed action.
*   **Terminative**: The completion of an action.
*   **Prospective**: Actions that are expected to occur.

### Perfect Tenses (`na haimsirí foirfe`)

Perfect tenses describe completed actions. They are used less frequently than in English, with the preterite often being preferred. Irish has several ways to form the perfect, each with a different aspectual nuance. They are compound forms using the auxiliary verb `bí`.

By changing the tense of `bí`, a perfect can be formed for every tense and mood (e.g., pluperfect, future perfect).

| | Resultative | Terminative | Translation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Present Perfect** | `Tá sé déanta agam.` | `Tá mé tar éis é a dhéanamh.` | I have done it. |
| **Pluperfect** | `Bhí sé déanta agam.` | `Bhí mé tar éis é a dhéanamh.` | I had done it. |
| **Future Perfect** | `Beidh sé déanta agam.` | `Beidh mé tar éis é a dhéanamh.` | I will have done it. |
| **Conditional Perfect** | `Bheadh sé déanta agam.` | `Bheinn tar éis é a dhéanamh.` | I would have done it. |

#### Formation of the Perfect with the Verbal Adjective

This form is **resultative**, emphasising the state after an action.
*   **Intransitive**: `bí` + Subject + Verbal Adjective
    *   *Tá siad imithe.* = They have gone.
*   **Transitive**: `bí` + Object + Verbal Adjective + `ag` + Subject
    *   *Tá an litir scríofa aici.* = She has written the letter.

This construction is formally a passive, but it functions as the primary active perfect construction in Irish because `bí ... ag` is used to mean "to have".

#### Formation of the Perfect with `tar éis` and Verbal Noun

This form is **terminative**, emphasising that an action has just been completed. The preposition `i ndiadh` is used in Ulster.
*   **Intransitive**: `bí` + Subject + `tar éis` + Verbal Noun
    *   *Tá mé tar éis snámh.* = I have just swum.
*   **Transitive**: `bí` + Subject + `tar éis` + Object + `a` + Verbal Noun
    *   *Tá sé tar éis an litir a scríobh.* = He has just written the letter.

#### Mixed Perfect

This combines the two perfect forms using `tar éis bheith` + verbal adjective. It is passive and emphasises the recent completion of an action.
*   *Tá sé tar éis bheith déanta aige.* = It has just been completed by him.

#### `deireadh` + Verbal Adjective

This construction also expresses the completion of an activity.
*   *An bhfuil deireadh ite agat?* = Are you finished eating?

#### Formation of the Perfect with `ar` and Verbal Noun

This form is passive and emphasises the state of an action.
*   *Tá an clog ar stad.* = The clock has stopped.
*   *Tá an doras ar oscailt.* = The door is open.

#### Anteriority Perfect with `do` + Subject

This construction describes an action that happened before the main clause's action. It is a sentence fragment, not a full sentence.
*   *ar oscailt an dorais dom* = after I had opened the door.
*   *ar dhéanamh na hoibre duit* = after you have done the work.

### The Progressive Aspect (`an fhoirm leanúnach`)

The progressive aspect describes an ongoing action and can be used in all tenses and moods by conjugating the verb `bí`.
*   **Present Progressive**: Used for actions happening now. In colloquial speech, this is the standard non-habitual present.
    *   *Tá mé ag féachaint ar an teilifís.* = I am watching television right now.
*   **Habitual Present Progressive**: Used for durational activities that happen regularly.
    *   *Bím ag féachaint ar an teilifís sa tráthnóna.* = I usually watch television in the afternoon.

### The Intentional Form (`an fhoirm aidhmeach`)

This form expresses an action that is about to happen, using phrases like `ar tí` (about to) or `le` (with the intention of).
*   *Bhí mé ar tí an carr a dhíol.* = I was about to sell the car.

### `bí` + `le` + Verbal Noun

This prospective construction can express purpose, duty, necessity, possibility, or certainty. With transitive verbs, it is passive.
*   *Tá an obair le déanamh agam.* = The work is to be done by me.

## Notes

### Note 1: Historical view of present tense endings

Some 19th-century grammars incorrectly claimed a distinction between a *simple* present (with synthetic endings like `-im`, `-ir`, `-idh`) and a *habitual* present (with the analytic ending `-ann`/`-eann`). For example, `buailidh sé` (he strikes) vs. `buaileann sé` (he strikes always). This was a misunderstanding. The ending `-eann` was originally a dependent form, not an exclusively habitual one. Today, the present progressive is used for the simple present, and the simple tense forms primarily express the habitual present (with some exceptions).

### Note 2: Future tense after `má`

While the habitual present is the standard form after `má` (if) when referring to the future, some grammar sources note that the future tense is possible if there is a clear reference to a specific event, or to emphasise that the condition will undoubtedly be met.
*   *Molfaidh sibh í má bheidh an moladh tuillte aici.* = You will praise her if she will have deserved the praise.

### Note 3: Subjunctive vs. Future Indicative

The statement that the present subjunctive is often replaced by the future indicative is relative. In spoken Irish, the forms can be very similar or identical, especially as the future marker `-f-` can be weak. The distinction is clearer in writing and with irregular verbs (e.g., `go dtaga sé` vs. `go dtiocfaidh sé`). Semantically, an action that is merely *possible* (subjunctive) is by nature in the future, making the future tense a logical substitute. The future indicative also carries a stronger sense of certainty. This replacement is most common in Munster Irish.

### Note 4: Attributive vs. Predicative Verbal Adjectives

In the perfect construction, the verbal adjective is used predicatively. This can sometimes lead to ambiguity, especially with masculine nouns where an attributive adjective would not be lenited.
*   *Tá an doras briste agam.* could mean "I have the broken door" (attributive) or "I have broken the door" (predicative perfect). The latter meaning is far more common.
With feminine nouns, the distinction is clear due to lenition:
*   *Tá an fhuinneog bhriste agam.* = I have the broken window. (attributive)
*   *Tá an fhuinneog briste agam.* = I have broken the window. (predicative)

### Note 5: Historical view of perfect formation

The linguist H. Zimmer stated in 1901 that perfect formations with the verbal adjective were generally only used with transitive verbs. He noted `imigh` (to go) as a rare exception he found for an intransitive verb. This contrasts with the modern language, where this perfect form is common with intransitive verbs in Connacht and Munster.

### Note 6: The `bí + ag` construction with adjectives

The perfect construction `tá sé déanta agam` (I have done it) is structurally similar to an older construction using regular adjectives, such as `Tá sé ullamh agam` (I have it ready). In this case, there is no passive meaning, only the resultative sense of "having" something in a certain state.
