In Irish, the role of the infinitive is filled by the **verbal noun** (`an tAinm Briathartha`). It is a versatile form that has properties of both a noun and a verb. Other terms for this form include verbal substantive or noun of activity.

A verbal noun can describe an activity and sometimes the result of that activity.

**Noun-like properties:**
*   It has a grammatical gender (masculine or feminine).
*   It can be declined, meaning it can have genitive and dative forms.
*   It can take a genitive attribute or a possessive pronoun as its object.
*   It is frequently used with prepositions like `ag`, `a`, `do`, `chun`, `le`, and `tar éis`.

**Verb-like properties:**
*   It can be modified by adverbs.
*   It can take a direct object, which is usually connected to the verbal noun with a preposition.

## Formation of the Verbal Noun

The formation of the verbal noun is highly irregular. While there are common patterns, many common verbs have unique verbal noun endings. Dialects may also use different forms for the same verb (e.g., `feiscint` vs. `feiceáil` for "to see").

### First Conjugation Verbs

#### Regular Formation
The most common regular ending is `-adh` or `-eadh` (masculine).
*   `bris` → `briseadh` (to break)
*   `mol` → `moladh` (to praise)
*   `cas` → `casadh` (to turn)
*   `póg` → `pógadh` (to kiss)

Often, the verb root is depalatalised (made broad):
*   `buail` → `bualadh` (to strike)

#### Other Common Formations

**Verbs ending in `-áil`**
The verbal noun has the same `-áil` ending (feminine).
*   `péinteáil` → `péinteáil` (to paint)

**Verbs ending in a long vowel + `-igh`**
The verbal noun ends in the long vowel (`-á`, `-é`, `-í`, `-ó`, `-ú`) and is masculine. This form historically comes from endings like `-óghadh`.
*   `báigh` → `bá` (to drown)
*   `pléigh` → `plé` (to discuss)
*   `cloígh` → `cloí` (to conquer)
*   `dóigh` → `dó` (to burn)
*   `dreoigh` → `dreo` (to wither)

**Verbs ending in a short vowel/consonant + `-igh`**
The verbal noun ends in `-í` (masculine).
*   `suigh` → `suí` (to sit)
*   `figh` → `fí` (to weave)
*   `nigh` → `ní` (to wash)

#### Irregular Formations

**No ending (zero ending)**
The verbal noun is the same as the verb stem.
*   `ól` → `ól` (to drink)
*   `at` → `at` (to swell)
*   `díol` → `díol` (to sell)
*   `fás` → `fás` (to grow)
*   `léim` → `léim` (to jump)

**No ending + depalatalisation**
The final slender consonant of the verb stem becomes broad.
*   `coisc` → `cosc` (to prevent)
*   `cuir` → `cur` (to put)
*   `braith` → `brath` (to feel)
*   `loit` → `lot` (to destroy)
*   `tomhais` → `tomhas` (to measure)

**Ending `-amh` (masculine)**
*   `déan` → `déanamh` (to do/make)
*   `seas` → `seasamh` (to stand)
*   `caith` → `caitheamh` (to throw, spend)
*   `léigh` → `léamh` (to read)
*   `maígh` → `maíomh` (to declare)
*   `maith` → `maitheamh` (to forgive)

**Ending `-áil` (feminine)**
*   `fág` → `fágáil` (to leave)
*   `gabh` → `gabháil` (to take)
*   `tóg` → `tógáil` (to lift)

**Ending `-úint` (feminine)**
*   `créid` → `créidiúint` (to believe)
*   `lean` → `leanúint` (to follow)

**Ending `-an` (masculine)**
*   `lig` → `ligean` (to allow)
*   `leag` → `leagan` (to lay, knock down)

**Ending `-int` (feminine)**
*   `féach` → `féachaint` (to look)
*   `tuig` → `tuiscint` (to understand)
*   `tairg` → `tairiscint` (to offer)

**Ending `-t` (feminine)**
*   `bain` → `baint` (to extract)
*   `roinn` → `roinnt` (to divide)
*   `deighil` → `deighilt` (to separate)

**Ending `-im` or `-m` (feminine)**
*   `tit` → `titim` (to fall)
*   `druid` → `druidim` (to close)
*   `seinn` → `seinm` (to play music)
*   `gair` → `gairm` (to call)

**Ending `-acht` (feminine)**
*   `fan` → `fanacht` (to wait, stay)
*   `éist` → `éisteacht` (to listen)
*   `gluais` → `gluaiseacht` (to move)

**Rarer endings**
*   `-achtáil` (f): `mair` → `maireachtáil` (to live, survive)
*   `-íl` (f) / `-ach` (m/f): `scread` → `screadaíl`/`screadach` (to scream)
*   `-achan` (f): `buaigh` → `buachan` (to win)
*   `-idh` (f): `iarr` → `iarraidh` (to ask)
*   Dialectal endings include `-bh(e)áil` and `-st(e)an`.

### Second Conjugation Verbs

#### Regular Formation
The regular ending is `-ú` (masculine), which historically comes from `-ughadh`.
*   `saothraigh` → `saothrú` (to work hard)

For verbs ending in a slender `-l`, `-n`, or `-r`, the verbal noun often ends in `-t` (feminine).
*   `oscail` → `oscailt` (to open)
*   `imir` → `imirt` (to play)

#### Irregular Formations

**Ending `-í` (masculine)**
*   `éirigh` → `éirí` (to rise)
*   `cónaigh` → `cónaí` (to reside)
*   `fiafraigh` → `fiafraí` (to ask)

**No ending (zero ending)**
The verbal noun is the same as the verb stem (sometimes minus `-igh`).
*   `aithris` → `aithris` (to imitate)
*   `foghlaim` → `foghlaim` (to learn)
*   `damhsaigh` → `damhsa` (to dance)

**No ending + depalatalisation**
*   `ceangail` → `ceangal` (to connect)
*   `iompair` → `iompar` (to carry)
*   `siúil` → `siúl` (to walk)

**Ending `-ach` (masculine)**
*   `ceannaigh` → `ceannach` (to buy)
*   `cumhdaigh` → `cumhdach` (to cover)
*   `cuardaigh` → `cuardach` (to search)

**Ending `-acht` (feminine)**
*   `dúisigh` → `dúiseacht` (to awaken)
*   `imigh` → `imeacht` (to go away)

**Ending `-achtáil` or `-áil` (feminine)**
*   `airigh` → `aireachtáil` (to perceive)
*   `teagmhaigh` → `teagmháil` (to meet, touch)
*   `coinnigh` → `coinneáil` (to keep, maintain)

**Ending `-amh` (masculine)**
*   `áirigh` → `áireamh` (to count, calculate)
*   `smaoinigh` → `smaoineamh` (to think)
*   `dealraigh` → `dealramh` (to appear)

### Verbal Nouns without a Corresponding Verb

Some verbal nouns exist without a full set of corresponding verb forms. These are often used to describe an ongoing activity, particularly one associated with a profession or a tool. They are commonly formed with suffixes like `-óireacht`, `-aireacht`, and `-aíocht`.

*   `feirmeoir` (farmer) → *Tá sé ag feirmeoireacht.* (He is working as a farmer.)
*   `siopadóir` (shopkeeper) → *Tá sé ag siopadóireacht.* (He is shopping.)
*   `Gaeilgeoir` (Irish speaker) → *Bíonn sé ag Gaeilgeoireacht ó dhubh go dubh.* (He speaks Irish from dawn till dusk.)
*   `cabaire` (chatterbox) → *Tá siad ag cabaireacht.* (They are chattering.)
*   `scéalaí` (storyteller) → *Tá sé ag scéalaíocht.* (He is telling stories.)

This can also happen with diminutives in `-ín`, forming verbal nouns in `-ínteacht` or `-íneacht`.
*   `croisín` (a tool for harvesting seaweed) → *Bhí sé ag croisínteacht.* (He was harvesting seaweed.)
*   `sláimín` (a small amount) → *Tá sé ag sláimínteacht.* (He is taking small amounts.)

## Declension of the Verbal Noun

As a noun, the verbal noun can be declined into the genitive, dative, and plural.

### The Genitive Case

The genitive of a verbal noun can be formed in two main ways, depending on its function in the sentence.

#### Verbal Adjective as Genitive
The verbal adjective is often used as the genitive form when the verbal noun is used transitively, i.e., when it takes an object (expressed as a genitive object or a possessive pronoun).

*   `ól` (to drink) → *lucht ólta fuisce* (whiskey drinkers; literally "people of the drinking of the whiskey")
*   `foghlaim` (to learn) → *lucht foghlamtha na Gaeilge* (Irish learners)
*   `bualadh` (to strike) → *chun a mbuailte* (to strike them)

Verbal nouns ending in `-adh`, `-ú`, and other long vowels (`-á`, `-é`, `-í`, `-ó`) **always** use the verbal adjective as their genitive form, for both transitive and intransitive uses.
*   `glanadh` (cleaning) → genitive `glanta`
*   `saothrú` (working hard) → genitive `saothraithe`
*   `suí` (sitting) → genitive `suite`

#### Substantive Genitive
A regular, noun-like genitive is formed according to the verbal noun's declension when it is used intransitively (without an object) or in a purely substantive sense.

*   `bréith` (to bear) → *lá breithe* (birthday; "day of birth")
*   `ól` (to drink) → *lucht óil* (drinkers; "people of the drinking")
*   `foghlaim` (to learn) → *lucht foghlama* (learners; "people of the learning")

Some verbal nouns **always** use a substantive genitive, even when transitive. This is true for those ending in:
*   `-áil` (genitive `-ála`)
*   `-áint` (genitive `-ána`)
*   `-úint` (genitive `-úna`)
*   `-cht` (genitive `-chta`)

#### When Substantive Genitive and Verbal Adjective Coincide
For some second conjugation verbs ending in `-l`, `-n`, or `-r` with verbal nouns in `-t`, the substantive genitive and the verbal adjective happen to have the same form.
*   `oscailt` (to open) → genitive and verbal adjective `oscailte`
*   `imirt` (to play) → genitive and verbal adjective `imeartha`

### The Dative Case

In Standard Irish, the dative form of a verbal noun is the same as its nominative form. This is particularly relevant when forming the progressive aspect with the preposition `ag`.

However, in some dialects, distinct dative forms exist for feminine verbal nouns of the second declension that end in a broad consonant. This is common for verbal nouns ending in `-ach`/`-each` that describe sounds or continuous actions. The dative is formed with `-aigh`/`-igh`.

*   `seitreach` (neighing) → *Tá na capaill ag seitrigh.* (The horses are neighing.)
*   `géimneach` (lowing) → *Tá na ba ag géimnigh.* (The cows are lowing.)
*   `béiceach` (shouting) → *Thosaigh m’athair ag béicigh.* (My father began to shout.)
*   `cogarnach` (whispering) → *Bhí sí ag cogarnaigh go séimh.* (She whispered gently.)
*   `léimneach` (jumping) → *Tá na bradáin ag léimnigh.* (The salmon are jumping.)

Masculine verbal nouns in `-ach`/`-each`, such as `ceannach` (buying), do not have a special dative form.
*   *Tá mé ag ceannach glasraí.* (I am buying vegetables.)

### The Plural

A plural form is only used when the verbal noun functions as a regular noun. The plural formation often follows a pattern based on the ending.

*   **Verbal nouns in `-adh`**: The plural is formed by adding `-í` to the verbal adjective form.
    *   `glanadh` (a cleaning) → verbal adjective `glanta` → plural `glantaí` (cleanings)
    *   Sometimes other endings are used: `briseadh` (a break) → `bristeacha` (breaks), as in `bristeacha amach` (outbreaks).
*   **Verbal nouns in `-ú`**: The plural ends in `-uithe` or `-ithe`.
    *   `saothrú` (a cultivation) → `saothruithe` (cultivations). Note: this is different from the genitive singular/verbal adjective `saothraithe`.
*   **Verbal nouns in `-áil`**: The plural ends in `-álacha`.
    *   `gabháil` (a capture) → `gabhálacha` (captures).

## Short and Long Forms (`foirmeacha fada agus gairide`)

Some verbs have two verbal nouns: a shorter, more common form and a longer one. The longer forms (with endings like `-chán`, `-nach`, `-neach`, `-arnach`) often carry a more abstract, nominal, or continuous meaning. The shorter forms tend to be more concrete and verbal.

Syntactically, the shorter form is often used transitively (with an object), while the longer form is used intransitively.

| Short Form (Transitive) | Long Form (Intransitive) | Meaning |
| :---------------------- | :----------------------- | :------ |
| `léamh`                 | `léitheoireacht`         | reading |
| `ní`                    | `níochán`                | washing |
| `léim`                  | `léimneach`              | jumping |
| `preabadh`              | `preabarnach`            | hopping |
| `luascadh`              | `luascarnach`            | swinging |
| `lúbadh`                | `lúbarnach`              | twisting |

*   *Bhí sí ag fí an éadaigh.* (She was weaving the cloth.)
*   *Chaith sí an lá ag fíochán.* (She spent the day weaving.)
