## General Principles

Irish has almost no true modal verbs, which are verbs whose meaning is solely modal. The verb `féad` (can) is an exception, though its origins are not modal. Instead, Irish expresses modality through a variety of idiomatic phrases.

These phrases often involve the auxiliary verb `bí` (to be) or the copula `is`. The conditional or attenuated form of a modal expression (e.g., "would have to", "could") is typically formed by using the conditional of the auxiliary verb (`bheadh` instead of `tá`) or the copula (`ba` instead of `is`).

Many of these phrases use prepositions to introduce the logical subject of the sentence.

-   **Objective statements** often use the copula with the preposition `do`. This is common for expressions of necessity or obligation, indicating a factual or moral duty.
    -   *B’éigean dom* - I had to.
    -   *Ní foláir dom* - I must.
    -   *Is fearr dom* - I had better.
-   **Subjective assessments** often use the copula with the preposition `le`. This is common for expressions of wanting or liking.
    -   *Ní foláir liom* - I think I should.
    -   *Is maith liom* - I like.
    -   *Ba mhaith liom* - I would like.
-   **Impersonal meanings** arise when the preposition and logical subject are omitted.
    -   *B’éigean* - One would have to.
    -   *Is féidir* - It is possible.
    -   *B’fhéidir* - It would be possible / Perhaps.

Some other verbs take on a modal meaning in certain contexts, especially in the future or conditional tense.
-   *Caithfidh* - must (literally 'will consume')
-   *Glacfadh* - should (literally 'would accept')

### Complementation of Modal Phrases

Modal expressions are usually followed by an infinitive construction (a verbal noun phrase) or a `go`/`nach` clause.

-   **Deontic modality** refers to what someone can, may, must, or should do. This is the most common type of modality and is usually expressed with an infinitive construction.
    -   *Caithfidh sé an bád a phéinteáil uair sa bhliain.* - He must paint the boat once a year.
-   **Epistemic modality** expresses a subjective assessment of probability or possibility ("it must be that...", "it can be that..."). This is usually expressed with a `go`/`nach` clause.
    -   *Caithfidh go bpéinteálann sé an bád uair sa bhliain.* - It must be that he paints the boat once a year.
    -   Simple infinitive phrases with `bheith` (to be) can also have an epistemic meaning: *Caithfidh sí bheith breoite.* - She must be ill.

### Negation

Irish clearly distinguishes between negating the modality itself (external negation) and negating the action (internal negation).

-   **External negation** uses the particle `ní` to negate the modal expression.
    -   *Ní chaithfidh sé imeacht.* - He doesn't have to leave. (It is not a must.)
-   **Internal negation** uses `gan` with a verbal noun or `nach` in a clause.
    -   *Caithfidh sé gan imeacht.* - He must not leave. (It is a must that he not leave.)
-   **Double negation** (`ní ... gan ...`) is possible and usually has a reinforcing affirmative meaning.
    -   *Ní féidir liom gan é a dhéanamh.* - I cannot help but do it.

Some modal expressions are formally negative in their basic form, such as `ní mór dom` (I must). These cannot be negated again to express "I don't have to".

### Note on Terminology

In this guide, "Subject" and "Object" refer to the logical subject or object of the modal expression—the person or thing that "must", "can", "should", etc. Grammatically, the structure can be different. For example, in copular sentences, the logical subject is part of the predicate, and the following clause or infinitive construction is the grammatical subject of the sentence.

## Must

### `caithfidh` + Subject + VN/`go`

This uses the future tense of the verb `caith` (to consume, throw).
-   *Caithfidh mé imeacht.* - I must go.
-   *Caithfidh tú é a dhéanamh.* - You must do it.

**Usage Notes:**
-   After `má` (if), the habitual present `caitheann` is used instead of the future: *Má chaitheann sé imeacht.* - If he must go.
-   Outside of Munster, the conditional `chaithfinn` is common for both "I would have to" and "I had to".
-   In Munster, the habitual present, imperfect, and preterite forms are also used. However, `b’éigean do` is a more common way to express the past.

**Negation:**
-   External negation with `ní` is uncommon. `Ní chaithfidh sé imeacht` means "He does not have to leave".
-   Internal negation with `gan` means "must not": `Caithfidh sé gan imeacht` means "He must not leave".

### `bí` + `ar` + Subject + VN

-   *Tá orm imeacht.* - I must go.
-   *Bhéadh ar Sheán teacht.* - Seán would have to come.

### `tar` + `ar` + Subject + VN

-   *Tháinig orm imeacht.* - I had to go.
-   *Ní thiocfaidh ort pingin a íoc.* - You will not have to pay a penny.

### `is` + `éigean` + `do` + Subject + VN

This is especially common in the past tense as `b’éigean do`.
-   *B’éigean dom é a dhéanamh.* - I had to do it.

### `ní mór` + `do` + Subject + VN

Despite the negative particle `ní`, this expression is positive ("I must").
-   *Ní mór duit bheith anseo.* - You must be here.
-   *Ní mór dom dul ar scoil.* - I must go to school.
-   Questions can be formed with `cá` instead of `ní`: *Cá mór duit bheith anseo?* - Shouldn't you be here?

### `ní foláir` + `do`/`le` + Subject + VN

-   With `do`, it indicates an objective necessity: *Ní foláir dom é a rá.* - I must say it.
-   With `le`, it indicates a subjective assessment: *Ní foláir liom é a rá.* - I think I should say it.

### `bí` + Object + `le` + VN + `ag` + Subject

This construction expresses necessity with transitive verbal nouns.
-   *Tá litir le scríobh agam.* - I must write a letter. (Literally: A letter is to be written by me.)

### `bí` + `ag` + Subject + `le` + VN (Ulster)

This form can be used with intransitive verbs.
-   *Tá agam le dul.* - I must go.
-   In sentences with `ach` (only), `le` is omitted: *Níl agat ach an cnaipe a bhrú.* - You only have to press the button.

### `bí` + `sé` + `d’fhiacha` + `ar` + Subject + VN

This is a less common way to express obligation.
-   *Tá sé d’fhiacha orm teacht.* - I am obliged to come.
-   Other nouns like `iallach`, `oibleagáid`, or `dualgas` can be used instead of `fiacha`.

### Epistemic "Must"

To express an assumption ("it must be that..."):
-   **Past:** `b’éigean dó go...`
    -   *B’éigean dó gur thit a gcodladh orthu.* - They must have fallen asleep.
-   **Present:** `ní foláir nó (go)...`
    -   *Ní foláir nó tá sí tinn.* - She must be ill.
-   **Present/Future:** `caithfidh (sé) go...` (`sé` is often omitted in Munster)
    -   *Caithfidh go bhfuil sí ann.* - She must be there.
-   **Present:** `ní féidir nó (go)...`
    -   *Ní féidir nó tá tú faoi dhraíocht.* - You must be under a spell.
-   **With `gan`:** `ní féidir do` + Subject + `gan` + VN
    -   *Níorbh fhéidir dom gan bheith ag teacht ar Chúm na nÉag.* - I must have come close to Coomnaneage.

## Should

### `is ceart do` + Subject + VN/`go`

-   *Is ceart do Sheán teacht.* - Seán should come.
-   *Ba cheart do Sheán é a dhéanamh.* - Seán should do it.

### `is cóir do` + Subject + VN/`go`

-   *Ba chóir duit é a dhéanamh.* - You should do it.

### `ghlacfadh` + Subject + VN (Ulster)

This uses the conditional of `glac` (to accept).
-   *Ghlacfainn imeacht.* - I should better go.

### `d’fhéad` (+ `do`) + Subject + VN (Ulster)

This uses the preterite of `féad` (can) to mean "should have".
-   *D’fhéad tú labhairt leis.* - You should have spoken to him.

### `is gann do` + Subject + ...

This expresses "at least should".
-   *Is gann dó é.* - It's the least he should do.
-   Alternatives include `is ar a laghad do` and `is beag an rud do`.

### Other Ways to Express "Should"

-   **Imperative (3rd person):** *Tagadh Seán!* - Seán should come!
-   **Indirect Imperative:** *Dúirt tú liom é a dhéanamh.* - You told me I should do it.
-   **Future Tense (for offers/orders):** *An gcabhróidh mé leat?* - Shall I help you?
-   **Conditional Tense:** *Dhéanfadh sé cúis, dar liom.* - It should suffice, I think.
-   **Participle of Necessity (rare/archaic):** `is beite do...`
    -   *Is beite do dhuine Dia a ghuí.* - One should pray to God.

### Epistemic "Should"

To express an opinion about a likely event:
-   *Ba cheart dó bheith tagtha faoi seo.* - He should have arrived by now.
-   *Ba cheart go mbeadh an teach críochnaithe...* - The house should be finished...
-   It can also be expressed with verbs of thinking (`cheapfainn go...` - I would think that...) or phrases indicating rumour (`tá sé in ainm a bheith saibhir` - he is said to be rich).

## Had Better / Should Preferably

### `is fearr do` + Subject + VN

-   *Is fearr dom teacht.* - I had better come.
-   *B’fhearr do Sheán teacht.* - Seán had better come / should have come.

### `is córa do` + Subject + VN

-   *...sin mar is córa duit dul chun cainte leis.* - ...that's why you should all the more go and talk to him.

### `is cirte do` + Subject + VN

-   *...ba chirte dom a rá...* - ...or rather I should say...

## Can / Be able to

### `is féidir le` + Subject + VN

-   *Is féidir liom Gaeilge a scríobh.* - I can write Irish.
-   To make a more objective statement, `do` can be used instead of `le`: *Ní féidir do na páistí é a dhéanamh.* - It is not possible for the children to do it.

### `tig le` + Subject + VN

`Tig` is a variant of `tagann` (comes). This is common in Connacht (`tig le`) and Ulster (`thig le`).
-   *Tig le Pól é a dhéanamh.* - Paul can do it.
-   The preterite `tháinig le` describes success: *Rinne mé chomh maith é agus a tháinig liom.* - I did it as well as I could.

### `is acmhainn do` + Subject + VN

This often implies having the financial means.
-   *Is acmhainn dó teach a cheannach.* - He can afford to buy a house.

### `bí` + Subject + `in ann` / `ábalta` + VN

This construction refers to having the ability to do something.
-   *Tá mé in ann Gaeilge a scríobh.* - I can write Irish. (Mainly Connacht)
-   *Tá mé ábalta Gaeilge a scríobh.* - I can write Irish. (Mainly Ulster and Munster)

### `féad`

This is a true modal verb, used mainly in Munster, especially in the future and conditional.
-   *Nach bhféadfá fanacht?* - Couldn't you stay?
-   *Féadfaidh sé teacht lá ar bith.* - He can come any day.

### Other Ways to Express "Can"

-   **Success:** `téigh ag` + Subject + VN
    -   *Chuaigh aige éirí ina sheasamh.* - He succeeded in standing up.
-   **Inherent Ability:** `bí` + Skill + `ag` + Subject
    -   *Tá Gaeilge agam.* - I can speak Irish.
    -   *Tá snámh agam.* - I can swim.
-   **Managing to do something:** `faigh` + VN
    -   *Gheobhainn an obair a dhéanamh.* - I could do the work.
-   **Might as well:** `bí` + `sé` + `chomh maith` + `ag` + Subject + VN
    -   *Tá sé chomh maith agam fanacht.* - I might as well stay.

### Negation: "Cannot" vs. "Cannot help but"

-   **Cannot:** Negate the main verb or copula.
    -   *Ní féidir liom é a dhéanamh.* - I cannot do it.
-   **Cannot help but:** Use a double negation (`ní ... gan ...`).
    -   *Ní féidir liom gan é a dhéanamh.* - I cannot help but do it.
    -   *Níor fhéad mé gan gáire.* - I could not help but laugh.

### Epistemic "Can"

To express possibility ("it can be that..."):
-   `tig (+ dó) go...`
    -   *Thiocfadh dó go dtearnadh botún.* - It could be that a mistake was made.
-   `d’fhéadfadh (sé) go...`
-   `is féidir go...`
-   `b’fhéidir go...` (Perhaps...)
    -   *B’fhéidir go bhfuil sé ann.* - Perhaps it is there.

## May / Be allowed to

### `bí cead ag` + Subject + VN

-   *Tá cead ag an bhfear fuisce a ól.* - The man may drink whiskey.
-   *An bhfuil cead agam dul go dtí an leithreas?* - May I go to the toilet?

### `is ceadmhach do` + Subject + VN

`Ceadmhach` is the adjective "allowed".
-   *Ní ceadmhach duit bean do dhearthár a bheith agat.* - You may not have your brother's wife.

### `féadfaidh` + Subject + VN

The future of `féad` can also mean "may".
-   *Féadfaidh tú imeacht anois.* - You may go now.

### `is miste le/do`

Used in polite questions for permission ("Would you mind if...?").
-   *Ar mhiste leat mé a dhul ansin?* - May I go there?
-   *Ní miste.* - I don't mind. (An affirmative answer)

### Not Allowed

This is expressed by negating `cead` or using an internal negation with a "must" construction.
-   *Níl cead agat imeacht.* - You are not allowed to go.
-   *Caithfidh tú gan imeacht.* - You must not go.

## To like

### `is maith le` + Subject + Object/VN

-   *Is maith liom beoir.* - I like beer.
-   *Ba mhaith liom fuisce a ól.* - I would like to drink whiskey.
-   For emphasis, `breá` (fine, lovely) can be used instead of `maith`: *Ba bhreá liom...*

### `taitin` + Object + `le` + Subject

-   *Taitníonn iasc liom.* - I like fish. (Literally: Fish pleases me.)
-   *Thaitin an capall leis.* - He liked the horse.

### `bí dúil ag` + Subject + `i` + Object

-   *Tá dúil agam sa tsiúl.* - I like hiking.

### Wishes (Optative)

Wishes are expressed with the present subjunctive.
-   *Go bhfóire Dia orainn!* - May God help us!

## To prefer / Would rather

### `is fearr le` + Subject + Object/VN

-   *Is fearr liom tae ná caife.* - I prefer tea to coffee.
-   *B’fhearr liom titim sa chomhrac.* - I would rather fall in battle.

### `is measa le` + Subject + Object/VN

Although `measa` means "worse", this can express a preference by focusing on the greater loss.
-   *Is measa liom Seán a chailleadh ná Séamas.* - I would rather lose Séamas than Seán. (Losing Seán is worse to me.)

### `is rogha le` + Subject + Object/VN

`Rogha` means "choice".
-   *Is rogha liom imeacht anois.* - I would rather leave now.

## To want

### `bí` + Object + `ó` + Subject

-   *Tá braon fuisce uaim.* - I want some whiskey.
-   *Tá uaim fuisce a ól.* - I want to drink whiskey.

### `teastaigh` + Object + `ó` + Subject

This can mean "to want" or "to need".
-   *Teastaíonn arán uaidh.* - He wants/needs bread.

### `is mian le` + Subject + Object/VN

-   *Is mian liom é a dhéanamh.* - I want/wish to do it.

### `bí` + Subject + `ag iarraidh` + Object/VN

The verb `iarr` (to ask, to try) is often used to mean "to want".
-   *Níl mé ag iarraidh éirí go fóill.* - I don't want to get up yet.
-   *An bhfuil tú ag iarraidh seacláide?* - Do you want chocolate?

### `bí fonn ar` + Subject + VN

This means "to feel like" doing something.
-   *Tá fonn orm é a dhéanamh.* - I feel like doing it.
-   *Bhí fonn codlata orm.* - I wanted to sleep.

## To need

### `bí gá ag` + Subject + `le` + Object

-   *Tá gá aige leis an bpeann.* - He needs the pen.

### `teastaigh` + Object + `ó` + Subject

As noted above, this can mean "to want" or "to need".
-   *Tá sé ag teastáil go mór uaim.* - I urgently need it.

### `bí` + Object + `de dhíth ar` + Subject

-   *Tá braon fuisce de dhíth orm.* - I need some whiskey.

### `ní gá do` + Subject + VN

This is the most common way to say "don't have to" or "don't need to".
-   *Ní gá duit é a dhéanamh.* - You don't need to do it.
-   With `ach` (only): *Ní gá duit ach clárú.* - You only need to register.

## To have

Irish does not have a single verb for "to have". Instead, it uses `bí` with prepositions.

### General Sense (Alienable Possession)

Use `bí` + Object + `ag` + Subject for things that can be transferred.
-   *Tá teach agam.* - I have a house.
-   *Níl carr againn.* - We don't have a car.

This form is also used for skills:
-   *Tá Gaeilge agam.* - I can speak Irish.
-   *Tá a fhios agam.* - I know.

### States and Inalienable Possession

Use `bí` + Object + `ar` + Subject for physical/psychological states, illnesses, and inseparable parts.
-   *Níl ocras orm.* - I am not hungry.
-   *Tá slaghdán ar an gcailín.* - The girl has a cold.
-   *Tá féasóg ar an seanfhear.* - The old man has a beard.
-   *Cén t-ainm atá ort?* - What is your name?

### Ownership

Use the copula `is` + `le` + Subject + Object to state ownership.
-   *Is liom an carr seo.* - This car is mine.
-   *Is le Pól an teach.* - The house belongs to Paul.

## `Bí` as an Auxiliary Verb

The verb `bí` is the most common auxiliary verb in Irish. It is used for:
-   **Describing existence:** *Tá sé ann.* - He exists / He is there.
-   **Describing states:** *Tá sé i nGaillimh.* - He is in Galway. *Tá sé tinn.* - He is ill.
-   **Forming aspects:**
    -   Progressive: *Tá sé ag ól.* - He is drinking.
    -   Perfect: *Tá sé tar éis ól.* - He has just drunk.
    -   Intentional: *Tá sé ar tí ól.* - He is about to drink.
-   **Forming passive-like constructions:** *Tá sé ólta aige.* - He has drunk it.
-   **Classification:** *Tá sé ina mhúinteoir.* - He is a teacher.
-   **"Have" and modal constructions:** As detailed above.

## `Déan` as an Auxiliary Verb

The verb `déan` (to do, to make) can act as an auxiliary verb with a verbal noun.

### Syntax

If the object of a transitive verbal noun is separated from it, the preposition `ar` is placed before the object.
-   Compare: *An teach a athrú a rinne sé.* (He changed the house.)
-   With `ar`: *Rinne sé athrú ar an teach.* (He changed the house.)

### Usage

`Déan` is used as an auxiliary to:
-   **Add emphasis or persistence to a command:** *Déan é a cheannach!* - Do buy it!
-   **Describe deliberate action or intent:** *Déanfaidh siad cuid acu a mharú.* - They will indeed kill some of them.
-   **Make a promise:** *Déanfaidh mé é a chloisint!* - I will listen to it, I promise!
-   **In "only" sentences:** *Ach ní dhearna sé ach gáire.* - But he only laughed.
-   **To emphasise the action in a cleft sentence:** *An fear a fheiceáil a rinne tú.* - You saw the man.
-   **As a substitute verb in short answers (Munster):** *Ar ólais an tae? - Dheineas.* - Did you drink the tea? - Yes.
-   **To form a passive progressive:** *Tá uasghrádú á dhéanamh ar an suíomh seo.* - This site is being updated.

### Advanced Note: `déan` and Unaccusative Verbs

The verb `déan` has an important intransitive use meaning "to become", which is a variant of the transitive `déan ... de` (to make something out of...).
-   *Rinne bean di.* - She became a woman.
-   *Rinne fíon den uisce.* - The water became wine.

This construction is unaccusative. The grammatical subject (`bean`, `fíon`) is not the agent of the action but is affected by it. There is no agent. This differs from the autonomous form, which implies an indefinite agent ("one").
-   **Transitive:** *Rinne sé fíon den uisce.* - He made wine from the water.
-   **Autonomous (Transitive):** *Rinneadh fíon den uisce.* - Wine was made from the water (by someone).
-   **Unaccusative:** *Rinne fíon den uisce.* - The water became wine (no agent).
