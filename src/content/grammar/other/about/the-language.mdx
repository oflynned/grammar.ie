Irish often appears to visitors as the seemingly indecipherable words on signs and maps. Below or next to "Dublin", for example, you will see *Baile Átha Cliath*. The former "Kingston" is now known as *Dún Laoghaire*, and "Limerick" is called *Luimneach*. The country itself is *Éire*, or in its long form, *Poblacht na hÉireann*.

Even in Dublin, finding the city centre is easier if you know it is called *an Lár*. On public toilet doors, a simple misunderstanding can occur: "M" stands for `mná` (women), while "F" stands for `fir` (men). These examples make it clear that Irish is not a dialect of English, but a distinct language with its own rich history.

## Genetic Relationships

The Celtic languages are divided into two main groups: Insular Celtic and Continental Celtic. The Continental Celtic languages, such as Gaulish, died out during the Roman era.

Insular Celtic languages survive to this day and possess many unusual features not found in other Indo-European languages, such as a distinct word order, conjugated prepositions, and initial mutations. These features are thought to be the influence of a pre-Indo-European substrate language spoken on the islands.

Insular Celtic is further divided into two branches:

*   **Goidelic (or Gaelic)**: These are "q-Celtic" languages, where the Proto-Indo-European sound *qw* was retained or became a *k* sound. This branch includes Irish, Scottish Gaelic, and Manx.
*   **Brythonic (or Brittonic)**: These are "p-Celtic" languages, where the *qw* sound developed into *p*. This branch includes Welsh, Breton, and Cornish.

This fundamental difference can be seen in core vocabulary. The Irish word `mac` ("son") corresponds to the Welsh `mab`. Similarly, "five" is `cúig` in Irish but `pump` in Welsh. Because of this, early Irish had no native `p` sound; all modern Irish words containing a `p` are loanwords.

## Languages in Ireland

While Irish is the first official language of Ireland, the majority of the population speaks English in daily life. Irish English (Hiberno-English) is a distinct dialect influenced by Irish grammar and vocabulary.

Other languages spoken in Ireland include:

*   **Shelta (Cant)**: The language of the Irish Travellers, with a vocabulary largely based on Irish.
*   **Ullans (Ulster Scots)**: A variant of Scots spoken in parts of Ulster, a legacy of Scottish settlement in the 17th century.

Scottish Gaelic is not spoken natively in Ireland today, though some eastern dialects of Ulster Irish were historically very similar to it.

## The Name of the Language

In Irish, the language is called `Gaeilge`. This is the modern standard form and the term used in the Connacht dialect. Before the 20th-century spelling reform, it was written as `Gaedhealg`.

Dialectal names for the language include:
*   `Gaeilg` or `Gaeilic` in Ulster
*   `Gaelainn` or `Gaolainn` in Munster

In English, the recommended terms are **Irish** or **Irish Gaelic**. Using "Gaelic" alone can be ambiguous, as it could also refer to Scottish Gaelic or Manx.

## History of Irish

The history of the Irish language is typically divided into several periods:

*   **Archaic Irish (c. 500–700 AD)**: The earliest form of the language, known primarily from names inscribed on Ogham stones.
*   **Old Irish (c. 700–900 AD)**: With Christianisation came the Latin alphabet and a flourishing of literature. Old Irish was one of the first European vernaculars, alongside Latin and Greek, to develop a significant literary tradition. Many Latin words entered the language during this time, such as `beannacht` ("blessing") from *benedictio*.
*   **Middle Irish (c. 900–1200 AD)**: The Viking invasions led to a simplification of Irish grammar and the adoption of some Scandinavian words, such as `fuinneog` ("window") from *vindauga* ("wind-eye").
*   **Early Modern or Classical Irish (c. 1200–1600 AD)**: Following the Norman invasion, Gaelic culture experienced a renewal. The immigrating Normans assimilated, adopting the Irish language. This period saw the adoption of many Norman French words, like `garsún` ("boy") from *garçon*. Classical Irish served as a unified literary standard across Ireland and Scotland.
*   **Modern Irish (c. 1600–present)**: The collapse of the Gaelic order in the 17th century led to the decline of the classical standard. Dialectal forms became more prominent, and the language retreated to rural areas. Under English rule, Irish was suppressed in public life. The Great Famine (1845–48) and subsequent emigration disproportionately affected the Irish-speaking rural poor, accelerating the language's decline.

In the late 19th century, a revival movement began, led by organisations like the Gaelic League (`Conradh na Gaeilge`). This led to the creation of a new literature and eventually to the establishment of an official standard grammar and spelling, which was formalised after the founding of the Irish Free State.

## Modern Irish

### Dialects

There are three main dialects of Irish spoken today, corresponding to the provinces where the language has survived most strongly:

1.  **Munster Irish** (`Gaelainn na Mumhan`)
2.  **Connacht Irish** (`Gaeilge Chonnacht`)
3.  **Ulster Irish** (`Gaeilge Uladh`)

The differences between them are generally not as great as those between, for example, regional dialects of German. Ulster Irish shares some features with Scottish Gaelic. There is no longer a distinct Leinster dialect, though a standardised, mixed form known as the `Lárchanúint` ("Central Dialect") is often taught in schools outside the Gaeltacht.

To provide a common reference for official use, an **Official Standard** (`an Caighdeán Oifigiúil`) was developed, which draws on features from all three major dialects.

### The Gaeltachtaí

The `Gaeltachtaí` are the regions, primarily along the western seaboard, where Irish is still the primary language of the community.

*   **Connacht (`Cúige Connacht`)**
    *   **County Galway (`Contae na Gaillimhe`)**: The largest Gaeltacht, including Cois Fharraige, Ceantar na nOileáin (the island district), Dúiche Sheoigheach (Joyce Country), and the Aran Islands (`Oileáin Árann`).
    *   **County Mayo (`Contae Mhaigh Eo`)**: Areas include Tuar Mhic Éadaigh, parts of Achill Island (`Oileán Acla`), and the Erris peninsula (`Iorras`).

*   **Ulster (`Cúige Uladh`)**
    *   **County Donegal (`Contae Dhún na nGall`)**: The second-largest Gaeltacht, with major Irish-speaking areas in the south (e.g., Gleann Cholm Cille), centre (e.g., Na Rosa, Gaoth Dobhair), and north (e.g., Ros Goill, Fánaid).

*   **Munster (`Cúige Mumhan`)**
    *   **County Kerry (`Contae Chiarraí`)**: The Dingle Peninsula (`Corca Dhuibhne`) and the Iveragh Peninsula (`Uíbh Ráthach`).
    *   **County Cork (`Contae Chorcaí`)**: Múscraí (Muskerry) and Cape Clear Island (`Oileán Chléire`).
    *   **County Waterford (`Contae Phort Láirge`)**: An Rinn (Ring), south of Dungarvan.

*   **Leinster (`Cúige Laighean`)**
    *   **County Meath (`Contae na Mí`)**: The areas of Ráth Cairn and Baile Ghib were established in the 1930s by speakers who relocated from Connacht.

## Orthography and Pronunciation

Irish spelling can seem intimidating, but it follows a consistent internal logic.

### Broad and Slender Consonants

Every consonant in Irish (except `h`) has two distinct pronunciations: **broad** and **slender**.
*   A consonant is **broad** when it is next to the vowels `a`, `o`, or `u`.
*   A consonant is **slender** when it is next to the vowels `e` or `i`.

This is governed by the spelling rule *caol le caol agus leathan le leathan* ("slender with slender and broad with broad"), which means that the vowels on either side of a consonant or consonant cluster must both be from the same group (broad or slender).

For example, to pronounce `beo` ("alive"), you shape your lips to say the slender `b` in `bí` ("be!"), but then pronounce the vowel sound `o`. This creates a slight 'y' glide. To pronounce `buí` ("yellow"), you shape your lips for the broad `b` in `bo` ("cow"), but then say the vowel sound `í`. This creates a slight 'w' glide. The `e` in `beo` and the `u` in `buí` are not fully pronounced; their main job is to indicate that the preceding consonant is slender or broad, respectively.

### Lenition (`Séimhiú`)

Lenition is a sound change, marked in writing by adding an `h` after a consonant. It typically softens the sound of the consonant.

*   `bh` is pronounced like 'v' or 'w'.
*   `ch` is a guttural sound, like the 'ch' in Scottish *loch*.
*   `dh`, `gh` are pronounced like a 'y' sound (slender) or a soft 'g' (broad).
*   `fh` is silent.
*   `mh` is pronounced like 'v' or 'w'.
*   `ph` is pronounced 'f'.
*   `sh`, `th` are pronounced 'h'.

In older texts, lenition was marked with a dot over the consonant (`ṗ`, `ḃ`, etc.), called a *ponc séimhithe*.

### Eclipsis (`Urú`)

Eclipsis is another sound change where a consonant is prefixed to the beginning of a word, "eclipsing" the original sound. Only the new, prefixed consonant is pronounced.

*   `g` eclipses `c` -> `gc` (pronounced 'g')
*   `b` eclipses `p` -> `bp` (pronounced 'b')
*   `n` eclipses `d` -> `nd` (pronounced 'n')
*   `d` eclipses `t` -> `dt` (pronounced 'd')
*   `bh` eclipses `f` -> `bhf` (pronounced 'v')
*   `n` eclipses `g` -> `ng` (pronounced 'ng' as in "sing")

The eclipsed letter is kept in writing to make the original word easier to recognise. For example, *ár bhfear* ("our man") is clearly derived from `fear` ("man").

### The Long Mark (`Síneadh Fada`)

The acute accent (`´`) over a vowel is called a `síneadh fada` or simply `fada`. It does not indicate stress but marks the vowel as long. This is a crucial distinction.

*   `fear` [far] means "man".
*   `féar` [fayr] means "grass".
*   `beár` [bawr] means "bar".
*   `béar` [bayr] means "bear".
