Irish, known as `Gaeilge` in the language itself, is the first official language of Ireland and a member of the Celtic language family. While English is spoken by most people in daily life, Irish has a rich history and is visible everywhere on signs, in place names, and in the `Gaeltacht` (Irish-speaking) regions.

To a visitor, Irish can seem indecipherable at first. On a sign for Dublin, you will see `Baile Átha Cliath`. The town of Dún Laoghaire was formerly known in English as Kingstown. Limerick is `Luimneach`, and the country itself is `Éire`. Even finding the city centre is easier if you know the Irish term, `an Lár`. On public toilet doors, an "M" stands for `mná` (women), while an "F" is for `fir` (men). These examples make it clear that Irish is not a dialect of English, but a distinct and ancient language.

## Relationship to Other Languages

The Celtic languages are divided into two main groups: Insular Celtic and Continental Celtic. The Continental branch, which included the language of the Gauls, died out during Roman times.

Insular Celtic survives to this day and is itself split into two groups: Goidelic (or Gaelic) and Brythonic. These languages have many unusual features not found in other Indo-European languages, such as initial mutations and conjugated prepositions.

The main difference between the two groups is how they treated the ancient Indo-European sound `*qw`.
*   In **Brythonic** languages, `*qw` became `p`. These are known as **p-Celtic** languages. This group includes Welsh (`Cymraeg`), Breton (`Brezhoneg`), and Cornish (`Kernewek`).
*   In **Goidelic** languages, `*qw` was kept as a `q`-sound, which later became `k` (written `c`). These are known as **q-Celtic** languages. This group includes Irish (`Gaeilge`), Scottish Gaelic (`Gàidhlig`), and Manx (`Gaelg`).

This fundamental split can be seen in core vocabulary. The Irish word for "son", `mac`, corresponds to the Welsh `mab`. Because of this, Irish had no native `p` sound until the Middle Ages; all modern Irish words containing a `p` are loanwords.

<Details summary="Historical note on p/q-Celtic">
  The distinction is clear in the word for "five": Irish `cúig` versus Welsh `pump`. The ancient Irish were aware of this difference and would sometimes substitute `c` for `p` in loanwords from Brythonic or Latin. For example, the Latin word *planta* (plant) came into Irish as `clann` (children) via the Welsh word `plant`. Similarly, the Latin *pascha* (from Hebrew) became Irish `cáisc` (Easter).
</Details>

## Languages Spoken in Ireland

While Irish is the first national language, most people in Ireland speak English in their daily lives. This form of English, known as **Hiberno-English**, is influenced by the Irish language in its accent, vocabulary, and grammar.

Besides these two main languages, you may also encounter:
*   **Shelta**: The language of the Irish Travellers, with a vocabulary largely based on Irish.
*   **Ullans (Ulster Scots)**: A variant of Scots spoken in Ulster, a legacy of Scottish settlers from the 17th century.

Scottish Gaelic is not natively spoken in Ireland today, though some eastern dialects of Ulster Irish were historically very close to it.

## The Gaelic Languages

### The Name of the Language

In Irish, the language is called `Gaeilge`. This is the modern standard form and the name used in the Connacht dialect. Historically, it was known as `Gaedhealg`. Dialectal names include `Gaeilg` or `Gaeilic` in Ulster and `Gaelainn` or `Gaolainn` in Munster.

In English, the recommended terms are **Irish** or **Irish Gaelic**. Using "Gaelic" alone can be ambiguous, as it could also refer to Scottish Gaelic or Manx.

### History of the Language

The Celts arrived in Ireland around 500 BCE. The earliest form of the language we have records of is **Archaic Irish** (`an Ghaeilge Ársa`), found in Ogham stone inscriptions from around 500–700 CE.

With Christianisation came the Latin alphabet, and from 700–900 CE, **Old Irish** (`an tSean-Ghaeilge`) flourished. This was a golden age for Irish culture, and a significant body of literature emerged, making Irish one of the first European languages besides Latin and Greek to do so. This period saw many Latin loanwords enter the language, such as `beannacht` (blessing) from *benedictio*.

The Viking invasions marked the beginning of the **Middle Irish** (`an Mheán-Ghaeilge`) period (c. 900–1200). The language began to simplify, and some Norse words were adopted, such as `fuinneog` (window) from *vindauga* ("wind-eye").

After the Norman invasion, a period of Gaelic cultural revival led to the era of **Classical Irish** (`an Ghaeilge Chlasaiceach`) from 1200–1600. This was a unified literary standard used across both Ireland and Scotland. Many Norman French words were borrowed, like `garsún` (boy) from *garçon*.

**Modern Irish** (`an Nua-Ghaeilge`) has been spoken since 1600. The collapse of the Gaelic order in the 17th century led to the decline of the classical standard, and spoken dialects became more prominent. Under English rule, Irish was suppressed in public life. The Great Famine (1845–48) and subsequent emigration devastated the Irish-speaking rural population. Parents began to follow the saying, "Keep Irish from the children!", believing English was necessary for survival and advancement.

A revival of interest in the language began in the late 19th century with the founding of the Gaelic League (`Conradh na Gaeilge`). This movement focused on the living dialects rather than reviving Classical Irish, eventually leading to the modern standard language and a spelling reform in 1945.

## The Dialects of Irish

There are three main dialects (`canúintí`) of Irish spoken today, corresponding to the provinces where the language has survived most strongly:
*   **Munster** (`Gaelainn na Mumhan`)
*   **Connacht** (`Gaeilge Connacht`)
*   **Ulster** (`Gaeilge Uladh`)

The differences are significant but generally less pronounced than those between, for example, regional dialects of German. Ulster Irish shares some features with Scottish Gaelic.

To provide a common standard for official use, an official standard grammar, **`an Caighdeán Oifigiúil`**, was created. The language taught in most schools outside of Irish-speaking areas is based on this standard, sometimes called the **`Lárchanúint`** ("Central Dialect").

<Details summary="Historical North/South Divide">
  Originally, there were two major dialect groups: a Northern Irish (Ulster, Connacht, Meath) and a Southern Irish (Munster, Leinster). This corresponded to the ancient division of the island into `Leath Chuinn` ("Conn's Half") and `Leath Mhogha` ("Mogh's Half"). This explains why Ulster and Connacht Irish still share many features not found in the Munster dialect.
</Details>

## The Gaeltachtaí

The `Gaeltachtaí` are the regions where Irish is still the daily language of a significant part of the community.

### Connacht (Cúige Connacht)

*   **County Galway (`Contae na Gaillimhe`)**: The largest Gaeltacht area, covering much of Connemara (including `An Spidéal`, `An Cheathrú Rua`, `Carna`), the Aran Islands (`Oileáin Árann`), and smaller areas east of Galway city.
*   **County Mayo (`Contae Mhaigh Eo`)**: Areas include Tourmakeady (`Tuar Mhic Éadaigh`), Achill Island (`Oileán Acla`), and the Erris peninsula (`Iorras`).

### Ulster (Cúige Uladh)

*   **County Donegal (`Contae Dhún na nGall`)**: The second largest Gaeltacht, with major Irish-speaking areas in the south-west (`Gleann Cholm Cille`), central region (The Rosses, `na Rosa`; Gweedore, `Gaoth Dobhair`), and northern peninsulas.

### Munster (Cúige Mumhan)

*   **County Kerry (`Contae Chiarraí`)**: Centred on the Dingle Peninsula (`Corca Dhuibhne`) and the Iveragh Peninsula (`Uíbh Ráthach`).
*   **County Cork (`Contae Chorcaí`)**: The Muskerry (`Múscraí`) region, including villages like `Baile Bhuirne`, and Cape Clear Island (`Oileán Chléire`).
*   **County Waterford (`Contae Phort Láirge`)**: The small but strong Gaeltacht of Ring (`An Rinn`) near Dungarvan.

### Leinster (Cúige Laighean)

*   **County Meath (`Contae na Mí`)**: The areas of Rathcarran (`Ráth Cairn`) and Gibstown (`Baile Ghib`) were established in the 1930s when families from the Connemara Gaeltacht were resettled there.

## Pronunciation and Spelling

Irish spelling can seem intimidating, but it follows a consistent internal logic. The key is to understand a few core principles.

### Broad and Slender Consonants

Every consonant in Irish has two distinct pronunciations: **broad** and **slender**.
*   A **broad** consonant has a sound similar to its English equivalent, but with the back of the tongue raised, as if preparing to say an "o" or "u" sound.
*   A **slender** consonant has a lighter, sharper sound, with the middle of the tongue raised, as if preparing to say an "e" or "i" sound.

The spelling tells you which version to use. A consonant is broad if it is next to a broad vowel (`a`, `o`, `u`). It is slender if it is next to a slender vowel (`e`, `i`).

For example, compare the words `bo` (cow) and `bí` (be!). The `b` in `bo` is broad, while the `b` in `bí` is slender. This distinction allows for minimal pairs that would otherwise sound the same:
*   `beo` (alive): The `e` makes the `b` slender.
*   `buí` (yellow): The `u` makes the `b` broad.

<Tip>
The fundamental rule of Irish spelling is **`leathan le leathan agus caol le caol`** ("broad with broad and slender with slender"). This means that on either side of a consonant or consonant group, the vowels must both be from the same class (either both broad or both slender). This is why a word like `ichea` is possible, but `icha` is not.
</Tip>

### Lenition (Séimhiú)

Lenition is a sound change, marked by adding an `h` after a consonant, that softens its pronunciation. In older texts, it was marked with a dot (`ponc séimhithe`) over the consonant.

*   `ch`, `ph`: Become fricatives, like `ch` in "loch" or `f` in "fun".
*   `bh`, `mh`: Sound like `v` or `w`.
*   `dh`, `gh`: Sound like the `g` in Spanish "fuego" or like an English `y`.
*   `sh`, `th`: Sound like an English `h`.
*   `fh`: Becomes completely silent.

### Eclipsis (Urú)

Eclipsis is another sound change where one consonant is "eclipsed" by another, which is written before it. Only the new, eclipsing consonant is pronounced.

*   `g` eclipses `c` -> `gc` (pronounced `g`)
*   `b` eclipses `p` -> `bp` (pronounced `b`)
*   `n` eclipses `g` -> `ng` (pronounced `ng`)
*   `d` eclipses `t` -> `dt` (pronounced `d`)
*   `n` eclipses `d` -> `nd` (pronounced `n`)
*   `bh` eclipses `f` -> `bhf` (pronounced like `v` or `w`)

The original letter is kept in the spelling to make the root word easier to recognise. For example, `bhfear` (eclipsed form of `fear`, man) is clearly identifiable, whereas if it were written `bhear`, it could be confused with the lenited form of a different word.

Lenition and eclipsis are not random; they are grammatical changes that occur in predictable situations, such as after certain prepositions, numbers, or possessive adjectives.

### Long Vowels (Síneadh Fada)

An accent mark, called a `síneadh fada` or just `fada`, indicates that a vowel is pronounced long. It does not mark stress. This is a important distinction that changes the meaning of words:
*   *fear* (man) vs. *féar* (grass)
*   *bear* (he might carry) vs. *béar* (a bear) vs. *beár* (a bar)

While Irish orthography has its complexities, it is far more regular than English or French. With an understanding of these core principles, the relationship between the written word and its pronunciation becomes much clearer.
