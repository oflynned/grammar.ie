A relative clause is a subordinate clause that refers back to a word in the main clause, known as the antecedent (`an réamhtheachtaí`). In English, these are often introduced by pronouns like "who", "which", or "that".

Irish makes a fundamental distinction between two types of relative clause: the **direct relative clause** and the **indirect relative clause**. This distinction is crucial and affects the choice of particle, the initial mutation, and the overall sentence structure.

- A **direct relative clause** is used when the antecedent is the subject or direct object of the relative clause.
- An **indirect relative clause** is used in most other cases, such as when the antecedent is the object of a preposition ("the house *in which* I live"), has a possessive relationship ("the man *whose* son is sick"), or relates to time, place, or reason ("the day *when* I arrived").

Relative clauses are used more frequently in Irish than in English, particularly in constructions like cleft sentences for emphasis.

## The Direct Relative Clause (`An Clásal Coibhneasta Díreach`)

The direct relative clause is used when its antecedent is the subject or direct object of the clause's verb.

- *An fear **a rinne sin***. (The man **who did that**.) — `an fear` is the subject of `rinne`.
- *An rud **a rinne sé***. (The thing **that he did**.) — `an rud` is the object of `rinne`.

### Particles and Verb Forms

The direct relative clause is introduced by a relative particle that causes an initial mutation. The verb form also changes.

| Context | Particle | Mutation | Verb Form |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Positive (Present/Future) | `a` | Lenition | Independent |
| Positive (Past) | `a` | Lenition | Independent |
| Negative (Present/Future) | `nach` | Eclipsis | Dependent |
| Negative (Past) | `nár` | Lenition | Dependent |

In Munster, `ná` is often used instead of `nach`.

**Special Verb Forms:**

- **Relative Forms:** In Connacht and Ulster, the present and future tenses often use a special relative form ending in `-s`, e.g., `a bhíos` (instead of `a bhíonn`), `a bheas` (instead of `a bheidh`).
- **`tá`:** The verb `tá` merges with the particle `a` to become `atá`.
- **Copula:** The copula has its own relative forms: `is` and `ba`/`ab` (positive), and `nach`/`nár`/`nárbh` (negative).

### Common Uses of the Direct Relative Clause

1.  **Attributive Clauses:** The most common use, where the clause describes a noun that is its subject or object.
    - *An fear **a tháinig**.* (The man **who came**.)
    - *An leabhar **a cheannaigh mé**.* (The book **that I bought**.)

2.  **Cleft Sentences:** Used to emphasise a part of the sentence by moving it to the front. The rest of the sentence becomes a direct relative clause.
    - *Is ag léamh **a bhí mé**.* (I was reading. Lit: "It is at reading **that I was**.")

3.  **After Interrogative Words:** Questions starting with `cé` (who), `cad`/`céard` (what), or `conas` (how) are structured as cleft sentences.
    - *Cé **a bhí ann**?* (Who was there?)
    - Note: If the interrogative word is combined with a preposition (e.g., `cé air`, `cad chuige`), an indirect relative clause is used instead.

4.  **After Conjunctions:** Certain conjunctions are followed by a direct relative clause.
    - `nuair`: *nuair **a bhí mé anseo*** (when I was here)
    - `mar`: *mar **a bhí sí*** (as she was)
    - `ó`: *ó **rachas sé*** (since he will go) - the particle `a` is absorbed.

5.  **In Comparisons:**
    - With equatives: *chomh mór **agus atá tú*** (as big as you are)
    - With comparatives: *níos fearr **ná a d’imir siad*** (better than they played)

6.  **With Verbs of Saying:** Used to frame direct speech.
    - *"Déan sin!" **a dúirt sé**.* ("Do that!" he said.)

7.  **Referring to a Whole Clause:** When a relative clause comments on a preceding statement, it uses `rud` or `ní` as its antecedent.
    - *Dúirt sé liom an leabhar a cheannach, **rud a rinne mé**.* (He told me to buy the book, **which I did**.)

## The Indirect Relative Clause (`An Clásal Coibhneasta Indíreach`)

The indirect relative clause is used when the antecedent is not the direct subject or object. Its relationship to the clause is shown by a **reflexive pronoun** (`forainm iartheachtach`) inside the clause. This pronoun can be a prepositional pronoun, a possessive pronoun, or a simple personal pronoun.

### Particles and Verb Forms

The indirect relative clause uses a different set of particles and always takes the dependent form of the verb.

| Context | Particle | Mutation | Verb Form |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Positive (Present/Future) | `a` | Eclipsis | Dependent |
| Positive (Past) | `ar` | Lenition | Dependent |
| Negative (Present/Future) | `nach` | Eclipsis | Dependent |
| Negative (Past) | `nár` | Lenition | Dependent |

In Munster, `go` (present/future) and `gur` (past) are typically used instead of `a` and `ar`. The negative particles are the same as for the direct relative clause.

### Types of Indirect Relative Clauses

#### Prepositional Relative Clauses

This form corresponds to English phrases like "on which", "to whom", or "with which". The relationship is shown by a prepositional pronoun inside the clause that refers back to the antecedent.

There are three main ways to structure this, with the first being the most common in modern Irish:

1.  **Particle... Prepositional Pronoun at the End:**
    - *An bord **a bhfuil** an forc **air**.* (The table on which the fork is.)
    - *An fear **ar labhair** mé **leis**.* (The man to whom I spoke.)

2.  **Preposition + Particle...:** This older form combines the preposition with the relative particle `a` or `ar`. It is less common now but still seen with prepositions like `ar`, `i`, and `le`.
    - `ar` + `a` -> `ar a`: *an bord **ar a bhfuil** an forc* (the table on which the fork is)
    - `le` + `a` -> `lena`: *an peann **lena scríobhann** sé* (the pen with which he writes)
    - `i` + `ar` -> `inar`: *an teach **inar rugadh** é* (the house in which he was born)

3.  **Prepositional Pronoun before the Particle:** This structure is now mainly used in questions.
    - *Cad **leis a** ndearna tú é?* (With what did you make it?)
    - *Cé **dó ar** thug tú an leabhar?* (To whom did you give the book?)

#### Genitive Relative Clauses

This form corresponds to the English "whose". The reflexive element is a possessive pronoun (`a`, `ár`, `bhur`) inside the clause.

- *An fear **a bhfuil a mhac** tinn.* (The man whose son is sick.)
- *An bhean **ar goideadh a carr**.* (The woman whose car was stolen.)
- *An cailín **a raibh mé ina cuideachta**.* (The girl in whose company I was.)

Questions asking "Whose...?" use this structure with `cé` as the antecedent.
- *Cé **a bhfuil a leabhar** anseo?* (Whose book is here?)

#### Adverbial Relative Clauses

This type is used when the antecedent refers to a time, place, reason, or manner. It corresponds to English "when", "where", "why", or "how". No reflexive pronoun is needed. This is often called the **DÁFTA rule**, from the initial letters of common antecedents:

- `dóigh` (manner)
- `áit` (place)
- `fáth` (reason)
- `treo` (direction)
- `am` (time)

Examples:
- *An áit **a bhfuil** mé.* (The place where I am.)
- *An t-am **ar tháinig** sé.* (The time when he came.)
- *An fáth **a ndúirt** sé é.* (The reason why he said it.)

This structure is also used in questions with antecedents like `cén fáth` (why) or `cén chaoi` (how).
- *Cén fáth **ar fhág** tú?* (Why did you leave?)

**Exception:** With some temporal nouns like `uair` (time, occasion) and `achar` (duration), a **direct** relative clause is used.
- *An chéad uair **a chonaic** mé é.* (The first time that I saw him.)

#### Object Relative Clauses (for Ambiguity)

A direct relative clause can sometimes be ambiguous. For example, *an fear a bhuaileann mé* could mean "the man who hits me" (antecedent = subject) or "the man whom I hit" (antecedent = object).

To remove this ambiguity, an indirect relative clause with a reflexive personal pronoun (`é`, `í`, `iad`) can be used to clarify that the antecedent is the object.

- *An fear **a mbuailim é**.* (The man whom I hit.)
- *Na daoine **ar chonaic mé iad**.* (The people whom I saw.)

This structure is most common when an animate antecedent is the object of an inanimate subject, which would otherwise sound awkward.

## Special and Complex Relative Clauses

### The Embedded Clause (`An Clásal Idiraisnéiseach`)

When a verb of thinking, saying, or believing comes between an antecedent and its relative clause, Irish often uses a "double relative clause" structure.

- English: The man **who I think** is sick.
- Irish: *An fear **a cheapaim atá** tinn.* (Lit: The man **that I think that is** sick.)

Here, the main clause (`cheapaim`) is turned into a relative clause, and the original subordinate clause (`go bhfuil sé tinn`) is also turned into a relative clause (`atá tinn`). Both relative clauses refer to the same antecedent (`an fear`).

- *Cé **a shíleann tú a** thiocfaidh?* (Who do you think will come?)
- *Sin an leabhar **a dúirt sé a** cheannaigh sé.* (That's the book he said he bought.)

This structure is required with verbs like `síl`, `ceap` (to think), `abair` (to say), `creid` (to believe), and expressions like `is dóigh le` (seems to) and `ba mhaith le` (would like).

### The Comparative Relative Clause (`An Clásal Breischéime`)

To use a comparative adjective to describe a noun (attributively), Irish uses a direct copula relative clause. This is the structure behind the superlative.

- *an fear **is mó*** (the biggest man; Lit: "the man who is bigger")
- *an cailín **is óige*** (the youngest girl)

This structure is also used to express an adverbial superlative ("runs the fastest"). The sentence becomes a copula sentence where the logical subject is the predicate, followed by two relative clauses.

- *Is é Seán **is luaithe a ritheann**.* (Seán runs the fastest.)
- *Is í an dias is troime **is ísle a chromann a ceann**.* (The heaviest ear of corn bows its head lowest.)

### The Generalising Relative Pronoun

The particles `a` (eclipsis) and `ar` (lenition) can act as a special relative pronoun meaning "all that", "that which", or "all those who". This form is unique because it has no antecedent; the pronoun itself contains the meaning of the antecedent. It always takes a dependent verb form.

- *Sin **a bhfuil** anseo.* (That is all that is here.)
- *Chuala mé **ar dhúirt** sé.* (I heard all that he said.)
- *Ní thaitníonn **a bhfaca** mé liom.* (I don't like all that I saw.)

This pronoun can be further specified with the preposition `de` to mean "all of the...".

- *Sin **a bhfuil de** scéalta agam.* (That is all of the stories I have.)

A common related form is `dá`/`dár` (`de` + `a`/`ar`), often used after superlatives and words like `gach` (every) or `aon` (any).

- *An leabhar is fearr **dár léigh** mé riamh.* (The best book that I have ever read. Lit: "...of all that I ever read.")
- *Gach duine **dá raibh** ann.* (Every person who was there.)

The negative of the generalising relative pronoun is formed using `an méid nach...` (the amount that... not...).

- *Níor chreid mé **an méid nár** thuig mé.* (I didn't believe what I didn't understand.)
