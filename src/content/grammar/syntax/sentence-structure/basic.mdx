The basic structure of an Irish sentence is different from English. Understanding the core word order is the first step to forming correct sentences.

## Core Word Order: VSO

The most fundamental rule of Irish syntax is that the verb comes first in the sentence. The standard word order for a simple sentence is **Verb-Subject-Object (VSO)**.

- **V**erb: The action (`ólann` - drinks)
- **S**ubject: The person or thing doing the action (`an fear` - the man)
- **O**bject: The person or thing receiving the action (`bainne` - milk)

*Ólann an fear bainne.* - The man drinks milk.

This VSO order is the foundation for statements, questions, and negative sentences.

Note that constructions involving the verbal noun often follow a different word order, typically Subject-Object-Predicate (SOP).

## Sentence Constituents

A simple sentence is made up of several parts, or constituents.

### The Predicate (`an fhaisnéis`)

The predicate is the core statement of the sentence. In most cases, it is an action expressed by a **predicative verb**, like `ólann` (drinks) or `siúlann` (walks). This guide focuses on sentences built around these verbs.

In other sentence types, the predicate can be a noun or an adjective, usually connected to the subject by the copula (`is`). This creates a slightly different structure: Copula-Predicate-Subject.

- **Predicative Noun**: *Is múinteoir é Pól.* (Paul is a teacher.)
- **Predicative Adjective**: *Tá Pól óg.* (Paul is young.)

### The Subject (`an t-ainmní`)

The subject is the "doer" of the action in an active sentence. It is usually a noun or pronoun in the nominative case.

- *Ritheann **an madra**.* - **The dog** runs.
- *Léann **sí** leabhar.* - **She** reads a book.

In synthetic verb forms, the subject is included in the verb's ending, and a separate subject pronoun must not be added.

- `táim` - I am (subject `mé` is included)
- `bhíomar` - we were (subject `muid` is included)

However, an additional subject can be joined with `agus` (and). The verb only agrees with the first subject (the one in the ending).

- *Táimse agus cara liom i nGaillimh.* - I and a friend of mine are in Galway.

### The Object (`an cuspóir`)

The object is the person or thing that the action is done to.

- The **direct object** receives the action directly.
  - *Feicim **an teach**.* - I see **the house**.
- The **indirect object** typically appears with a preposition.
  - *Thug sé an leabhar **dom**.* - He gave the book **to me**.

Unlike some languages, Irish does not allow two direct objects. One must be expressed with a preposition.

- *Mhúin sí Gaeilge **dom**.* - She taught me Irish. (Literally: She taught Irish **to me**.)

## The Simple Positive Sentence

In a positive statement, the verb comes first, followed by the subject, the object, and then any other information like place, manner, or time.

### Word Order with Complements

The general order is:
**Verb + Subject + Direct Object + Indirect Object + Place + Manner + Time**

- *Thug an múinteoir an leabhar don dalta sa seomra ranga inné.*
- The teacher gave the book to the student in the classroom yesterday.

Some adverbs of time, such as `riamh` (ever), `i gcónaí` (always), `go minic` (often), and `fós` (still), can be placed before the object.

### Position of Pronominal Objects

A simple personal pronoun used as a direct object usually moves to the very end of the sentence, after all other complements.

**Verb + Subject + Indirect Object + ... + Direct Pronominal Object**

- *Thug sé don fhear **é**.* - He gave **it** to the man.
- *Scríobh sé litir dom **í**.* - He wrote me a letter. (lit. He wrote a letter to me, it.)

This is a common pattern, but the pronoun can sometimes appear earlier, as long as it comes after the verb, subject, and any obligatory objects.

### Word Order with Phrasal Verbs

When a verb is closely linked with a preposition or adverb (a "phrasal verb"), the indirect object or adverb is often placed between the subject and the direct object.

**Verb + Subject + Indirect Object/Adverb + Direct Object**

- *Chuir sí **uirthi** a cóta.* - She put her coat **on**.
- *Thug sé **dom** an litir.* - He gave **me** the letter.

This structure is mandatory in the imperative.

- *Scríobh **dom** litir!* - Write me a letter!

### Moving Complex Subjects

If a subject is very long (for example, modified by a relative clause), it can be moved to the end of the sentence. This is common with verbs of movement, appearance, and existence.

- *Tháinig isteach sa teach tábhairne **an fear a bhí ina chónaí i Spidéal**.*
- **The man who lived in Spiddal** came into the pub.

## The Negative Sentence

To make a sentence negative, you add a negative particle before the verb. The rest of the word order stays the same.

| Tense/Mood | Formula | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Present, Future, etc. | `Ní` + Verb + Subject + Object | *Ní fheiceann tú an fear.* (You do not see the man.) |
| Past (Preterite) | `Níor` + Verb + Subject + Object | *Níor ól mé fuisce.* (I did not drink whiskey.) |
| Imperative | `Ná` + Verb + Subject + Object | *Ná hól fuisce!* (Do not drink whiskey!) |

- `Ní` and `níor` cause **lenition** of the following verb.
- `Ná` causes an **h-prefix** on a following vowel but no lenition.
- All these particles require the **dependent form** of the verb where one exists.
- In Ulster, `cha` and `char` are often used instead of `ní` and `níor`.

### Multiple Negation

Irish does not have double negation in the same way as some languages. A sentence has only one negative particle (`ní`). However, words that correspond to "no", "nothing", or "nobody" are used alongside `ní`. These are called negative polarity items.

- `aon` - any
- `rud ar bith` - anything
- `aon duine` - anyone
- `tada` - nothing/anything

*Ní fhaca mé **aon duine**.* - I didn't see **anyone**. (lit. "Not saw I any person.")
*Ní dúirt sé **tada**.* - He said **nothing**. (lit. "Not said he anything.")

True double negation can occur across clauses (`ní ... nach ...`), creating an emphatic positive meaning.

- *Níl aon duine ann **nach** dtuigeann é.* - There is no one there who doesn't understand it. (i.e., Everyone there understands it.)

### Negating the Verbal Noun

The verbal noun is negated using the preposition `gan` (without).

- *D'imigh sé **gan focal a rá**.* - He left **without saying a word**.

### Emphatic Negation with `diabhal` or `dheamhan`

A strong, informal way to negate a statement is to use `diabhal` (devil) or `dheamhan` (demon). This is a common idiom and is not considered swearing. It translates roughly as "no", "not a", or "the hell... not".

There are several patterns:

1.  **`Diabhal/dheamhan` + Noun + Relative Clause**
    This means "no..." or "not a single...". The relative form of `bí` is often omitted.
    - *Diabhal Gaeilge atá aige.* - He has no Irish.
    - *Dheamhan pingin a bhí agam.* - I didn't have a penny.

2.  **`Diabhal/dheamhan` + Indirect Relative Clause**
    This means "not".
    - *Dheamhan ar thuig mé focal de.* - I didn't understand a word of it.

3.  **`Diabhal/dheamhan` + Generalising Relative Pronoun**
    This combination means "nothing".
    - *Diabhal a bhfuil déanta agam.* - I have done nothing.

## The Semi-Negative Sentence ("only")

To express the idea of "only", Irish often uses a "semi-negative" construction. The sentence starts with the negative particle `ní`, and the word `ach` (but) introduces the part of the sentence that is being limited.

**`Ní` + Verb + ... + `ach` + Limited Element**

- *Ní raibh ann **ach** Pól.* - **Only** Pól was there. (lit. "Was-not there but Paul.")
- *Ní ólann sé **ach** uisce.* - He **only** drinks water. (lit. "Not drinks he but water.")
- *Ní dhearna sé **ach** gáire.* - He did **nothing but** laugh.

## Questions and Answers

Forming and answering questions in Irish follows specific patterns that differ significantly from English.

### Polar Questions (Yes/No Questions)

To turn a statement into a question that can be answered with "yes" or "no", you add an interrogative particle to the beginning of the sentence.

| Tense/Mood | Particle | Formula | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Positive** | | | |
| Present, etc. | `An` | `An` + Verb + Subject + Object? | *An ólann tú fuisce?* (Do you drink whiskey?) |
| Past | `Ar` | `Ar` + Verb + Subject + Object? | *Ar ól tú fuisce?* (Did you drink whiskey?) |
| **Negative** | | | |
| Present, etc. | `Nach` | `Nach` + Verb + Subject + Object? | *Nach n-ólann tú fuisce?* (Don't you drink whiskey?) |
| Past | `Nár` | `Nár` + Verb + Subject + Object? | *Nár ól tú fuisce?* (Didn't you drink whiskey?) |

- `An` and `nach` cause **eclipsis**.
- `Ar` and `nár` cause **lenition**.
- All particles require the **dependent form** of the verb where one exists.
- In Munster, `ná` is often used instead of `nach`.

A question with `an` or `ar` is neutral. A question with `nach` or `nár` implies that the speaker expects a "yes" answer.

### Answering Polar Questions

Irish has no single words for "yes" and "no". To answer a polar question, you **repeat the verb from the question** in its positive or negative form.

- *An ólann tú fuisce?* (Do you drink whiskey?)
  - **`Ólann.`** (Yes. / lit. "Drink.")
  - **`Ní ólann.`** (No. / lit. "Don't drink.")

- *Ar ól tú fuisce?* (Did you drink whiskey?)
  - **`D'ól.`** (Yes. / lit. "Drank.")
  - **`Níor ól.`** (No. / lit. "Didn't drink.")

The subject and object are omitted as they are understood from the context. This is called an **answer ellipsis**.

The words `sea` and `ní hea` are sometimes mistaken for "yes" and "no". They are forms of the copula and are only used to answer copula questions with an indefinite predicate.

- *An múinteoir é?* (Is he a teacher?)
  - **`Sea.`** (Yes, he is.)
  - **`Ní hea.`** (No, he isn't.)

### Information Questions (Wh-Questions)

Questions that ask for specific information (who, what, where, why, etc.) are formed using a **cleft sentence**. The interrogative word comes first, followed by a relative clause.

**Interrogative Word + Relative Particle (`a`/`ar`) + Rest of Sentence**

The interrogative word acts as the predicate of a hidden copula.

- **Declarative**: *Rinne Pól é.* (Pól did it.)
- **Question**: *Cé **a** rinne é?* (Who did it? / lit. "Who is it that did it?")

- **Declarative**: *Cheannaigh sí an leabhar.* (She bought the book.)
- **Question**: *Cad **a** cheannaigh sí?* (What did she buy? / lit. "What is it that she bought?")

When the interrogative word involves a preposition, it is usually followed by an indirect relative clause.

- *Cé leis **ar** labhair tú?* (Who did you speak with? / lit. "Who is it with him that you spoke?")

### Answering Information Questions

The answer to an information question often mirrors the cleft structure of the question. The answer replaces the interrogative word.

- *Cé a rinne é?* (Who did it?)
  - **`Pól`** `(a rinne é).` - **Paul** (did it).

- *Cad a cheannaigh sí?* (What did she buy?)
  - **`An leabhar`** `(a cheannaigh sí).` - **The book** (is what she bought).

### Disjunctive Questions (Choice Questions)

Questions that offer a choice are usually formed with `cé acu` (which of them) or the interrogative particle `an`.

- *Cé acu is fearr leat, tae nó caife?* - Which do you prefer, tea or coffee?
- *An ólann tú tae nó caife?* - Do you drink tea or coffee?

## Special Topic: The Subject in Detail

While the basic VSO pattern is straightforward, the role of the subject can sometimes be complex.

### Grammatical vs. Logical Subject

In many idiomatic expressions, the person or thing that would be the subject in English appears in Irish as a prepositional phrase. This is the **logical subject**. The **grammatical subject** is a different noun in the sentence.

- *Tá ocras **orm**.* - I am hungry. (lit. "Is hunger **on me**.")
  - Grammatical subject: `ocras` (hunger)
  - Logical subject: `orm` (on me)

- *Tá Gaeilge **aici**.* - She speaks Irish. (lit. "Is Irish **at her**.")
  - Grammatical subject: `Gaeilge` (Irish)
  - Logical subject: `aici` (at her)

### Grammatical Subjectlessness

Some verbs and constructions can appear without any grammatical subject at all. The verb is in the 3rd person singular, and the logical subject is expressed with a preposition. This is common with **unaccusative verbs**, which describe events that happen without an agent.

- *Neartaigh **ar a ghlór**.* - His voice grew stronger. (lit. "Strengthened on his voice.")
- *Theip **orm** teacht.* - I failed to come. (lit. "Failed on-me to come.")

### Subject Omission (Ellipsis)

Besides answers to questions, the subject can be omitted in other contexts.

- **Chaining Actions**: When listing a series of actions performed by the same subject, the subject is only stated after the first verb.
  - *D'éirigh sé, d'ith sé a bhricfeasta agus d'fhág sé an teach.*
  - He got up, ate his breakfast, and left the house.
  - Can be shortened to: *D'éirigh sé, d'ith a bhricfeasta agus d'fhág an teach.*

This is not possible if you start with a synthetic verb form; the synthetic ending must be repeated on subsequent verbs.
