Word order in Irish is typically rigid, following a Verb-Subject-Object (VSO) pattern. Unlike English, you cannot easily reorder sentence elements or use intonation to emphasise a particular word. Instead, Irish uses specific grammatical structures, known as `treisiú` or `neartú`, to highlight different parts of a sentence.

The most common methods involve using the copula (`is`) to create special sentence types.

## Emphasis Using the Copula

There are two main ways to use the copula for emphasis: the cleft sentence and the pseudo-cleft sentence.

-   **The Cleft Sentence (`an abairt scoilte`)**: The element you want to emphasise is "split off" and moved to the front of the sentence, where it becomes the predicate of the copula. The rest of the original sentence follows as a relative clause.
    -   *Tógann sé teach.* (He builds a house.)
    -   *Is teach a thógann sé.* (It is a house that he builds.)

-   **The Pseudo-Cleft Sentence (`an abairt bhréagscoilte`)**: The element you want to emphasise is moved to the end of the sentence. The sentence begins with a phrase like `séard` ("the thing that is") or `is é rud` ("it is the thing"), followed by a relative clause.
    -   *Séard a thógann sé ná teach.* (What he builds is a house.)

Because free reordering of sentence elements is not possible in Irish, these two structures are essential for controlling focus and emphasis.

## The Cleft Sentence (`an abairt scoilte`)

The cleft sentence is the most common way to emphasise a part of a sentence.

### Usage of the Cleft Sentence

Cleft sentences have several functions beyond simple emphasis:

-   **Emphasis and Contrast**: This is the most straightforward use, highlighting one element over others.
    -   *Is é an leabhar a léigh sé.* (He read **the book**... and not the newspaper.)

-   **Text Structuring**: They are often used to introduce new information, people, or topics, especially at the beginning of a text or a new scene.
    -   *Cad a tháinig ort? - Na saighdiúirí a scaoil liom.* (What happened to you? - **The soldiers** shot at me.)

-   **Emphasising Truth or Degree**: Sometimes, the cleft structure is used to add weight to the entire statement or to express a high degree of something, rather than just focusing on the fronted element.
    -   *Is againn a bhí an lá!* (What a great day we had!)
    -   *Is aige atá an fhoighne.* (He has a lot of patience.)
    -   *Nach é atá te!* (Isn't it hot! / How hot it is!)
    -   *"Tá sé fuar." - "Is é atá."* ("It's cold." - "It certainly is.")

### Emphasising Sentence Elements

The basic structure of a cleft sentence is `is` + [emphasised element] + [direct relative clause].

-   **What can be emphasised?** Almost any sentence element can be fronted: the subject, direct or indirect object, adverbs, prepositional phrases, and even subordinate clauses. You always emphasise the entire element, not just a single word (e.g., you move `an fear mór`, not just `mór`).
-   **Pronouns**: Personal pronouns like `mé`, `tú`, `é` are used. Emphatic forms like `mise` are used for stronger contrast.
    -   *An tú a fuair é?* (Did **you** find it?)
    -   *Mise a rinne é.* (**I** did it, not you.)
-   **Copula Forms**: You can use other forms of the copula (`ní`, `an`, `nach`, `gur`), but past/conditional forms (`ba`, `níor`) are usually avoided in favour of present forms.
    -   *Ní anois!* (Not now!)
-   **The Pronoun `é/í/iad`**: Before certain nouns (like definite nouns), a pronoun `é`, `í`, or `iad` is placed after the copula.
    -   *Is é an fear sin a chonaic mé.* (I saw **that man**.)
-   **Omitting `is`**: The present tense copula `is` is often omitted, especially before indefinite nouns or in answers to questions. If `is` is omitted, any accompanying pronoun (`é/í/iad`) is also dropped.
    -   *Fear a chonaic mé.* (I saw **a man**.) (Not: *Is fear...*)
    -   *Cé a dúirt sin? - Pól a dúirt sin.* (Who said that? - **Paul** said that.)

| Emphasis | Structure | Example | English |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Normal** | Verb + Subject + Object | *Feiceann tú an fear.* | You see the man. |
| **Subject** | `Is` + Subject + `a` + Verb... | *Is tú a fheiceann an fear.* | **You** see the man. |
| **Object** | `Is` + Object + `a` + Verb... | *Is é an fear a fheiceann tú.* | It is **the man** you see. |
| **Indirect Object** | `Is` + Prep. Phrase + `a` + Verb... | *Is don fhear a thugann tú é.* | It is **to the man** you give it. |
| **Adverb** | `Is` + Adverb + `a` + Verb... | *Is abhaile a théann an fear.* | It is **home** the man goes. |
| **Predicative Adj.** | `Is` + Adjective + `a` + Verb... | *Is te atá sé.* | It is **hot** that it is. |

**Further Notes:**

-   With adverbs and prepositional phrases, `is ea` can be used after the emphasised element: *Abhaile is ea a théann an fear.* (Home it is that the man goes.)
-   With temporal and locational phrases, `is ann` can be used: *An áit a bhfuil do chroí is ann a thabharfas do chosa thú.* (Where your heart is, there your feet will carry you.)
-   Interrogative questions (`cé?`, `cad?`) are inherently cleft sentences: *Cé a rinne é?* (Who did it? / Who is it that did it?).
-   The relative form of `bí` is sometimes omitted, especially with measurements: *Cén aois é?* for *Cén aois atá sé?* (How old is he?).

#### Emphasis in the Progressive Aspect

When emphasising elements in a sentence using the progressive aspect (`tá` + `ag` + verbal noun), the structure changes.

| Emphasis | Structure | Example | English |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Normal** | `Tá` + Subj + `ag` + VN + Obj (Gen) | *Tá mé ag scríobh an leabhair.* | I am writing the book. |
| **Subject** | `Is` + Subj + `atá` + `ag` + VN... | *Mise atá ag scríobh an leabhair.* | **I** am writing the book. |
| **Verbal Noun** | `Is ag` + VN + `atá` + Subj + Obj (Nom) | *Is ag scríobh atá mé an leabhar.* | It is **writing** the book I am. |
| **Object** | `Is` + Obj (Nom) + `atá` + Subj + `a` + VN | *Is é an leabhar atá mé a scríobh.* | It is **the book** that I am writing. |

Notice two key changes:
1.  When the object is separated from the verbal noun, it reverts from the genitive to the nominative/accusative case.
2.  When the object itself is emphasised, the particle `ag` becomes `a` (which causes lenition).

### Emphasising the Action

To emphasise the verb or action itself, you can't simply move the verb. Instead, you use a construction with the verbal noun.

**1. Using the Progressive Aspect**

You can front the entire verbal noun phrase (`ag` + VN + Object).

| Emphasis | Structure | Example | English |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Normal** | `Tá` + Subj + `ag` + VN + Obj (Gen) | *Tá mé ag scríobh an leabhair.* | I am writing the book. |
| **Action** | `Is ag` + VN + Obj (Gen) + `atá` + Subj | *Is ag scríobh an leabhair atá mé.* | It is **writing the book** that I am. |

**2. Using `déan` and the Verbal Noun**

You can create a cleft sentence where the action is expressed as a verbal noun, and the main verb becomes a form of `déan` (to do).

| Emphasis | Structure | Example | English |
| :--- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Normal** | Verb + Subject + Object | *Bhuail tú an fear.* | You hit the man. |
| **Action** | Object + `a` + VN + `a rinne` + Subject | *An fear a bhualadh a rinne tú.* | It was **hitting the man** that you did. |

### Cleft Sentences with `go`/`nach` Clauses

You can also use cleft structures to emphasise parts of a `go`/`nach` clause.

-   **Emphasis within the clause**: The element moves to the front of the subordinate clause, which then begins with `gur`/`nach` instead of `go`/`nach`.
    -   *Dúirt sé go raibh sé ina chónaí i gCorcaigh.* (He said that he lived in Cork.)
    -   *Dúirt sé gur i gCorcaigh a bhí sé ina chónaí.* (He said that it was **in Cork** that he lived.)

-   **Emphasis in the main sentence**: The element moves to the very front of the entire sentence. This creates a "double relative clause".
    -   *Is i gCorcaigh a dúirt sé a bhí sé ina chónaí.* (It was **in Cork** that he said he lived.)

### Cleft Sentences with Comparatives

Comparatives are often handled with a cleft-like structure, especially when they would otherwise appear inside a relative clause. The structure becomes `is` + [comparative] + `a` + [verb].

| Type | Example | English |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Normal** | *Bíonn na laethanta níos fuaire sa gheimhreadh.* | The days are colder in winter. |
| **Cleft** | *Sa gheimhreadh is fuaire a bhíonn na laethanta.* | In winter the days are **colder**. |

## The Pseudo-Cleft Sentence (`an abairt bhréagscoilte`)

This structure emphasises an element by moving it to the end of the sentence.

### Emphasising the Object

The sentence starts with a phrase like `séard` or `is é rud`, followed by a relative clause, and ends with the emphasised object, often introduced by `ná`.

| Type | Structure | Example | English |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Normal** | Verb + Subject + Object | *Chonaic mé capall.* | I saw a horse. |
| **Pseudo-Cleft** | `Séard a` + Verb + Subj + `ná` + Object | *Séard a chonaic mé ná capall.* | What I saw was **a horse**. |

**Notes on Form:**

-   `Is é rud` is often contracted to `séard` (Connacht) or `sé rud` (Munster, Ulster). In Ulster, `siod` is also common.
-   The conjunction `ná` (lit. "than") is standard but often omitted outside Munster, replaced by a pause.
-   Instead of `rud` (thing), a more specific noun can be used: *Is é an leabhar a léigh sí ná "Cré na Cille".* (The book she read was "Cré na Cille".)

### Emphasising a `go`/`nach` Clause

The same structure is used to emphasise a subordinate clause, which acts as the object of the main verb.

| Type | Structure | Example | English |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Normal** | Verb + Subj + `go` clause | *Dúirt sé go bhfaca sé an fear.* | He said that he saw the man. |
| **Pseudo-Cleft** | `Séard a` + Verb + Subj + `ná` + `go` clause | *Séard a dúirt sé ná go bhfaca sé an fear.* | What he said was **that he saw the man**. |

The conjunction `ná` is only used before `go`; it is not used before `nach`.

### Emphasising the Subject

This is less common but possible. The structure is `Is é a` + [verb phrase] + `ná` + [subject].

| Type | Structure | Example | English |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Normal** | Verb + Subject + Object | *Rinne Séamas é.* | Séamas did it. |
| **Pseudo-Cleft** | `Is é a` + Verb + Obj + `ná` + Subject | *Is é a rinne é ná Séamas.* | The one who did it was **Séamas**. |

### Emphasising the Action

To emphasise the action, the sentence begins with an introductory phrase, followed by a relative clause containing the original sentence. The emphasised element isn't moved, but the introductory phrase signals that the action is the focus.

| Introductory Phrase | Example | English |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `Is é an chaoi` | *Is é an chaoi ar bhuail tú an fear.* | What you did was hit the man. |
| `Is amhlaidh` | *Is amhlaidh a bhuail tú an fear.* | The fact is, you hit the man. |
| `Is ea` | *Is ea a bhuail tú an fear.* | It is the case that you hit the man. |

Note: `Is é an chaoi` takes an indirect relative particle (`ar` in the past tense), while the others take a direct relative particle (`a`).

### Pseudo-Clefts in Interrogative Sentences

Rhetorical questions can use a pseudo-cleft structure with an interrogative word like `céard` or `cad`, followed by `ach` or `ná`.

-   *Céard eile a chonaic sí ansin ach capall?* (What else did she see there but a horse?)
-   *Cad a dhéanfainn, mar sin, ach é a bhualadh?* (What else should I have done but hit him?)

## Emphasising Factuality with `go`/`nach` Clauses

To emphasise the truth or factuality of a statement, especially in questions, negations, and conditions, you can use an introductory copula phrase followed by a `go`/`nach` clause.

| | Negative | Question | Condition |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `é` | `ní hé go` | `an é go` | `más é go` |
| `ea` | `ní hea go` | `an ea go` | `más ea go` |
| `amhlaidh` | `ní hamhlaidh go` | `an amhlaidh go` | `más amhlaidh go` |
| `rud` | `ní rud é go` | `an rud é go` | `más rud é go` |

-   *Ní hé nach raibh athair agus máthair aige.* (It is not that he had no father and mother.)
-   *An amhlaidh go bhfuil tú ag imeacht?* (Is it the case that you are leaving?)

## Emphasis Without the Copula

Other methods of changing word order exist that do not rely on the copula.

### Left and Right Shifting (`Nominativus Absolutus`)

This technique involves moving a complex phrase to the beginning (left-shifting) or end (right-shifting) of a sentence for clarity or emphasis. A "resumptive" pronoun is left behind in the phrase's original position.

#### Left-Shifting the Subject

A long subject, especially one modified by a relative clause, can be moved to the front. A pronoun (`sé`, `sí`, `siad`) then takes its place in the main clause.

-   **Normal**: *Bhí an fear a chonaic mé ar an tsráid ag dul abhaile.* (The man whom I saw on the street was going home.)
-   **Left-Shifted**: *An fear a chonaic mé ar an tsráid, bhí sé ag dul abhaile.* (The man whom I saw on the street, he was going home.)

This is very common with `an té` (the one who):
-   *An té a bhíos idir dhá stól, gabhfaidh sé go hurlár.* (He who is between two stools will fall to the floor.)

#### Left- and Right-Shifting the Object

A complex object can be shifted to the front or back to avoid ambiguity. A resumptive pronoun (`é`, `í`, `iad`) is left in its place.

-   **Ambiguous**: *Chonaic mé an fear a rinne an obair inné.* (Did I see the man yesterday, or did the man do the work yesterday?)
-   **Left-Shifted**: *An fear a rinne an obair inné, chonaic mé é.* (The man who did the work yesterday, I saw him.)
-   **Right-Shifted**: *Chonaic mé é inné, an fear a rinne an obair.* (I saw him yesterday, the man who did the work.)

This principle also applies to prepositional phrases, where a prepositional pronoun is left behind.

### The Anacoluthon (`an t-éigeangal`)

This is not a reordering but a "restarting" of the sentence after a complex element. The verb is repeated.

-   **Normal**: *Chonaic an fear a rinne an obair inné mé.* (The man who did the work yesterday saw me.)
-   **Anacoluthon**: *Chonaic an fear a rinne an obair, chonaic sé inné mé.* (The man who did the work, he saw me yesterday.)

### Fronting in Special Cases

In a few specific contexts, an element can be moved to the very front of the sentence, before the verb, without a copula or a resumptive pronoun.

#### "Narrative Fronting" in Negative Sentences

In negative sentences, an indefinite subject or object can be moved before the negative particle (`ní`, `cha`) to strongly emphasise the negation, meaning "not a single..." or "no... at all".

-   **Normal**: *Ní bhfuair mé litir.* (I did not receive a letter.)
-   **Fronted**: *Litir ní bhfuair mé.* (**Not a** letter did I receive.)
-   *Braon eile ní bhfaighir.* (You will not get **another drop**.)
-   *Litir ná glaoch ní bhfuair mé uaithi.* (I received **neither a letter nor a call** from her.)

In the progressive aspect, this fronting changes `ag` to `a` before the verbal noun:
-   *Pingin ní raibh mé a shaothrú.* (**Not a** penny was I earning.)

#### Fronting in Optative Sentences (Wishes)

Objects can be moved to the front of sentences expressing a wish, before `go` (positive) or `nár` (negative).

-   *Trócaire is grásta go bhfaighe tú!* (Mercy and grace may you receive!)
-   *Toil Dé go ndéantar!* (God's will may it be done!)

#### Archaic Fronting

In poetic or archaic language, an object can be fronted in a positive sentence, followed by the particle `go`.

-   *Mo chairde gaoil gur thréig mé.* (My dear friends I abandoned.)
