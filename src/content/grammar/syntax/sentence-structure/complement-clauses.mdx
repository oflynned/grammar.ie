A complement clause is a subordinate clause that functions as a core part of a main clause, typically as its subject or object. In Irish, these are most often introduced by the particles `go` (that) and `nach` (that... not). They are the equivalent of English "that"-clauses.

## Forming Complement Clauses

The introductory particle changes depending on the tense and whether the clause is positive or negative.

### Present and Future Tenses

In the present and future tenses, `go` is used for positive clauses and `nach` for negative ones. Both particles cause eclipsis of the following verb and require its dependent form.

| Type | Structure | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Positive** | `go` + Verb (eclipsed) + Subject + Object | *Sílim go bhfuil tú sásta.* (I think that you are happy.) |
| **Negative** | `nach` + Verb (eclipsed) + Subject + Object | *Sílim nach bhfuil tú sásta.* (I think that you are not happy.) |

### Past Tenses

In the past tense (and conditional mood), `gur` is used for positive clauses and `nár` for negative ones. Both particles cause lenition of the following verb and require its dependent form.

| Type | Structure | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Positive** | `gur` + Verb (lenited) + Subject + Object | *Bhí mé sásta gur shíl tú é.* (I was satisfied that you believed it.) |
| **Negative** | `nár` + Verb (lenited) + Subject + Object | *Bhí mé sásta nár shíl tú é.* (I was satisfied that you did not believe it.) |

**Dialect Note:** In Munster Irish, `ná` is often used instead of `nach`. This form does not cause any initial mutation.

## Grammatical Roles of Complement Clauses

A complement clause can serve several grammatical functions within a sentence.

-   **Object Clause:** The most common role, acting as the direct object of a verb of thinking, saying, or feeling.
    -   *Creidim go dtiocfaidh sé.* (I believe that he will come.)
-   **Subject Clause:** Acting as the subject of the main verb.
    -   *Is maith liom go bhfuil tú ann.* (I like that you are there. / Lit: That you are there is good with me.)
-   **Predicate Clause:** Acting as the predicate in a copular sentence.
-   **Attributive Clause:** Modifying a noun, similar to a genitive attribute.
    -   *Tá eagla orm go dtitfidh mé.* (I am afraid that I will fall. / Lit: Fear is on me that I will fall.)
-   **After a Preposition:** The clause can be governed by a preposition.
    -   *gan trácht ar go mbeidh sí ina hiníon agam* (not to mention that she will be my daughter)

## Other Types of Complement Clauses

Besides `go`/`nach` clauses, other constructions can also function as complements:

-   **Indirect Questions:** Introduced by `an` (whether) or `nach` (whether not), or by interrogative words like `cé` (who) and `cad` (what).
    -   *Chuir sé ceist orm an raibh mé riamh ansin.* (He asked me if I had ever been there.)
-   **Clauses with `chomh`:** Expressing "how".
    -   *Ní dhearna sé dearmad go deo ar chomh cruachroíoch is a bhí an rialtas.* (He never forgot how hard-hearted the government had been.)

## Word Order and Adverbs

The particles `go`, `nach`, `gur`, and `nár` must stand immediately before the verb of the complement clause. Adverbs or adverbial phrases cannot be placed between the particle and the verb.

Adverbial phrases that modify the complement clause must be placed either:
1.  Before the entire clause (before `go`/`nach`).
2.  At the end of the entire clause.

This rule also applies to compound conjunctions like `i dtreo go` (so that). Any adverbial phrase appears between the two parts of the conjunction, e.g., `i dtreo [adverb] go...`.

## Subject Clauses and the Expletive Pronoun `sé`

When a `go`/`nach` clause acts as the subject of a verb, it often appears after the verb. In this case, an expletive (or "dummy") pronoun `sé` (it) is sometimes used in the normal subject position.

-   **`sé` is often required** if other words come between the verb and the subject clause.
    -   *Tharla sé go minic go raibh an t-earrach níos fuaire ná an geimhreadh.* (It often happened that spring was colder than winter.)
-   **`sé` is often optional** if the subject clause immediately follows the verb.
    -   *Tharla (sé) go raibh roinnt airgid aici.* (It happened that she had some money.)
-   **`sé` is necessary** after certain verbs.
    -   *Bhreathnaigh sé go raibh aithne acu uilig air.* (It seemed that they all knew him.)
-   **`sé` is not used** in copular sentences where the clause is the subject.
    -   *Is maith liom go bhfuil tú ann.* (I like that you are there.)

## Complement Clauses vs. Verbal Noun Constructions

In many cases, a verbal noun construction can be used instead of a `go`/`nach` clause, even when the subjects of the main and subordinate clauses are different.

-   **`go`-Clause:** *Ba mhaith liom go mbeifeá sásta.* (I would like that you would be satisfied.)
-   **Verbal Noun:** *Ba mhaith liom tú a bheith sásta.* (I would like you to be satisfied.)

However, the two constructions are not always interchangeable and can carry different meanings.

### Modality: Conclusion vs. Obligation

With modal verbs like `caithfidh` (must), the choice of construction distinguishes between a logical conclusion (epistemic modality) and an obligation (deontic modality).

-   **`go`-Clause (Conclusion):** *Caithfidh gur imigh sé.* (It must be that he has left. / He must have left.)
-   **Verbal Noun (Obligation):** *Caithfidh sé imeacht.* (He must leave.)

### Intention vs. Statement

With verbs of thinking or saying, the verbal noun construction can imply an intention or goal, whereas the `go` clause simply reports a thought or fact.

-   **`go`-Clause (Statement):** *Shíl sé go ndearna sé é.* (He thought that he had done it.)
-   **Verbal Noun (Intention):** *Shíl sé é a dhéanamh.* (He intended to do it.)

### Indirect Speech: Statement vs. Command

In indirect speech, the `go` clause reports a statement, while the verbal noun construction reports a command (imperative).

-   **`go`-Clause (Statement):** *Dúirt sé liom gur imigh sé.* (He told me that he had gone.)
    -   Direct speech: *"D’imigh mé."* (I went.)
-   **Verbal Noun (Command):** *Dúirt sé liom imeacht.* (He told me to go.)
    -   Direct speech: *"Imigh!"* (Go!)

## Alternative Phrasing

Instead of using a `go` clause at the beginning of a sentence, it is common to state the main point first and add the verb of thinking, saying, or feeling at the end as a tag.

-   **With `go` clause:** *Déarfainn go bhfuil a mhalairt ar fad fíor.* (I would say that the complete opposite is true.)
-   **With postposed verb:** *Tá a mhalairt ar fad fíor, déarfainn.* (The complete opposite is true, I would say.)

Similarly, modal expressions like `b'fhéidir` (perhaps) can be moved.

-   **With `go` clause:** *B’fhéidir go mbeidh an t-ádh linn.* (Perhaps we will be lucky.)
-   **With postposed adverb:** *Beidh an t-ádh linn, b’fhéidir.* (We will be lucky, perhaps.)

When a verb like `dúirt` is used to quote direct speech, it is introduced by a relative clause: *“Tá a mhalairt fíor,” a dúirt mé.* ("The opposite is true," I said.)

## Transformation into Relative Clauses

A sentence containing a `go` complement clause can be transformed into a structure with a relative clause. This often involves turning the complement clause into a second, embedded relative clause.

-   **Original:** *Shíl mé go raibh an fear i nGaillimh.* (I thought that the man was in Galway.)
-   **Transformed:** *Sin é an fear a shíl mé a bhí i nGaillimh.* (That is the man who I thought was in Galway.)
