This page covers two less common but idiomatic sentence structures in Irish: short, verb-less sentences used for commands or lively narration, and optative sentences used to express wishes, blessings, and curses.

## Short Sentences

Irish uses several types of short, verb-less sentences, particularly in commands and narrative styles.

### With Directional Adverbs and `le`

This structure uses a directional adverb with the preposition `le` to form an imperative, similar to the English "Down with you!". It can also be used in a lively narrative style as a declarative sentence.

- *Suas an crann leis agus …* - Up the tree with him and…
- *Síos an bóthar liom …* - Down the road I went…

Adverbial pronouns can also function as directional adverbs in this pattern:

- *agus as go brách leis* - and away with him forever (`as` = out)

This structure is so common as a command that the adverbs sometimes take on imperative verb endings:

- *Síosaigí libh!* - Down with you (plural)!
- *Anuasadh leis!* - Down with it!

The preposition `le` is also frequently used to intensify verbs of motion:

- *D’imigh sí léi.* - She went away. (literally, "She went with her.")
- *Imigh leat!* - Clear off! / Be off with you!

### With an Introductory Demonstrative Pronoun

This construction creates a sense of "off we go with..." or "away with...". It begins with a demonstrative pronoun, usually `seo` ("here") or `siúd` ("there"), which acts as the starting point of the movement.

- **`seo`** (preterite `b’eo`) is typically used in a first-person narrative.
- **`siúd`** (preterite `b’iúd`) is used for greater distance or in a third-person narrative.
- The pronoun `sin` is not used in this construction.
- The preterite forms (`b’eo`, `b’iúd`) are optional, even when the main narrative is in the past tense.

The pronoun is followed by a direction or purpose. The preposition `le` can be used, but a simple personal pronoun is also common.

**Examples:**

- *Seo liom síos mar a raibh na naomhóga.* - Off I went down to where the boats were.
- *B’eo leis an madra ina dhiaidh.* - Off went the dog after it.
- *agus b’eo linn go dtí an caladh* - and off we went to the harbour.
- *Seo libh canaídh Amhrán na bhFiann.* - Off with you, sing the Soldier's Song!
- *Seo abhaile iad.* - Off home with them.
- *agus siúd leo ar thóir an fhuadaitheora* - and off they went in pursuit of the kidnapper.
- *Siúd thar an droichead é.* - Off he went over the bridge.
- *B’iúd iad ag imeacht ar cosa in airde.* - Off they went at a gallop.
- *Phreab Séanna ina shuí agus siúd suas é.* - Séanna jumped up and up he went.

In a sequence of actions, `siúd` precedes `seo`:

- *Siúd amach an giorria agus seo sinne ina dhiaidh.* - Out went the hare and off we went after it.

## The Optative Sentence (`an clásal guítheach`)

The subjunctive mood can form an independent main clause to express wishes, blessings, curses, or hopes. These are known as optative sentences. They are often fixed, traditional phrases.

### The Real Optative (`an guítheach réalta`)

The real optative expresses a wish that is considered possible. It uses the present subjunctive.

| Type | Structure |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Positive** | `Go` + Present Subjunctive + Subject + Object |
| **Negative** | `Nár` + Present Subjunctive + Subject + Object |

- The particle `go` causes eclipsis.
- The particle `nár` causes lenition. (`nár` is a combination of `ná` "that not" and the old perfect particle `ro`).
- With the verb `bí`, the negative form is `ná raibh` (as `raibh` already contains the `-r-`).
- The copula forms are `gura` and `nára`.

**Examples:**

- *Go raibh maith agat!* - Thank you! (literally, "May there be good at you.")
- *Go n-éirí an bóthar leat!* - Good luck! (literally, "May the road rise with you.")
- *Go dtaga do ríocht!* - Thy kingdom come!
- *Go bhfóire Dia orainn!* - May God help us!
- *Gura slán an scéalaí!* - May the storyteller be safe!
- *Nár laga Dia do lámh!* - May God not weaken your hand!
- *Nár lige Dia!* - God forbid!

Objects and other sentence parts can be moved to the front for emphasis:

- *Trócaire is grásta go bhfaighe tú!* - Mercy and grace may you receive!
- *Toil Dé go ndéantar!* - God's will be done!
- *Rath ná raibh ort go deo!* - May you never have luck!
- *Ar dheis Dé go raibh a hanam.* - May her soul be at the right hand of God.

#### Elliptical Optative Sentences

Many common blessings and curses are elliptical, omitting `go` and the verb.

- *(Go mbeannaí) Dia duit!* - Hello! (literally, "(May God bless) you.")
- *(Go raibh) Slán leat!* - Goodbye! (literally, "(May) health (be) with you.")
- *(Go raibh) Bail ó Dhia ort!* - Good luck! (literally, "(May) prosperity from God (be) on you.")

In the negative, `gan` ("without") can replace `ná raibh`.

- *Gan rath ná sonas ort!* - May you have no luck or happiness! (Same as *Ná raibh rath ná sonas ort!*)

A verbal noun can also be used instead of a subjunctive verb, often to form a curse. The person affected is usually introduced with `ar` ("on") or `chuig` ("to").

- *Luí gan éirí ort!* - May you lie down and never get up again! (literally, "Lying without rising on you!")
- *Cur gan choinneall air!* - May he be buried without a candle!
- *Briseadh na gcos chugat!* - May you break your legs! (literally, "Breaking of the legs to you!")

### The Irreal Optative (`an guítheach samhalta`)

The irreal optative expresses a wish that is contrary to fact or unlikely to happen. It uses the past subjunctive.

| Type | Structure |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Positive** | `Dá` + Past Subjunctive + Subject + Object |
| **Negative** | `Mura` + Past Subjunctive + Subject + Object |

Both `dá` ("if") and `mura` ("if not") cause eclipsis.

**Examples:**

- *Dá bpósadh sé an cailín seo!* - If only he had married this girl!
- *Mura gceannaíodh sé an seanchapall!* - If only he had not bought the old horse!

In response to a statement, an elliptical form (`dá` + verb) is enough to express a wish:

- *"Dá mbeadh!" arsa Síle.* - "If only it were so!" said Síle.

#### Alternative Constructions

A wish can be expressed using `dá mb’áil le` ("if it were the wish of"):

- *Dá mb’áil leis teacht.* - If only he had come. (literally, "If it were his wish to come.")

In the second person, this can be a polite way to make a request in the present:

- *Dá mb’áil leat é a dhéanamh!* - Please be so kind as to do it. (literally, "If it were your wish to do it.")

Another alternative uses `ach` ("but") with a verbal noun construction:

- *Ach mé greim a fháil air!* - If only I could get hold of him! (literally, "But me to get a grip on him!")
