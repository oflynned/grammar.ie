### Definition

In Irish grammar, a **small clause** is a construction where a form of the verb `bí` (to be) is omitted. Because it lacks a finite verb, it is an infinitive construction.

While similar structures exist in English (e.g., in picture captions: "Me on holiday"), they are far more common and structurally important in Irish. They can function as an alternative to finite subordinate clauses or other infinitive constructions.

Essentially, any sentence using the verb `bí` can be thought of as `bí` plus a small clause. The verb `bí` adds finite properties (like tense and mood) to the small clause. When `bí` is omitted under certain grammatical conditions, the small clause remains.

### The Structure of a Small Clause

A small clause always contains a subject and a predicative (an adjective, prepositional phrase, or verbal noun phrase).

-   **Positive:** Subject + Predicative
-   **Negative:** `gan` + Subject + Predicative

#### The Subject

The case of the subject and the form of pronouns depend on the surrounding syntax.

-   **Case:** The subject is often in the nominative (e.g., after `agus` or `ach`), but can be in the accusative (e.g., after `gan` or verbs of perception) or dative (after prepositions in phrasal verbs).
-   **Pronoun Forms:** The subject pronouns `sé`, `sí`, and `siad` are replaced by the disjunctive forms `é`, `í`, and `iad`. For `tú`, both `tú` and `thú` are found.
    -   *Tá sé sásta.* (He is happy.) → `é sásta`
    -   *Tá sí ag gáire.* (She is laughing.) → `í ag gáire`
-   **Exception with Verbs of Motion:** When the subject of the small clause is also the subject of a main verb of motion (or beginning/stopping), the conjunct pronouns `sé`, `sí`, `siad` are used.
    -   *Tháinig sé ag damhsa.* (He came dancing.)
-   **Pronoun Position:** A simple pronoun subject can move to the end of the small clause, especially when it is also the object of the main clause.
    -   *Chuala mé thú ag gáire.* or *Chuala mé ag gáire thú.* (I heard you laughing.)
    -   This movement is not possible if the small clause is introduced by a preposition (like `gan`) or a conjunction.
-   **Prepositional Pronouns:** If a small clause with a pronoun subject follows a phrasal verb, the preposition and pronoun combine to form a prepositional pronoun.
    -   *Leanann sí **uirthi** ag gáire.* (She continued laughing.) (from `leanann sí ar` + `í ag gáire`)

#### Negation with `gan`

Small clauses are negated using the preposition `gan` (without).

-   *Níl sé ag damhsa.* (He is not dancing.) → `gan é ag damhsa`
-   *Níl againn ach iad.* (We only have them.) → `gan againn ach iad`

However, `gan` cannot be used to negate a small clause after verbs of perception or beginning. In these cases, the main verb must be negated instead.

-   *Ní chonaic mé Eoghan ag imeacht.* (I did not see Eoghan leave.)
-   *Ní aireoidh tú uait mé.* (You will not miss me.)

### Common Uses of the Small Clause

Small clauses appear in a wide variety of grammatical contexts.

#### With Conjunctions `agus` and `ach`

They function like subordinate clauses, often indicating attendant circumstances.

-   *Agus é ag gáire, thit sé go talamh.* (While he was laughing, he fell to the ground.)
-   *Aireoidh tú uait mé is gan mé ann.* (You will miss me when I am no longer there.)

A special form `is amhlaidh ... agus` means "namely" or "that is to say".

-   *Is amhlaidh a bhí sé agus caipín ar a cheann.* (So he was, namely with a cap on his head.)

In the Munster dialect, `bí + agus + small clause` is a common structure.

-   *Bhí Eoghan agus eagla air.* (Eoghan was afraid. Literally: Eoghan was and fear on him.)

#### After Verbs of Perception

With verbs like `feic` (see), `clois` (hear), and `airigh` (feel/notice), a small clause often replaces the "accusative with infinitive" construction found in other languages.

-   *Chuala mé thú ag gáire.* (I heard you laughing.)
-   *Chonaic sé í ina Maighdean Mhara.* (He saw her as a mermaid.)
-   *Aireoidh tú uait mé.* (You will miss me. Literally: You will feel me from you.)

#### After Verbs of State Change

Verbs of beginning, stopping, and continuing are frequently followed by a small clause.

-   *Thosaigh mé ag ól.* (I began to drink.)
-   *Choinnigh sé ag cur ceisteanna.* (He kept asking questions.)
-   *Leanann sé air ag léamh.* (He continued to read.)

#### After `cuir` (to make/cause)

The verb `cuir` can be used with a small clause to mean "to make someone do something".

-   *Cuirfidh sé ag gáire thú.* (It will make you laugh.)

#### After `tarlaigh` (to happen)

-   *Tharla é ina dhlíodóir.* (It happened that he was a lawyer.)
-   *ó tharla ina chónaí ina aonar é* (since he lives alone)

#### After Verbs of Motion

The subject of the main verb is also the subject of the small clause. This often expresses purpose.

-   *Tháinig sé ag foghlaim Gaeilge.* (He came to learn Irish.)
-   *Tháinig sé ina rith.* (He came running.)

#### In Cleft Sentences

When an adverb or measurement is fronted in a cleft sentence, the relative form of `bí` (`atá`, `a bhí`, etc.) is often omitted, leaving a small clause.

-   *Is annamh ag gáire é.* (He rarely laughs.) (from *Is annamh a bhíonn sé ag gáire.*)
-   *Cén aois thú?* (How old are you?) (from *Cén aois atá tú?*)
-   *Ba mhinic iad ina suí lasmuigh.* (They often sat outside.)

This also occurs with fronted progressive forms, often in short, idiomatic interjections.

-   *Ní á mhaíomh ort é.* (I'm not begrudging it to you.)
-   *Ní ag teacht romhat é.* (Excuse the interruption.)

#### As a Noun Attribute

A small clause can modify a noun, functioning like a relative clause.

-   *an mhí le teacht* (the coming month)
-   *obair le déanamh* (work to be done)
-   *fear ag dul aníos an bóthar* (a man going up the road)

#### As a Standalone Clause

Small clauses can stand alone as main clauses, particularly in narrative prose and poetry. This stylistic choice removes a specific time reference, creating a more descriptive or pictorial effect.

-   *É ag breathnú amach ar an gcuan.* (He looks/looked out at the harbour.)
-   *D’fhanadar tamall maith mar sin – eisean á ceistiú agus ise á fhreagairt.* (They remained like that for a good while – he questioning her and she answering him.)

They are also common in headings and titles.

-   *An Chré á Cumadh* (The Earth is Formed)

#### Other Contexts

-   **Depictive Adjectives:** An adjective describing the state of the subject or object during an action.
    -   *Tháinig sé abhaile fliuch báite.* (He came home soaking wet.)
    -   *Itheann siad amh é.* (They eat it raw.)
-   **Optative Sentences:** The optative form `go raibh` is often omitted.
    -   *Bail ó Dhia ort.* (God's blessing on you.)
-   **Phrases with `mairg` and `trua`:**
    -   *Is mór an trua gan é anseo.* (It is a great pity that he is not here.)

### A Note on Terminology

The term "small clause" comes from generative grammar. Traditional Irish grammar sometimes refers to this structure as `fo-rá saor` or an "absolute phrase". A modern literal translation might be `mionchlásal`.
