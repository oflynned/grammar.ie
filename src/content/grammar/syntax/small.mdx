A small clause is a common feature of Irish grammar where a subject and a predicate appear without a verb. This happens when a form of the verb `bí` (to be) is omitted, leaving a verbless but complete phrase. Understanding small clauses is key to recognising many natural-sounding Irish sentences, from simple descriptions to complex narrative styles.

## Definition

In Irish, a small clause is a construction that has a subject and a predicate but is missing a finite verb form. Specifically, it's a phrase where a form of the verb `bí` (to be) would normally be expected but is left out.

While less common in English (e.g., picture captions like "Me on holiday"), small clauses are a prominent and frequent feature of Irish. They can function as an alternative to subordinate clauses or infinitive constructions, and can even stand alone as complete, if elliptical, sentences.

<Details summary="Linguistic and historical notes">
  The verb `bí` is the core of the predicate in the sentences it forms. A form of `bí` (either a finite form or the verbal noun `bheith`) can be omitted under certain conditions without losing the core meaning. In fact, for some constructions, it *must* be omitted. This means that sentences with `bí` can be thought of as the verb `bí` adding finite properties (like tense) to an underlying small clause.

  The term "small clause" comes from generative grammar. There is no universally established term in traditional Irish grammar. Older descriptions might use the term `fo-rá saor` (free subordinate clause), while C. Ó Cadhlaigh called them "absolute phrases". The term `Mionchlásal` is a direct translation of the English term.
</Details>

## Form

A small clause consists of a subject followed by a predicative element (such as an adjective, a prepositional phrase, or a verbal noun).

| Type | Structure |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Positive** | Subject + Predicative |
| **Negative** | `gan` + Subject + Predicative |

### Pronoun Forms

When the subject of a small clause is a third-person pronoun, the disjunctive forms (`é`, `í`, `iad`) are used instead of the standard subject pronouns (`sé`, `sí`, `siad`).

*   *Tá sé sásta.* - He is happy. → becomes the small clause: *é sásta*
*   *Tá sí ag gáire.* - She is laughing. → becomes the small clause: *í ag gáire*

For the second person singular, both `tú` and `thú` can appear as the subject.

<Note>
An important exception occurs after verbs of motion, or verbs of starting, stopping, and continuing. If the subject of the main verb is the *same* as the subject of the small clause, the standard conjunct forms (`sé`, `sí`, `siad`) are used.

*   *Tháinig sé ag damhsa.* - He came dancing. (Here, `sé` is the subject of both `tháinig` and the implied `ag damhsa`.)
</Note>

### Pronoun Position

Simple pronouns acting as the subject of a small clause can sometimes move to the end of the clause, especially when they are also the object of the main clause.

*   *é ag gáire* → *ag gáire é*
*   *agus chuir sé sínte ar fhleasc mo dhroma síos sa díog mé* - and he laid me stretched out flat on my back down into theditch. (The small clause is `sínte ... mé`.)

However, the pronoun cannot move to the end if the small clause is introduced by a preposition (like `gan`) or a conjunction. In these cases, the subject must remain at the beginning.

### Negation with `gan`

Small clauses are negated using the preposition `gan` (without), similar to how infinitive constructions are negated.

*   *Níl sé ag damhsa.* - He is not dancing. → becomes the small clause: *gan é ag damhsa*
*   *Níl againn ach iad.* - We only have them. → becomes the small clause: *gan againn ach iad*

<Warning>
Negation with `gan` is not possible after verbs of perception (like `feic`, see) or verbs of beginning. In these cases, the main verb of the sentence must be negated instead.

*   *Ní fhaca mé Eoghan ag imeacht.* - I did not see Eoghan leave.
*   *Ní aireoidh tú uait mé.* - You will not miss me.
</Warning>

### With Phrasal Verbs

If a small clause with a pronoun subject follows a phrasal verb, the preposition from the verb combines with the pronoun to form a prepositional pronoun.

*   *Leanann sí **uirthi** ag gáire.* - She continued laughing. (This comes from `Leanann sí ar` + `í ag gáire`.)

## Usage

Small clauses appear in a wide variety of grammatical contexts.

### With Conjunctions `agus` and `ach`

They often function like subordinate clauses after the conjunctions `agus` (and) and `ach` (but).

*   *Agus é ag gáire thit sé go talamh.* - While he was laughing, he fell to the ground.
*   *Aireoidh tú uait mé is gan mé ann.* - You will miss me when I am no longer there.

A special pattern `is amhlaidh ... agus` uses a small clause to mean "namely" or "that is to say".

*   *Is amhlaidh a bhí sé agus caipín ar a cheann.* - So he was, namely with a cap on his head.

<Dialect region="Munster">
In Munster Irish, a particular construction `bí + agus + Small Clause` is used where Standard Irish would use a simple sentence.

*   *Bhí Eoghan agus eagla air.* - Eoghan was afraid. (Standard: *Bhí eagla ar Eoghan.*)

This entire construction can then become a larger small clause if the form of `bí` is omitted, for example in a cleft sentence: *Ba mhinic Eoghan agus eagla air* (Eoghan was often afraid).
</Dialect>

### After Verbs of Perception

Small clauses are common after verbs of seeing, hearing, and feeling, such as `feic` (see), `clois` (hear), and `airigh` (feel). This structure is often used where English would use a construction like "I saw him leave".

*   *Chuala mé thú ag gáire.* - I heard you laugh.
*   *Chonaic sé í ina Maighdean Mhara.* - He saw her as a mermaid.
*   *Aireoidh tú uait mé.* - You will miss me. (Literally: "You will feel me from you.")

### After Verbs of Starting, Stopping, or Continuing

*   *Thosaigh mé ag ól.* - I began to drink.
*   *Choinnigh sé ag cur ceisteanna.* - He kept asking questions.
*   *Leanann sé air ag léamh.* - He continued to read.

### After the Verb `cuir`

The verb `cuir` can use a small clause to mean "to make someone do something".

*   *Cuirfidh sé ag gáire thú.* - It will make you laugh.

### After the Verb `tarlaigh`

The verb `tarlaigh` (to happen, turn out) can be followed by a small clause.

*   *Tharla é ina dhlíodóir.* - It turned out that he was a lawyer.
*   *ó tharla ina chónaí ina aonar é* - since he lives alone.

### After Verbs of Motion

When the subject of the verb of motion is also the subject of the action in the small clause, this structure is used. It can often imply purpose.

*   *Tháinig sé ina rith.* - He came running.
*   *Tháinig sé ag foghlaim Gaeilge.* - He came to learn Irish.

### In Cleft Sentences

When an adverb or a measurement is moved to the front of a sentence for emphasis (a cleft sentence), the relative form of `bí` is often omitted, leaving a small clause.

*   *Is annamh ag gáire é.* - He rarely laughs. (Instead of: *Is annamh a bhíonn sé ag gáire.*)
*   *Ach b’fhada ina dúiseacht í.* - But she had been awake for a long time.
*   *Cén aois thú?* - How old are you? (Instead of: *Cén aois atá tú?*)
*   *Is iomaí rogha agat fós.* - You still have many options. (Instead of: *Is iomaí rogha atá agat fós.*)

A special case is the clefted form for existence, `Is ann do...` (He/she/it exists), which comes from omitting `atá` from `Tá sé ann`.

*   *Is ann dó.* - He exists.
*   *An ann do Dhia?* - Does God exist?

### As a Noun Attribute

A small clause can act as an attribute modifying a noun, similar to a relative clause.

*   *an mhí le teacht* - the coming month (the month to come)
*   *obair le déanamh* - work to be done
*   *fear ag dul aníos an bóthar* - a man going up the road

### As a Free-standing Clause

In prose and poetry, small clauses can stand alone as main clauses. This stylistic device removes the temporal aspect, creating a more static, pictorial scene.

*   *É ag breathnú amach ar an gcuan.* - He looks/looked out at the harbour.
*   *D’fhanadar tamall maith mar sin – eisean á ceistiú agus ise á fhreagairt.* - So they remained for a good while – he questioning her, and she answering him.

This is also common in headings and titles.

*   *An Chré á Cumadh* - The Earth being Formed (a chapter title from *Cré na Cille*)

### Other Contexts

*   **Optative (Wishing) Sentences:** The verb `bí` is often omitted.
    *   *Bail ó Dhia ort.* - God's blessing on you. (from *Go raibh bail...*)
*   **Conditional Clauses:** The verbal noun `bheith` can be omitted in a series of conditions.
    *   *Dá n-éalódh sé agus é ina iarrthóir, ní ligfí ar ais é.* - If he were to escape and (be) an applicant, he would not be allowed back.
*   **Depictive Adjectives:** An adjective describing the state of the subject during an action.
    *   *Tháinig sé abhaile fliuch báite.* - He came home soaking wet.
    *   *Itheann siad amh é.* - They eat it raw.

### Constructions to Avoid

<Warning>
Small clauses in Irish are formed by omitting a form of `bí`. Do not confuse this with omitting the copula `is`. While omitting the copula is common in certain sentence types, it is a different grammatical structure.

Furthermore, Irish does not use small clauses after verbs like "to call", "to name", or "to consider", where some other languages might. Instead, Irish uses a `go`/`nach` clause.

*   *Cheap mé gur óinseach a bhí inti.* - I considered her a fool.

You cannot say `*Cheap mé í ina hóinseach*`.
</Warning>
