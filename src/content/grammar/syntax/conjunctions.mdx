A conjunction (`cónasc`) is a word that connects clauses, phrases, or individual words. Irish has a rich variety of conjunctions, which can be categorised into several types based on their form and function.

In addition to single-word conjunctions, Irish frequently uses multi-word phrases, or "compound conjunctions", to express complex relationships between ideas. Adverbs are also commonly used in a similar way to link clauses.

## Types of Conjunctions

Conjunctions can be classified by their structure as either simple or compound.

### Simple Conjunctions

Simple conjunctions consist of a single word. They are the most basic building blocks for connecting ideas.

Examples include:
- `agus` (and)
- `ach` (but)
- `nó` (or)
- `go` (that)
- `ó` (since)
- `mar` (because, as)
- Conditional words: `má` (if), `dá` (if), `mura` (if not)
- Interrogative words: `cé` (who), `céard` (what), `cá` (where), `conas` (how)
- Interrogative particles: `an` (whether), `nach` (whether not)

### Compound Conjunctions

Compound conjunctions are multi-word phrases that function as a single unit. They are typically formed from a combination of other grammatical elements.

A compound conjunction often has two main parts:

**1. A first element, which can be:**
- A simple conjunction: `ach`, `nó`
- A simple preposition: `le`, `faoi`, `mar`
- A preposition and a noun (often a compound preposition): `tar éis` (after), `de bhrí` (because of), `ar shlí` (in a way)
- A noun alone: `toisc` (reason), `cionn` (reason), `fad` (length)
- A verb form: `bíodh` (let it be), `cuir i gcás` (suppose)
- An adjective: `fiú` (even), `chomh luath` (as soon)

**2. A second element, which is often:**
- The conjunction `go`/`nach` (that / that not)
- The relative particle `a`/`nach` (that / that not)
- The conditional conjunctions `dá`, `má`, `mura` (if / if not)
- The interrogative particle `an`/`nach` (whether / whether not)

An intervening `agus` (and), often shortened to `is`, frequently appears between the first and second elements. For example, `i riocht is go` (so that) is common alongside `i riocht go`.

<Note>
Adverbs and even entire subordinate clauses can be inserted between the parts of a compound conjunction, appearing before the second element like `go` or `nach`.
</Note>

## Conjunctive Adverbs

Many adverbs in Irish can function like conjunctions by creating a logical link between two clauses. These are known as conjunctive adverbs.

They are often formed from a compound preposition combined with the third-person masculine possessive pronoun `a` and a demonstrative pronoun like `sin`.

- *ina dhiaidh sin* - nevertheless (literally "after that")
- *dá bhrí sin* - therefore (literally "by reason of that")

These adverbs often have a corresponding compound conjunction that introduces the other clause in the logical relationship.

- **Conjunction**: `de bhrí go` (because)
- **Conjunctive Adverb**: `dá bhrí sin` (therefore)

- **Conjunction**: `i ndiaidh go` (although)
- **Conjunctive Adverb**: `ina dhiaidh sin` (nevertheless)

## Alternatives Using Infinitive Constructions

Instead of using a conjunction to introduce a subordinate clause, Irish often uses an infinitive construction (a phrase built around a verbal noun). In these cases, a preposition introduces the phrase instead of a conjunction.

- **Subordinate Clause**: *toisc go bhfuil sé anseo* - because he is here
- **Infinitive Construction**: *toisc é a bheith anseo* - because of him being here

- **Subordinate Clause**: *le go mbuaileann sé an fear* - so that he hits the man
- **Infinitive Construction**: *leis an fear a bhualadh* - to hit the man

These constructions are very common for expressing purpose, cause, and time relationships (before, after, while).

## Coordinating Conjunctions

The distinction between coordinating and subordinating conjunctions is less critical in Irish than in some other languages because word order does not change in subordinate clauses. However, the concepts are still useful for understanding function. Coordinating conjunctions link elements of equal grammatical rank.

## Inclusive Conjunctions

Inclusive conjunctions connect items in a list where all parts are included.

### And

The word for "and" has two main forms: `agus` and the short form `is`.

<Note>
The short form `is` is easily confused with the copula `is`. Context is key to telling them apart. Colloquially, you may also hear `'gus` or `'s`. In older texts, the Tironian note **⁊** (resembling a '7') is often used as an abbreviation for `agus`.
</Note>

- *Tá mé sásta inniu **agus** bhí mé sásta inné.* - I am happy today and was happy yesterday.
- *Pól **agus** Seán* - Paul and John
- *Pól **is** Seán* - Paul and John

#### Peculiarities of Agus

**In Enumerations**
In a list, Irish often places `agus` between *every* item (polysyndetic coordination), unlike English which typically uses "and" only before the last item.

- *Pól **agus** Seán **agus** Séamas **agus** Dóirín a bhí ann.* - Paul, John, James and Doreen were there.

However, `agus` is usually omitted between multiple adjectives describing the same noun (asyndetic coordination).

- *an cailín óg álainn* - the young, beautiful girl
- *Tá an aimsir te tirim.* - The weather is hot and dry.

When listing verbs, any verbal particle must be repeated after `agus`.

- *Deir siad gur cheannaigh **agus** gur dhíol siad na tithe.* - They say that they bought and sold the houses.

**In Negative Enumerations**
In negative lists with `gan` (without), `gan` or `ná` is used instead of `agus` or `nó`.

- *Tháinig sé **gan** ór **gan** airgead.* - He came without gold and silver.
- ***gan** bróg **ná** stoca* - without shoe or stocking

#### Further Uses of Agus

**In Positive Answers**
`Agus` can be used to add an affirmation, similar to "also" or "too".

- *An bhfuil cead agam tobac a chaitheamh? - Tá **agus** fáilte.* - May I smoke? - Yes, and you're welcome.
- *Ólfaidh mé fuisce. - Ólfaidh **agus** mise.* - I will drink whiskey. - Me too.

**With `amhlaidh`**
The phrase `amhlaidh ... agus` means "namely" or "that is to say", often introducing a small clause that describes a state.

- *Is **amhlaidh** a tháinig sé abhaile **agus** a shrón briste.* - So he came home, with his nose broken.

**With Imperatives**
`Agus` can connect two imperatives, where the second clause often expresses a result or purpose.

- *Dún an doras **agus** ná gabhadh an bhó amach.* - Close the door so that the cow doesn't go out.

**In Comparisons**
In comparisons of equality, `agus` means "as". This is common after `chomh` (so) and other expressions of equality like `ionann` (equal) or `a oiread` (as much).

- *chomh maith **agus a** rinne sé é* - as well as he did it
- *Ní h**ionann** sin **agus a** bhí sé.* - That is not as it was.
- *Níl **a leithéid** de rud **agus** taibhsí ann.* - There is no such thing as ghosts.

It also appears after words like `thar` (over), `breis` (more), and `tuilleadh` (more) to mean "than".

- *breis **agus** céad bliain ó shin* - more than a hundred years ago

**With Infinitive Constructions**
Instead of connecting two similar full clauses, Irish often replaces the second clause with a verbal noun construction after `agus`. This avoids repetition.

- *Chuamar ann go bhfeicfimis é **agus** éisteacht leis.* - We went there so that we could see him and listen to him. (Instead of: *...agus go n-éistfimis leis*)

**For Circumstantial Description**
When `agus` is followed by an infinitive construction or a "small clause" (a clause where a form of `bí` is omitted), it describes a surrounding circumstance. The meaning can be temporal (while), causal (because), concessive (although), or conditional (if), depending on the context.

- *D’imigh tú **agus** fearg ort.* - You left in anger. (Literally: "You left and anger on you.")
- ***Agus** é ag gáire, thit sé go talamh.* - While he was laughing, he fell to the ground.
- *D’fhág sé an teach **agus** gan muid ach ag toiseacht.* - He left the house, although we had barely started.

<Details summary="Linguistic note">
This use of `agus` to introduce a circumstantial clause is structurally similar to a feature in Semitic languages, such as the *wāw al-ḥāl* ("the 'and' of the circumstance") in Arabic.
</Details>

### Both ... and ...

| Irish Phrase | Literal Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| `… chomh maith le …` | "… as good with …" |
| `idir … agus …` | "between … and …" |
| `ní hamháin … ach … freisin` | "not only … but … also" |

- *Bhí Pól **chomh maith le** Seán sásta.* - Both Paul and Seán were satisfied.
- ***Idir** mhná **agus** fhir a bhí sásta.* - Both men and women were satisfied.

### Nor

The conjunction `ná` is used in negative contexts where English might use "and" or "or". It appears after negative particles like `ní` and prepositions like `gan` (without).

- *Ní maith liom tae **ná** caife.* - I don't like tea or coffee.
- *Tháinig sé gan bróg **ná** stoca.* - He came without shoe or stocking.

In negative answers, `ná` adds emphasis or another negative item.

- *An raibh Pól ann? - Ní raibh, **ná** Seán.* - Was Paul there? - No, and neither was John.

<Warning>
**Four types of `ná`**

Learners must be careful to distinguish between four different words spelled `ná`:
1.  The conjunction `ná` meaning "nor" or "or" in negative contexts.
2.  The conjunction `ná` meaning "than" in comparisons (e.g., `níos fearr ná` - better than).
3.  The negative verbal particle `ná` used with imperatives (e.g., `Ná déan sin!` - Don't do that!).
4.  A particle used in Munster Irish in cleft sentences and instead of `nach`.
</Warning>

### Neither ... nor ...

| Irish Phrase | Literal Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| `ní … ná …` | "not … nor …" |
| `ní … agus ní …` | "not … and not …" |
| `ní … ná ní …` | "not … nor not …" |

* **Ní** raibh Pól **ná** Seán sásta.* - Neither Paul nor Seán was satisfied.
* **Ní** íosfaidh mé **agus ní** ólfaidh mé.* - I will not eat and I will not drink.

### And also not / and even less

To emphatically negate a second clause, you can use `agus ní mó` (and also not) or `agus is lú` (and even less).

- *Ní raibh sé anseo **agus ní mó** a bhí sé ansin.* - He was not here and he was not there either.
- *Ní raibh sé sásta **agus is lú** a bhí mé sásta.* - He was not satisfied, and I was even less satisfied.

### On the one hand … on the other hand …

| Irish Phrase | Literal Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| `ar láimh amháin … ar an láimh eile …` | "on one hand … on the other hand …" |

* **Ar láimh amháin de** bhí sé leisciúil, **ar an láimh eile de** rinne sé an obair go maith.* - On the one hand he was lazy, on the other hand he did the work well.

### Furthermore, moreover

These are typically conjunctive adverbs:
- `chomh maith leis sin`
- `ina theannta sin`
- `ar a bharr sin`
- `thairis sin`

### Namely, that is

- `is é sin (le rá)` (that is (to say))
- `mar atá` (as is)
- `eadhon` (abbreviated as `.i.`)

<Note>
The word `eadhon` is now rare in speech and mostly found in writing. It is usually read aloud as `is é sin`.
</Note>

## Exclusive Conjunctions

Exclusive conjunctions present a choice or contrast, where one part of the statement excludes another.

### Or

The main word for "or" is `nó`.

<Dialect region="Connacht, Munster">
`Nó` is commonly pronounced /nuː/, as if spelled `nú`.
</Dialect>

- *An raibh Pól **nó** Seán sásta inniu?* - Was Paul or Seán happy today?

#### Further Uses of Nó

**With a Negation**
The combination `ní ... nó` can have a strong affirmative meaning, effectively reversing the negation.

* **Ní** chreidim **nó** tharla sé.* - I am convinced that it happened. (Literally: "I don't believe or it happened," implying "I don't believe that it *didn't* happen.")
* **Ní** foláir **nó** tá aithne agat air.* - You must know him.

**With `cuma`**
`Cuma` (it doesn't matter, same) is followed by `nó` in the sense of "as".

- *Is cuma **nó** bás é.* - It is the same as death.

**As "for"**
Occasionally, `nó` can be used as a causal conjunction meaning "for".

- *...**nó** is léir nach bhfuil sé furasta.* - ...for it is obvious that it is not easy.

### Either ... or ...

The phrase `sin nó` is used to express "either ... or ...".

- *Bhí Pól sásta, **sin nó** bhí Seán sásta.* - Either Paul was satisfied, or Seán was satisfied.

### Otherwise

After an imperative, `nó` means "otherwise".

- *Glaoigh air **nó** codlóidh sé go tráthnóna.* - Call him, otherwise he'll sleep until evening.

Other phrases for "otherwise" include `nó neachtar acu` and `nó ceann éigin`.

## Contrastive Conjunctions

These conjunctions introduce an idea that contrasts with the preceding one.

### But, however, rather

The main word for "but" is `ach`.

- *Tá mé sásta inniu **ach** ní raibh mé sásta inné.* - I am happy today, but I was not happy yesterday.

Other words like `áfach` (however) and `mar sin féin` (nevertheless) also express contrast.

#### Further Uses of Ach

**In Negative Answers**
`Ach` introduces a correction or alternative in a negative answer.

- *An dochtúir é? - Ní hea, **ach** múinteoir.* - Is he a doctor? - No, but a teacher.

**Meaning "except"**
In negative sentences, `ach` often means "except".

- *Ní raibh aon duine ar bord **ach** cailín óg.* - There was no one on board except a young girl.

**Meaning "if"**
`Ach` can mean "if" when it connects a main clause in the conditional with a subordinate clause in the past indicative.

- *D’imeoinn cheana **ach** ní raibh mé réidh.* - I would have left already if I hadn't been ready.

**With Infinitive Constructions**
Like `agus`, `ach` can introduce an infinitive construction or small clause. It often has a restrictive or conditional meaning ("provided that", "if") or a temporal one ("as soon as", "when").

- *Gheobhaidh tú é **ach** íoc as.* - You'll get it, provided you pay for it.
- *Bhí sé sásta **ach** an leithscéal sin a ghabháil leis.* - He was satisfied as soon as he received that apology.

**For Reinforcement**
`Ach` can be used to reinforce a statement, similar to a modal particle.

- *Céard eile a chonaic sí ansin **ach** capall?* - What else did she see there but a horse?

### Only

In a semi-negative sentence (`ní ... ach`), the structure means "only".

* **Ní** raibh mé sásta **ach** inné.* - I was only satisfied yesterday. (Literally: "I was not satisfied but yesterday.")

### Not only … but also …

The phrase is `ní hamháin ... ach ...`.

* **Ní hamháin go** bhfuil mé sásta inniu **ach** bhí mé sásta inné freisin.* - I am not only happy today, but I was also happy yesterday.

### Except

| Irish Phrase | Literal Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| `ach amháin (go)` | "but only (that)" |
| `cé is moite de/go` | "although is ... of/that" |
| `seachas` | "besides" |

- *Níor leagadh aon chúram eile ormsa **ach amháin** fanacht leis.* - No other task was imposed on me except to wait for him.
- *daoine **seachas** eisean* - people other than him

## Subordinating Conjunctions

Subordinating conjunctions introduce a subordinate clause, which depends on a main clause to form a complete sentence.

## Complementary Conjunctions

These conjunctions introduce a clause that functions as the subject or object of the main verb, completing its meaning.

### That / that not

The most important complementary conjunction is `go` (that) and its negative form `nach` (that not).

| | Present Tense | Mutation | Past Tense | Mutation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Positive** | `go` | Eclipsis | `gur` | Lenition |
| **Negative** | `nach` | Eclipsis | `nár` | Lenition |

- *Deir sé **go** bhfuil deifir air.* - He says that he is in a hurry.
- *Dúirt sí **nár** fhoghlaim sí a cuid.* - She said that she had not learned her part.

<Dialect region="Munster">
In Munster, `ná` is often used instead of `nach`. After a negative main clause, `ná go` can be used.
- *Deir sé **ná** fuil sí ann.* - He says that she is not there.
</Dialect>

## Circumstantial Conjunctions

These conjunctions describe the circumstances surrounding the main action, without specifying a strict causal or concessive link.

### In view of the fact that (considering that)

| Positive | Negative | Literal Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `agus go` | `agus nach` | "and that" |
| `tar éis go` | `tar éis nach` | "after that" |
| `i ndiaidh go` | `i ndiaidh nach` | "after that" |
| `nuair a` | `nuair nach` | "when" |

- *Ní haon ionadh sin **agus gurb** eisean do mhac.* - That is no wonder, considering that he is your son.
- *Ba cheart duit a bheith amuigh **agus** an lá chomh breá sin.* - You should be outside, considering the day is so fine.

## Causal Conjunctions

Causal conjunctions introduce a clause that gives the reason for the main clause's action.

### Because

Irish has a very wide variety of ways to say "because".

| Positive | Negative | Literal Meaning | Dialect |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `mar` | `mar ní/nach` | "as" | Standard |
| `ó` | `ó nach` | "since" | Standard |
| `de bhrí (is) go` | `de bhrí (is) nach` | "from strength (and) that" | Standard |
| `toisc go` | `toisc nach` | "purpose, that" | Munster |
| `faoi (is) go` | `faoi (is) nach` | "under (and) that" | Connacht |
| `(ar/as) siocair go` | `(ar/as) siocair nach` | "(on/from) reason, that" | Ulster |
| `ós rud é go` | `ós rud é nach` | "since it is a thing, that" | Munster |
| `de bharr go` | `de bharr nach` | "from top, that" | Connacht |
| `mar gheall ar go` | `mar gheall ar nach` | "as pledge on, that" | Connacht |

- *Bhí fearg air **faoi go** raibh siad ag déanamh gleo.* - He was angry because they were making noise.
- *Ní dheachaigh mé go hÉirinn **de bharr go** raibh mé tinn.* - I did not go to Ireland because I was ill.

### Since / for

Conjunctions like `mar`, `óir`, and `arae` function like "for" or "since", introducing a reason. They are typically followed by a main clause structure, not a `go`-clause (except in indirect speech).

- *Bhí faitíos roimpi **mar** bhí sí an-ard.* - They were afraid of her, for she was very tall.
- ***Ós** í an Ghaeilge an teanga náisiúnta...* - Since Irish is the national language...

<Warning>
The word `mar` has several meanings depending on the clause that follows:
- `mar tá sé` - **because** he is (followed by a verb)
- `mar a bhfuil sé` - **where** he is (followed by an indirect relative clause)
- `mar atá sé` - **as** he is (followed by a direct relative clause)
</Warning>

### Therefore / for this reason

These are conjunctive adverbs that introduce a result.

| Irish Phrase | Literal Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| `dá bhrí sin` | "from that strength" |
| `mar sin` | "as that" |
| `ar an ábhar sin` | "on that cause" |

- *Déanaim smaoineamh, **dá bhrí sin** táim ann.* - I think, therefore I am.

## Concessive Conjunctions

Concessive conjunctions introduce a circumstance that hinders the main action but does not prevent it.

### Although / even if

| Positive | Negative | Literal Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `cé go` | `cé nach` | "who, that" |
| `(d’)ainneoin go` | `(d’)ainneoin nach` | "in spite of, that" |
| `i ndiaidh go` | `i ndiaidh nach` | "after that" |
| `bíodh (is) go` | `bíodh (is) nach` | "be [it] (and) that" |
| `má (... féin)` | `mura (... féin)` | "if (... self)" |

<Dialect region="Ulster">
The forms `gidh` or `gí` are often used instead of `cé`.
</Dialect>

- *Ní chreidim é **cé go** bhfeicim é.* - I don't believe it, although I see it.
- ***Má** tá sé bocht **féin**, tá sé fial.* - Although he is poor, he is generous.
- *Dúirt sí liom gan dul amach **dá** mbeadh an lá te **féin**.* - She told me not to go out, even if the day was warm.

### Nevertheless / All the same

These are conjunctive adverbs that express a contrast despite a previously mentioned fact.

| Irish Phrase | Literal Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| `ina dhiaidh sin (féin)` | "after that (itself)" |
| `mar sin féin` | "like that itself" |
| `ar a shon sin` | "on its behalf that" |
| `dá ainneoin sin` | "despite that" |

- *Níor cheap mé go dtiocfadh sé; **ina dhiaidh sin féin**, tháinig.* - I didn't think he would come; nevertheless, he came.

## Final Conjunctions

Final (or purposive) conjunctions introduce a clause that expresses the purpose or goal of the main action.

### In order that / To

| Positive | Negative | Literal Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `le go` | `le nach` | "with that" |
| `chun go` | `chun nach` | "to that" |
| `ionas go` | `ionas nach` | "manner, that" |
| `i dtreo (is) go` | `i dtreo (is) nach` | "in direction (and) that" |
| `ar eagla go` | - | "for fear that" (lest) |
| - | `sula` | "before" (so as not to) |

The verb in a final clause is typically in the future or conditional. The subjunctive can also be used.

- *Déan deifir **le go** mbeidh tú réidh in am.* - Hurry up so that you will be ready in time.
- *Chuaigh sé abhaile **sula** bhfeictí é.* - He went home so as not to be seen.
- *Ná tugaigí breith **ar eagla go** dtabharfaí breith oraibh.* - Judge not, lest ye be judged.

## Consecutive Conjunctions

Consecutive conjunctions introduce a clause that expresses the result or consequence of the main action.

### So that

| Positive | Negative | Literal Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `go` | `nach` | "that" |
| `agus go` | `agus nach` | "and that" |
| `i riocht is go` | `i riocht is nach` | "in form and that" |

These are often used after expressions like `chomh ... sin` (so ... that).

- *Bhí mé **chomh** tuirseach **sin gur** thit mé i mo chodladh.* - I was so tired that I fell asleep.
- *Chodail mé rófhada **i riocht is go** raibh mé déanach.* - I overslept, so that I was late.

## Restrictive Conjunctions

These conjunctions introduce a condition or limitation on the main clause.

### Provided that / On condition that

| Positive | Negative | Literal Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `ach go` | `ach nach` | "but, that" |
| `ar choinníoll go` | `ar choinníoll nach` | "on condition, that" |
| `ar chuntar go` | `ar chuntar nach` | "on condition, that" |

- *Tig leat é a bhriseadh **ar chuntar go** n-íocfaidh tú as.* - You may break it, on condition that you pay for it.

### As far as / As long as

| Irish Phrase | Clause Type | Literal Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `fad a` | direct relative | "extent, which" |
| `chomh fada is a` | direct relative | "so far, and which" |
| `sa mhéid (is) go` | `go`/`nach`-clause | "in the extent (and) that" |

- *Níl aon eagla orm roimhe **fad atá** mo ghunna liom.* - I am not afraid of him, as long as I have my gun with me.

### Supposing that

| Positive | Negative | Literal Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `cuir i gcás go` | `cuir i gcás nach` | "put in case, that" |
| `abair (is) go` | `abair (is) nach` | "say, (and) that" |

* **cuir i gcás go** mbeadh sé ann* - supposing he were there
* **abair go** bhfuil an ceart agat* - supposing you are right

## Conditional Conjunctions

Conditional conjunctions introduce a condition (protasis) upon which the main clause (apodosis) depends. Irish distinguishes between real (possible) and unreal (hypothetical) conditions.

### If / In case

| Condition | Positive | Mutation | Negative | Mutation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Real** | `má` | Lenition | `mura` | Eclipsis |
| **Unreal** | `dá` | Eclipsis | `mura` | Eclipsis |

* **Má** bhíonn sé sásta, tiocfaidh na fir.* - If he is satisfied, the men will come. (Real)
* **Dá** mbeadh lá maith ann, dhéanfainn é.* - If it were a nice day, I would do it. (Unreal)
- *Tiocfaidh fearg uirthi **mura** ndéanfar di é.* - She will be angry if it is not done for her. (Real)
* **Mura** rachainn leis, ní fheicfinn í.* - If I had not gone with him, I would not have seen her. (Unreal)

<Details summary="Related words and etymology of `dá`">
The conditional `dá` (if) should not be confused with several other words spelled the same way:
- `dá` (from `de` + `a`): "of his/her/its" or "to his/her/its"
- `dá` (from `de` + relative `a`): "of which" or "to which"
- `dá`: the number "two" (usually lenited as `dhá`)

Etymologically, the conditional `dá` also derives from the preposition `de` plus a relative particle.
</Details>

### If it were not the case that / Unless

The most common way to express this is with `murach`. It describes the consequences if a real, existing condition were absent.

- *Ní chreidfinn é **murach go** bhfaca mé féin é.* - I would not believe it if I had not seen it myself.
* **Murach** an balla, thitfeadh an teach.* - If it weren't for the wall, the house would fall.

## Conjunctions in Indirect Questions

These conjunctions introduce indirect or reported questions.

### Whether

The verbal particles `an` (positive) and `nach` (negative) are used.

| | Present Tense | Past Tense |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Positive** | `an` | `ar` |
| **Negative** | `nach` | `nár` |

- *Níl mé cinnte **an** bhfuil mé sásta.* - I am not sure whether I am satisfied.
- *Ní mé **ar** chuala sé é.* - I wonder whether he heard it.

<Dialect region="Connacht">
The conditional conjunction `má` (if) is sometimes used to mean "whether".
- *Níl a fhios agam **má** tá Gaeilge aige.* - I don't know whether he speaks Irish.
</Dialect>

### Whether … or (not)

| Irish Phrase | Structure |
| :--- | :--- |
| `an … nó nach …` | Interrogative particle |
| `cé acu a … nó nach …` | Direct relative clause |

- *Níl a fhios agam **an** bhfuil mé sásta **nó nach** bhfuil.* - I don't know whether I am satisfied or not.
- *Abair liom **cé acu a** bheidh tú ann **nó nach** mbeidh.* - Tell me whether you will be there or not.

## Conjunctions of Indifference

These conjunctions express that the outcome of the main clause is unaffected by the alternatives presented in the subordinate clause.

### Whether … or (not) / Regardless of whether

| Irish Phrase | Structure |
| :--- | :--- |
| `pé acu a … nó nach …` | Direct relative |
| `cibé acu a … nó nach …` | Direct relative |
| `bíodh … nó ná bíodh` | Imperative |
| `má … nó mura …` | Conditional |

- *Is cuma liom **pé acu atá** tú sásta **nó nach** bhfuil.* - I don't care whether you are satisfied or not.
- *Beimid ann, **tagadh sé nó ná tagadh**.* - We will be there, whether he comes or not.
* **Más** maith olc leat é, caithfidh tú é a dhéanamh.* - Whether you like it or not, you have to do it.

## Substitutive Conjunctions

These conjunctions introduce an action that takes the place of another.

### Instead of

The phrases `in áit`, `in ionad`, and `i leaba` are used. They are prepositions that introduce an infinitive construction, not a full subordinate clause.

- *Thuas a bhí sé, **in áit a bheith** farainne.* - He was upstairs, instead of being with us.
* **In ionad a dhul** ag obair, chuaigh sé go dtí an pub.* - Instead of going to work, he went to the pub.

## Comparative Conjunctions

These conjunctions introduce a clause of comparison.

### As if

| Irish Phrase | Clause Type |
| :--- | :--- |
| `ar nós dá` | Unreal conditional |
| `amhail is dá` | Unreal conditional |
| `mar a` | Direct relative |
| `faoi mar a` | Direct relative |

- *Chorraigh sí **ar nós dá** mbeadh sí ag iarraidh éirí.* - She moved as if she were trying to get up.
- *Lean sé den obair **faoi mar nach** gcloisfeadh sé mé.* - He continued the work as if he couldn't hear me.

### (Just) as

| Irish Phrase | Clause Type |
| :--- | :--- |
| `mar a` | Direct relative |
| `faoi mar a` | Direct relative |
| `de réir mar a` | Direct relative |
| `agus a` | Direct relative |

- *D’fhág sí é **sa chaoi a** raibh sé.* - She left it as it was.
* **De réir mar a** thuigim…* - As I understand it…

### As … as / so … as

This comparison of equality is formed with `chomh` + adjective.

| Structure | Example |
| :--- | :--- |
| `chomh` + Adj + `le` + Noun | *Tá sé **chomh hard le** an teach.* (He is as tall as the house.) |
| `chomh` + Adj + `agus a` + Clause | *Tá sé **chomh hard agus a** shíl mé.* (He is as tall as I thought.) |

### (Greater) than

This comparison of inequality is formed with `níos` + adjective + `ná`.

| Structure | Example |
| :--- | :--- |
| `níos` + Adj + `ná` + Noun | *Tá sé **níos airde ná** an teach.* (He is taller than the house.) |
| `níos` + Adj + `ná (mar) a` + Clause | *Tá sé **níos airde ná mar a** shíl mé.* (He is taller than I thought.) |

## Temporal Conjunctions

Temporal conjunctions introduce a clause that specifies the time of the main action.

### Until

| Irish Phrase | Literal Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| `go dtí go` | "until comes, that" |
| `nó go` | "or that" |
| `go` | "that" |

- *Fan **nó go** dtiocfaidh sé.* - Wait until he comes.
- *Ní thiocfaidh sé **go** bhfaighidh sé cuireadh.* - He will not come until he gets an invitation.

### Since / Ever since

The preposition `ó` is used as a conjunction, followed by a direct relative clause.

- *Ní fhaca mé iad **ó chuala** mé an scéal.* - I have not seen them since I heard the news.

### As soon as

| Irish Phrase | Clause Type |
| :--- | :--- |
| `ach a/ar` | Indirect relative |
| `chomh luath is a` | Direct relative |
| `a luaithe a` | Direct relative |

- *Glaofaidh sí **chomh luath is a** thiocfaidh sí abhaile.* - She will call as soon as she gets home.
- *Inseoidh mé dó é **ach a bhfeicfidh** mé é.* - I will tell him as soon as I see him.

### Scarcely … when

This is expressed with the structure `ní túisce ... ná ...` (no sooner ... than ...).

* **Ní túisce** a bhí an focal ráite aige **ná** rith sé.* - Scarcely had he spoken the word when he ran.

### Before

The main conjunction is `sula` (present/future) and `sular` (past).

- *Phéinteáil sé an doras **sular** tháinig Pádraig.* - He painted the door before Pádraig came.
- *Beidh sé anseo **sula** dtiocfaidh mé ar ais.* - He will be here before I come back.

### While, as

| Irish Phrase | Structure |
| :--- | :--- |
| `fad a` | Direct relative clause |
| `agus` | Small clause |
| `le linn` | Infinitive construction |

- *Bhí sé ag caoineadh **fad a** bhí sé ag caint liom.* - He was crying while he was talking to me.
* **Agus é ag gáire**, thit sé go talamh.* - While he was laughing, he fell to the ground.

### After

There is no single conjunction for "after". Instead, Irish uses infinitive constructions with prepositions like `tar éis` or `i ndiaidh`.

- *Tiocfaidh an bhean **tar éis imeacht don** fhear.* - The woman will come after the man has gone.

Alternatively, `nuair` (when) is used with a perfect tense.

- *Tiocfaidh an bhean **nuair a bheas** an fear imithe.* - The woman will come when the man has gone.

### When / As

The most common word is `nuair`, which is followed by a direct relative clause.

- ***Nuair a** bhí mé óg, bhí mé i mo chónaí i nDún na nGall.* - When I was young, I lived in Donegal.

## Local Conjunctions

Local conjunctions introduce a clause specifying a location.

### Where

| Irish Phrase | Clause Type |
| :--- | :--- |
| `mar a/ar` | Indirect relative |
| `(an) áit a/ar` | Indirect relative |
| `cá/cár` | Indirect relative (in questions) |

- *Fan **san áit a** bhfuil tú.* - Stay where you are.
- *Lorg iad **mar ar** chuir tú iad.* - Look for them where you put them.
- *Níl a fhios agam **cá** bhfuil tú.* - I don't know where you are.
