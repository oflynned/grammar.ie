## Predicate and Subject in Copula Sentences

In any sentence, the **predicate** (`faisnéis`) is the core statement being made. In a standard sentence with a verb like `rith` (to run) or `léigh` (to read), the verb itself forms the predicate.

However, in sentences that identify or classify something, the verb "to be" (in Irish, the copula `is`) does not form the predicate alone. Instead, it connects the subject to the actual statement.

-   **Identification:** *He is Paul.* The predicate is "Paul".
-   **Classification:** *He is a student.* The predicate is "a student".
-   **Describing a quality:** *He is tall.* The predicate is "tall".

In these examples, the predicate is a noun or an adjective that tells us *what* or *how* the subject is.

### Word Order: Predicate-Subject-Object (PSO)

While English typically follows a Subject-Verb-Object (SVO) word order, Irish uses a Verb-Subject-Object (VSO) pattern. In copula sentences, this translates to a **Predicate-Subject-Object (PSO)** order, meaning the predicate usually comes first, followed by the subject.

-   *Is múinteoir é.* (He is a teacher.)
    -   Copula: `Is`
    -   Predicate (P): `múinteoir` (a teacher)
    -   Subject (S): `é` (he)

## Deviations from the Standard Word Order

In certain types of copula sentences, particularly those involving identification, the standard "Copula - Predicate - Subject" word order can be altered.

### Pronouns in the First and Second Person

The personal pronouns `mé`, `tú`, `sinn`, `muid`, and `sibh` always appear immediately after the copula, regardless of whether they are the grammatical subject or predicate.

-   *Is mé an fear.* (I am the man.)

In this sentence, `mé` is the subject, even though it is in the position normally occupied by the predicate. A construction like `*Is é an fear mé*` is not standard.

To avoid this ambiguity, affirmative sentences often make the pronoun the predicate by using its emphatic form. This restores the standard PSO word order.

-   *Is mise an fear.* (I am the man. / lit: The man is *I*.)
    -   Predicate: `mise`
    -   Subject: `an fear`

This creates a clear distinction:
-   An **unstressed** pronoun (`mé`, `tú`) after the copula is the **subject**.
-   An **emphatic** pronoun (`mise`, `tusa`) after the copula is the **predicate**.

### Proper Nouns and Demonstratives

Proper nouns and nouns modified by a demonstrative adjective (`sin`, `seo`) often appear as the subject. When they do, they are separated from the copula by a third-person pronoun (`é`, `í`, `iad`) which acts as a placeholder or "secondary predicate". This formally maintains the PSO structure.

-   *Is é Pól an múinteoir.* (Paul is the teacher.)
    -   Subject: `Pól`
    -   Predicate: `an múinteoir`
-   *Is é an fear sin an dalta.* (That man is the student.)
    -   Subject: `an fear sin`
    -   Predicate: `an dalta`

Substantive demonstrative pronouns (`é sin`, `iad seo`) also function as the subject in this position.

-   *Is é sin an dalta.* (That one is the student.)
-   *Is iad seo na múinteoirí.* (These are the teachers.)

These sentences often appear in a shortened form where the demonstrative combines with the copula.

-   *Sin é an dalta.* (That is the student.)
-   *Seo iad na múinteoirí.* (These are the teachers.)

### Emphasising the Predicate

While unusual, it is possible to place a proper noun or demonstrative subject at the end of the sentence. This is done to place strong emphasis on the predicate, suggesting it is unique or particularly significant.

-   *Is é an múinteoir Pól.* (Paul is *the* teacher.)
-   *Is é an dalta an fear sin.* (That man is *the* student.)

### Sentences with Two Nouns

**Identification Sentences**
When an identification sentence contains two definite nouns, it can be ambiguous which is the subject and which is the predicate.

-   *Is é an dalta an fear.*

This could mean either "The man is the student" or "The student is the man". The meaning is usually determined by context or intonation.

-   **Context:** If answering the question *Cé hé an fear?* (Who is the man?), the answer *Is é an dalta an fear* means "The man is *the student*." (`an dalta` is the predicate).
-   **Intonation:** When the subject comes first, speakers often insert a slight pause before the predicate: *Is é an dalta: an fear.* (The man is *the student*.)

**Classification Sentences**
In rare cases, the subject and predicate can be swapped in classification sentences if both are indefinite nouns. This is most common in proverbs.

-   *Tír gan teanga, tír gan anam.* (A country without a language is a country without a soul.)
    -   Predicate: `tír gan anam`
    -   Subject: `Tír gan teanga`

### Short Forms with Demonstratives

In classification sentences, the demonstrative pronoun often appears at the end in the standard form.

-   *Is amhrán é sin.* (That is a song.)
-   *Is fear é seo.* (This is a man.)

However, short forms where the demonstrative subject begins the sentence are very common.

-   *Sin amhrán.* (That is a song.)
-   *Seo fear.* (This is a man.)

## Grammatical Interpretations

The analysis above reflects the common understanding in modern grammars, where the subject is the "theme" (known information) and the predicate is the "rheme" (new information). This view accepts the word order variations described.

An alternative theory, notably from O'Nolan's *New Era Grammar of Modern Irish*, proposes that the element immediately following the copula is **always** the predicate. In this view, a subject can never directly follow the copula unless separated by a secondary predicate (`é`, `í`, `iad`).

According to this interpretation:
-   In *Is mé an fear*, the pronoun `mé` would be the predicate, regardless of its non-emphatic form.
-   The PSO rule would apply almost without exception.

Historically, the structure *Is mé an fear* may have evolved from Old Irish. The modern copula `is` is an impersonal form meaning "it is". Therefore, an Old Irish sentence like *Is mé in fer* could be read as "It is I, the man," where `mé` is the predicate and `in fer` is an apposition (a noun phrase that renames another noun beside it). This historical context helps explain why `mé` appears directly after the copula in the modern language.
