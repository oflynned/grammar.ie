Interrogative words, or question words, are used to ask for specific information, such as "who", "what", "where", and "why". In Irish, these words have a distinct structure and syntax that is important for learners to understand.

While they can be classified as pronouns (`cé` - who), adverbs (`cá` - where), or adjectives (`cén` - which), this distinction is often blurred. Many interrogative adverbs are formed from pronouns, and many interrogative words can function in multiple roles. Syntactically, they all behave in a similar way.

A common feature of Irish question words is that they almost all begin with the letter "c", such as `cé`, `cá`, and `cad`.

<Details summary="Historical note">
  The "c" in Irish question words originates from the same Indo-European root "kw-" that gives us "qu-" in Latin (`quod`), "wh-" in English (`what`), and "w-" in German (`was`). The Irish word `cad` (what) is a direct cognate of these examples.
</Details>

## Syntax of Question Words

The syntax of Irish question words is fundamentally different from English. They contain an implied form of the copula (`is`), meaning a word like `cé` functions as a complete clause: "who is". This makes the entire question a copula sentence where the question word is the predicate.

If the question only involves the copula, the rest of the sentence is the subject.

*   *Cé hé sin?* - Who is that?
*   *Cá haois dó?* - How old is he? (Literally: "What age to-him?")

If the question involves another verb (e.g., "Who *says* that?"), the subject of the copula sentence is a relative clause.

*   *Cé a deir sin?* - Who says that?
    (Literally: "Who-is [the one] who says that?")
*   *Cad a rinne sé?* - What did he do?
    (Literally: "What-is [the thing] that he did?")
*   *Tuige ar tháinig tú annseo?* - Why did you come here?

This structure is a core concept in Irish grammar and is essential for forming questions correctly.

## Usage in Indirect Questions

Interrogative words are also used to introduce indirect questions. These are subordinate clauses that follow verbs of asking, knowing, thinking, or expressions of uncertainty.

*   *Níl a fhios agam cé hé an fear seo.* - I don't know who this man is.
*   *Smaoinigh mé roimh ré ar céard a déarfainn.* - I thought beforehand about what I would say.
*   *Ní raibh sé cinnte cad a déarfadh sé.* - He wasn't sure what he would say.
*   *...féachaint cad a bhí le rá ag an údar.* - ...to see what the author had to say.

## A Common Pitfall

<Warning>
In English, question words like "what" and "who" can also function as relative pronouns ("I know what you did"). This is not the case in Irish. You cannot use interrogative words like `cad` in this way. Instead, you must use phrases like `an rud a` (the thing that) or the general relative pronoun `a`.

| Avoid | Prefer |
| :--- | :--- |
| *… cad a bhí uaidh* | *… a raibh uaidh* |
| *… cad a bhí tuillte acu* | *… an rud a bhí tuillte acu* |

- *Thug mé dó a raibh uaidh.* - I gave him (everything) he needed.
- *Thug sé dóibh an rud a bhí tuillte acu.* - He gave them what they deserved.
</Warning>

## Usage in Exclamations

Irish question words can also be used in exclamatory sentences to express surprise or emphasis, much like "What a...!" or "How...!" in English.

*   *Cad é mar thubaiste!* - What a disaster!
*   *Cad é mar atá an fear láidir ar lár!* - How astonishing that the strong man is down!
*   *Cad é a mhinice …* - How often…!
*   *Cad a bhfuil agam á fhulaingt laistigh!* - What I have to suffer inside!

The main Irish interrogative words are detailed on the following pages:
*   `cé` (who, what, which)
*   `cá` (where, which, how)
*   `cad` (what)
*   `céard` (what)
*   `conas` (how)
*   `cathain` (when)
*   `an` (in certain "how" questions)
