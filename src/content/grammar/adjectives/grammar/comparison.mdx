## Degrees of Comparison

Irish has several ways to compare adjectives, known as the degrees of comparison (`céimeanna na comparáide`).

-   **Positive (`an bhunchéim`)**: This is the basic form of the adjective found in the dictionary. It describes a quality without comparison.
-   **Comparative (`an bhreischéim`)**: This form expresses "more" (e.g., `bigger`, `faster`). Crucially, the same form is used for the **superlative** (`an sárchéim`), meaning "most" (e.g., `biggest`, `fastest`). Irish does not have a separate superlative form for adjectives.

The distinction often made between a comparative with `níos` and a "superlative" with `is` is an oversimplification. The form with `níos` is always comparative, but the form with `is` can be either comparative or superlative depending on the context.

> **Historical Note:** Old Irish had a distinct superlative ending (`-em`/`-am`), but this fell out of use. The modern system relies entirely on the comparative form and syntactic context to express superlative meaning.

## Forming the Comparative

The comparative form of an adjective is always used with a part of the copula, such as `is`, `ba`, or `níos`. The regular comparative form is identical to the **genitive singular feminine** form of the adjective.

### Regular Formation

**First Declension**

-   Adjectives ending in a consonant are slenderised and add `-e`.
    -   `ban` → `baine` (female)
    -   `glic` → `glice` (clever)
    -   `ramhar` → `raimhre` (fat)
-   Adjectives ending in `-ach`/`-each` take the ending `-aí`/`-í`.
    -   `aisteach` → `aistí` (strange)
    -   `salach` → `salaí` (dirty)
-   Adjectives in `-mhar`/`-far` become `-mhaire`/`-faire` in the standard language, but often `-mhara`/`-fara` in Ulster.
    -   `greannmhar` → `greannmhaire` (funny)

**Second Declension**

-   Adjectives ending in `-úil` are made broad and take the ending `-a`, becoming `-úla`.
    -   `spéisiúil` → `spéisiúla` (interesting)
-   Adjectives ending in `-ir` are made broad, syncopated if polysyllabic, and take the ending `-a`, becoming `-ra`.
    -   `deacair` → `deacra` (difficult)
    -   `cóir` → `córa` (fair, just)

**Third Declension**

-   In the standard language, adjectives ending in a vowel do not change.
    -   `dorcha` → `dorcha` (dark)
    -   `beo` → `beo` (alive)
    -   An exception is `nua` → `nuaí` (new).
-   **Dialectal Forms:** In Connacht and Ulster, monosyllabic adjectives ending in a long vowel often add `-cha` or `-chte`. In Munster, they add `-tha`/`-the`.
    -   `beo` → `beocha`/`beoichte`/`beotha`
    -   `breá` → `breácha`/`breáichte`/`breátha`
    -   This also applies to some monosyllabic adjectives ending in a silent `-bh`, like `tiubh` (`tiúcha`, standard `tibhe`) and `dubh` (`dúcha`, standard `duibhe`).

### Irregular Formation

A few common adjectives have irregular comparative forms.

| Positive | Comparative | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `mór` | `mó` | bigger, more |
| `beag` | `lú` | smaller, less |
| `maith` | `fearr` | better |
| `dona` | `measa` | worse |
| `olc` | `measa` | more evil |
| `fada` | `faide`, `sia` | longer |
| `breá` | `breátha` | more beautiful |
| `te` | `teo` | hotter |
| `furasta` | `fusa` | easier |
| `iomaí` | `lia` | more |
| `fogas` | `foisce` | nearer |
| `ionúin` | `ansa`, `ionúine` | dearer |
| `dócha` | `dóichí` | more probable |
| `tréan` | `treise`, `tréine` | stronger |
| `tirim` | `tirime`, `tiorma` | drier |
| (none) | `túisce` | sooner |
| (none) | `neasa` (do) | nearer (to) |

**Notes on Irregular Forms:**

-   `túisce` and `neasa` have no positive form.
-   The adjective `mall` (slow) often uses `moille` as its comparative instead of the regular `maille`.
-   The adverb `minic` (often) has the regular comparative `minice` but also the irregular `minicí`.
-   Dialects may have other irregular forms, such as `gránna` → `gráinne` (ugly) or `olc` → `oilce` (evil).

### Formation with `mó` and `lú`

For adjectives whose comparative form is the same as the positive (e.g., third declension), or for emphasis, `mó` (more) and `lú` (less) can be used, similar to English "more" and "less".

**With an Adjective**

This construction uses a double copula.

| Context | Structure | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Present | `is mó`/`lú` + `is` + Positive | *Is mó is crua é ná riamh.* (It is harder than ever.) |
| Past/Conditional | `ba mhó`/`lú` + `ba` + Positive | *Ba mhó ba chrua é ná riamh.* (It was harder than ever.) |

-   `is lú is...` can mean "less" or "not so": *is lú is sean* (less old, not so old).
-   `is mó is` followed by another comparative can mean "too": *is mó is airde* means `ró-ard` (too high).
-   Negated `ní mó ná...` means "not very": *Ní mó ná sásta a bhí sé.* (He was not very satisfied.)

**With a Noun**

This structure creates a "nominative of quality", similar to saying "a man of greatest fame".

| Context | Structure | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Present | `is mó`/`lú` + Noun | *an fear is mó cáil* (the most famous man) |
| Past/Conditional | `ba mhó`/`lú` + Noun | *an fear ba mhó cáil* (the most famous man) |

-   *na hoibrithe is lú pá* - the lowest-paid workers (lit. "the workers of least pay")
-   *na hailt is mó léamh* - the most-read articles (using a verbal noun)

### The Suffix `-de` ("all the more")

The suffix `-de` can be added to a comparative form to mean "all the more" or "the...the...". It derives from the prepositional pronoun `de` ("of it, by it").

-   `fearr` + `de` → `fearrde` (all the better)
-   `mó` + `de` → `móide` (all the more/bigger)
-   `measa` + `de` → `miste` (all the worse, irregular)

This form is used in copular sentences where the cause or extent of the comparison is mentioned or implied.

-   *Is fusaide é a dhéanamh.* - It is all the easier to do.
-   *Is fearrde duine gáire.* - Laughter does a person good. (lit. "A person is all the better for laughter.")
-   *Ní miste liom é.* - I don't mind it. (lit. "It is not all the worse with me.")
-   *Ní móide go rachfaidh mé.* - It is unlikely that I will go.

In modern Irish, this "all the more" sense is often expressed with `is amhlaidh is...`:

-   *Is amhlaidh is fearr é.* - It is all the better.

## Using the Comparative

The comparative form is purely **predicative**; it cannot be used directly before a noun like in English ("the taller man"). Instead, Irish uses constructions with the copula, typically in a relative clause.

Usage depends on whether the adjective is:
-   **Predicative**: *Tá sé níos airde.* (He is taller.)
-   **Attributive**: *an fear is airde* (the taller/tallest man)
-   **Adverbial**: *Ritheann sé níos tapúla.* (He runs faster.)

And whether the comparison is:
-   **Dependent**: with a comparison partner (`ná` - than).
-   **Independent**: without a comparison partner.

### Predicative Usage

**Dependent Comparison (with `ná`)**

When a comparison partner is introduced by `ná` (than), two main structures are used.

1.  **Simple Copula Sentence:**
    -   Present: `is` + Comparative
        -   *Is gile an ghrian ná an ghealach.* (The sun is brighter than the moon.)
    -   Past/Conditional: `ba` + Comparative
        -   *Ba ghile an ghrian ná an ghealach.* (The sun was brighter than the moon.)

2.  **With the Verb `bí`:**
    -   Present: `níos` + Comparative
        -   *Tá an ghrian níos gile ná an ghealach.* (The sun is brighter than the moon.)
    -   Past/Conditional: `ní ba` + Comparative
        -   *Bhí an ghrian ní ba ghile ná an ghealach.* (The sun was brighter than the moon.)

> **What is `níos`?**
> The word `níos` is a contraction of `ní` (a thing) and `is` (the relative form of the copula). So, `níos fearr` literally means "a thing that is better". Similarly, `ní ba` means "a thing that was better".

**Independent Comparison (without `ná`)**

When no comparison partner is mentioned, the structure with `bí` and `níos`/`ní ba` is most common.

-   *Tá an aimsir níos teo inniu.* - The weather is warmer today.
-   *Bhí an ghrian ní ba ghile inné.* - The sun was brighter yesterday.

**Other Predicative Constructions**

-   **Getting/Becoming**: Use `éirigh` + `níos`.
    -   *D’éirigh an aimsir níos teo.* - The weather got warmer.
-   **Much/Far**: Use `cuid mhaith`, `go mór`, or `i bhfad` before `níos`.
    -   *Tá sé i bhfad níos fearr.* - It is far better.
-   **A Little**: Use `beagán`, `pas beag`, or `rud beag` before `níos`.
    -   *Tá sé beagán níos airde.* - It is a little higher.
-   **Extent of Difference**: State the amount, often with `de` ("by").
    -   *Bhí sé deich mbliana ní b’óige ná í.* - He was ten years younger than her.
    -   *Tá sé níos sine de chúig bliana.* - He is five years older (older by five years).
-   **Multiplicative Difference**: Several patterns exist for "X times bigger".
    -   *Tá sé seacht n-uaire níos airde.* - It is seven times higher.
    -   *Tá sé seacht n-oiread chomh hard.* - It is seven times as high.

### Attributive Usage (Modifying a Noun)

To say "a better car" or "the tallest man", Irish uses relative clauses where the comparative is predicative.

**With an Indefinite Noun**

-   Use `níos`/`ní ba` after the noun.
    -   *Ba mhaith liom carr níos fearr.* - I would like a better car.
-   Use a copular relative clause (`is`/`ba`).
    -   *Tá leabhar is mó agamsa ná do cheannsa.* - I have a bigger book than yours.
-   Use a copular sentence with `de`.
    -   *Is fearr de bhia é ná cál.* - It is a better food than kale.

**With a Definite Noun (Comparative and Superlative)**

This structure is used for both comparative ("the taller man") and superlative ("the tallest man") meanings. Context determines the translation.

The structure is: `an` + Noun + `is`/`ba` + Comparative.

-   *an fear is mó* - the taller man / the tallest man

**Distinguishing Comparative from Superlative**

-   **Comparative**: A comparison partner with `ná` follows.
    -   *an fear is mó ná mise* - the man who is taller than me
-   **Superlative**: The group is specified with a prepositional phrase.
    -   `de`/`acu`: *an fear is mó de na daoine* (the tallest of the people); *an fear is mó acu* (the tallest of them).
    -   `i`/`ar`: *an cailín is áille in Éirinn* (the most beautiful girl in Ireland); *an fear is mó ar an domhan* (the tallest man in the world).
-   **Superlative**: The group is specified with a relative clause.
    -   *an fear is mó atá ann* - the tallest man there is
    -   *an fear is mó dá bhfaca mé riamh* - the tallest man I have ever seen

**Other Attributive Patterns**

-   **Qualitative Nominative**: A noun follows the comparative.
    -   *an fear is airde clú* - the man of highest fame
    -   *an fíon is deise blas* - the wine with the most beautiful taste
-   **Possessives**: You cannot say *`ár dteach is mó`*. Instead, use a relative clause.
    -   *an teach is mó atá againn* - our biggest house
-   **Ordinals**: "The second tallest man".
    -   *an dara fear is mó* - the second tallest man

### Adverbial Usage

**Comparative Adverbs**

To say "faster", "more clearly", etc., you can use `níos` or a copular construction.

-   *Ritheann Seán níos luaithe.* - Seán runs faster.
-   *Is luaithe a ritheann Seán.* - Seán runs faster. (lit. "It is faster that Seán runs.")

**Superlative Adverbs**

To say "fastest", "best", etc., Irish uses more complex structures.

-   **Double Relative Clause**:
    -   *Is é Seán is luaithe a ritheann.* - Seán runs fastest. (lit. "It is Seán who is fastest who runs.")
-   **Verbal Noun Construction**:
    -   *Is é Seán is luaithe rith.* - Seán runs fastest. (lit. "It is Seán who is of fastest running.")

## The Equative (`an chéim chothroim`)

The equative expresses equality ("as big as"). It is formed with `chomh` ... `le`/`agus`.

-   `chomh` + Adjective + `le` + Noun
    -   *Tá sé chomh mór le Pól.* - He is as big as Paul.
-   `chomh` + Adjective + `agus` + Clause
    -   *Tá sé chomh mór agus a bhí sé.* - He is as big as he was.

`Chomh` adds an h-prefix to adjectives beginning with a vowel: `chomh hard` (as high).

**Asking "How...?**

Use `cé chomh` to ask questions like "How big...?".

-   *Cé chomh mór is atá sé?* - How big is it?

## The Excessive (`an chéim iomarcach`)

The excessive expresses "too much" of a quality ("too big").

The most common way is with the prefix `ró-`, which causes lenition.

-   `rómhór` - too big
-   `róshean` - too old
-   `ró-ard` - too high (hyphenated before a vowel)

An alternative is the idiomatic use of `mór` and `beag` with the copula and the prepositions `le` (subjective opinion) or `do` (objective capacity).

-   *Is mór liom é.* - I think it's too much.
-   *Is mór dom é.* - It is too much for me.
-   *Ní mór dom é a dhéanamh.* - I must do it. (lit. "It is not too much for me to do it.")
-   *Is beag liom é.* - I think it's too little.
-   *Ní beag dom é.* - It is enough for me.
