Abstract nouns describe qualities or concepts rather than concrete objects. In Irish grammar, this term often refers specifically to nouns derived from adjectives.

These abstract nouns are divided into two types: **ordinary** and **gradual**. While the form is often the same for both, they can differ and have distinct uses.

## Ordinary Abstract Nouns

The ordinary abstract noun (`an t-ainmfhocal teibí gnách`) expresses a quality in a general sense, without reference to a specific bearer of that quality.

### Formation

Ordinary abstract nouns are typically formed in one of the following ways:

| Formation Method | Gender | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Comparative form of the adjective | Feminine | `minice` (frequency), `bacaí` (lameness) |
| Comparative + `-(e)acht` or `-(a)íocht` | Feminine | `deirgeacht` (redness), `clistíocht` (cleverness) |
| Adjective + `-(a)íl` (esp. from `-ach`) | Feminine | `bacaíl` (lameness) |
| Adjective + `-(e)as` or `-(e)adas` | Masculine | `maitheas` (goodness), `beos` (liveliness), `dorchadas` (darkness) |
| Adjective + `-achar` (esp. from `-ach`) | Masculine | `lagachar` (weakness), `salachar` (dirt) |
| Adjective used as a noun | Varies | `maith` (the good) |

-   **Irregular forms:** A few nouns have an irregular `-ad` ending, such as `leithead` (width) from `leathan` (wide), `raimhead` (fatness) from `ramhar` (fat), and `laghad` (smallness) from `beag` (small). The noun `oiread` (amount) also belongs to this group, though it has no corresponding adjective.
-   **Multiple forms:** Many adjectives can form more than one abstract noun, e.g., from `maith` (good) we get `maith`, `maithe`, and `maitheas`.
-   **Restrictions:** The comparative form of certain adjectives **cannot** be used as an ordinary abstract noun. This applies to adjectives ending in `-mhar` or a vowel (e.g., `grianmhaire` from `grianmhar`), those with a comparative in `-a` (e.g., `fearúla` from `fearúil`), and the irregular `dóichí` from `dócha`. These forms are used only as gradual abstract nouns.

### Usage

The ordinary abstract noun is used to express a quality in general, often with the definite article.

-   *Ní maith liom an dorchadas.* - I don't like darkness.

## Gradual Abstract Nouns

The gradual abstract noun (`an t-ainmfhocal teibí céime`) is used to indicate a specific degree or extent of a quality. It is closely related to the adjective's comparative form and is sometimes called the **abstract comparative**.

### Formation

-   For adjectives ending in `-ach`, `-mhar`, or a vowel, the gradual abstract noun is the same as the **comparative** form.
    -   Examples: `crua` -> `crua`, `aisteach` -> `aistí`, `deireanach` -> `deireanaí`, `dócha` -> `dóichí`, `greannmhar` -> `greannmhaire`.
    -   This also applies to adjectives whose comparative ends in `-a`, e.g., `deacra` from `deacair`.
-   Other adjectives use either the **comparative** form or a form ending in `-(e)acht` or `-íocht`.
    -   Examples: `minic` -> `minice` or `minicíocht`; `moch` -> `moiche` or `moicheacht`; `ard` -> `airde` or `airdeacht`.
-   Some adjectives use a **special form** as their gradual abstract noun. For example, the gradual abstract noun of `maith` (good) is usually `feabhas`, not `maitheas` or `maithe`. Some of these forms, like `feabhas`, are used *only* as gradual abstract nouns.
    -   Note: In Munster, `méid` is sometimes used instead of `méad` for 'size' or 'extent'.

### Usage

The gradual abstract noun is used when referring to the extent or degree of a quality, particularly in the syntactic structures detailed below. It is also used after words like `méad` (size, extent).

-   *méid na donachta* - the extent of the wickedness

## Syntax of the Gradual Abstract Noun

The gradual abstract noun appears in several key syntactic constructions to express degree, comparison, and extent. These constructions often involve the particle `a`, which is historically the masculine 3rd person singular possessive pronoun, 'his'. It functions as a proleptic pronoun, referring to the following clause, noun, or pronoun. Although it originates as a possessive, it is often treated simply as a particle indicating degree (`mír chéime`).

### `a` + Abstract Noun: "how good it is"

This structure is used to comment on the degree of a quality.

| Structure | Example |
| --- | --- |
| `a` + gradual abstract noun + direct relative clause | *Is iontach a fheabhas atá sé.* (It is wonderful how good he is.) |
| `a` + gradual abstract noun + noun + direct relative clause | *Inis dom a laghad airgead atá agat.* (Tell me how little money you have.) |

**Notes on this construction:**

-   The particle `a` causes lenition. It is always the masculine form, never the feminine `a` ('her'). The literal meaning of the first example is "Its wonder is his goodness that he is".
-   **Questions:** This structure can be turned into a question using `cá` (`cá` + `a` > `cá`), as explained below.
-   **With prepositions:** It can combine with a preceding preposition, e.g., `trína` (`trí` + `a`).
    -   *Fuair sé ardmholadh trína fheabhas a rinne sé an obair.* - He received great praise for how well he did the work.
-   **With `ar`:** The preposition `ar` creates several common adverbial phrases:
    -   `ar a laghad` - at least
    -   `ar a mhéad` - at most
    -   `ar a luaithe` - at the earliest
    -   `ar a dhéanaí` - at the latest
    -   `ar a fheabhas` - excellently
    -   `ar a dhonacht` - as badly as possible
-   **With conjunctions:**
    -   With `agus`, it describes a circumstance: *Tá sé go han-mhaith agus a óige atá sé.* (He is very good considering how young he is.)
    -   With `ach`, it has a restrictive sense: *Sin é an bealach is fearr ach a dheacracht atá sé.* (That is the best way, even if it is difficult.)
-   **Following nouns:**
    -   A following noun may be in the genitive (especially uncountable nouns): `a laghad údair` (how little cause).
    -   More often, the noun is in the nominative: `a laghad airgead` (how little money).
    -   A partitive dative with `de` can also be used: *Tá a fhios agam a fheabhas de bhean thú.* (I know what a good woman you are.)
-   **As... as:** The structure can express "as ... as": *Labhramar a laghad Béarla agus ab fhéidir.* (We spoke as little English as possible.)
-   **... enough:** After `is leor` or `ní beag`, it means "... enough":
    -   *Is leor a laghad.* - It is small enough.
    -   *Ní beag a dhonacht.* - It is bad enough.

**Alternative:** Instead of this construction, you can often use `chomh` + adjective: *chomh maith agus atá sé* (as good as he is / how good he is).

**Note on relative clauses:** The direct relative clause is sometimes introduced by `agus` or `is` (e.g., *a fheabhas agus a bhí sé*), likely by analogy with the `chomh ... agus` structure. This is generally considered superfluous.

### `cá` + Abstract Noun: "how good is it?"

To ask a direct or indirect question about the degree of a quality, `cá` is used.

| Structure | Example |
| --- | --- |
| `cá` + gradual abstract noun + direct relative clause | *Cá fheabhas atá sé?* (How good is he?) |
| `cad é a` + gradual abstract noun + direct relative clause | *Cad é a fheabhas atá sé?* (How good is he?) |

**Notes on this construction:**

-   This `cá` is a contraction of the interrogative word `cá` and the particle `a` (`cá` < `cá` + `a`).
-   It causes lenition.
-   **Dialectal variants:**
    -   Connacht/Munster: `cé` (e.g., *cé fhad?* - how long?). In Munster, `méid` is often not lenited: *cé méid?* (how much/many?).
    -   Other forms include `cén` (< `cé` + `an`), `cad é a`, and `cad é an`.
-   **Following nouns:** A noun can follow `cá` + abstract noun, indicating what is being measured: *cé mhéad duine?* (how many people?).
-   **Indirect questions:** It is important to distinguish between an indirect question (which uses `cá`) and a statement about degree (which uses `a`).
    -   *Níl a fhios agam cá fheabhas atá sé.* - I don't know how good he is. (Indirect question)
    -   *Is iontach a fheabhas atá sé.* - It is wonderful how good he is. (Statement)

**Alternatives:**

-   `cá` + adjective + indirect relative clause (note: no `a` and no lenition): *Cá fada a raibh tú ansin?* (How long were you there?)
-   `cé chomh` + adjective: *Cé chomh maith agus a bhí sé?* (How good was it?)

### `dá` + Abstract Noun: "however good"

This construction expresses a concessive idea, like "however good it may be".

| Context | Structure | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| With verbs | `dá` + abstract noun + relative clause | *dá fheabhas a rinne sé é* (however well he did it) |
| With `bí` | `dá` + abstract noun + relative clause | *dá fheabhas atá sé* (however good he is) |
| With `bí` (omitted) | `dá` + abstract noun + subject | *dá dheacracht é* (however difficult it may be) |
| With nouns | `dá` + abstract noun + noun + clause | *dá mhéad dúil a bhí aige ann* (however much he liked it) |
| As an attribute | noun + `dá` + abstract noun | *amhras dá laghad* (the slightest doubt) |

**Notes on this construction:**

-   The initial `dá` is a combination of the preposition `de` and the particle `a` (`dá` < `de` + `a`, "of his"). It causes lenition. The literal sense is partitive: "of whatever difficulty".
-   **Relative clause variations:** The relative clause that follows can take several forms:
    -   Direct relative clause: *dá fheabhas a rinne sé é*.
    -   Indirect relative clause, often with a second `dá` (`de` + relative pronoun): *dá fheabhas dá ndéarna sé é*. This is common in Connacht.
    -   With `mar`: *dá fheabhas mar a dheineamair é*.
-   **Omitted verb:** A form of the verb `bí` can be omitted, leaving just the subject. This structure resembles a copula sentence.
    -   *dá fheabhas í an bhean* - however good the woman may be.
-   **Following nouns:** A noun immediately following the abstract noun is usually in the nominative singular: *dá laghad ciall atá aige* (however little sense he has).
-   **As an attribute:** The phrase can follow a noun, often in negative sentences, to mean "whatsoever" or "the slightest".
    -   *Níl amhras dá laghad faoi.* - There is not the slightest doubt about it.
-   **Dialectal variants:** In Connemara, `dhá` is sometimes used instead of `dá`.

**Alternative (Donegal):**

In Donegal, a different structure is common: Adjective + `agus (eile) mar` + direct relative clause.
-   *maith agus eile mar atá siad* - however good they are.
-   *óg agus uile mar a bhí sé ag an am* - however young he was at the time.

### `dá ... is ea is ...`: "the bigger, the better"

This structure expresses a proportional relationship: as one quality increases, so does another.

| Structure | Example |
| --- | --- |
| `dá` + abstract noun ... `is ea is` + comparative | *Dá mhéad é is ea is fearr.* (The bigger it is, the better.) |
| `dá` + abstract noun ... `is amhlaidh is` + comparative | *Dá mhéad é is amhlaidh is fearr.* (The bigger it is, the better.) |

**Notes on this construction:**

-   The first part uses the same `dá` construction as in "however good". The difference is that here it expresses a proportional, not a concessive, relationship.
    -   *Dá laghad a gcím díot is amhlaidh is fearr dom.* - The less I see of you, the better for me.
-   The phrase `is ea` or `is amhlaidh` links the two clauses. `is ea` marks the first clause as the topic of the main copula sentence. `Amhlaidh` is an adverb meaning 'so' or 'thus'.
-   In subordinate clauses, `gurb ea` is used: *Is dócha dá mhéad é gurb ea is fearr é.* (It's probable that the bigger it is, the better.)
-   **Important distinction:** Compare the proportional and concessive meanings:
    -   *Dá laghad é is ea is fearr é.* - The smaller it is, the better it is. (Proportional)
    -   *Dá laghad é is fearr é.* - However small it is, it is the better one. (Concessive)

#### Alternatives and Other Forms

-   **Ulster:** The second clause can be introduced by `sin mar is`.
    -   *Dá mhéad é sin mar is fearr é.* - The bigger he is, the better he is.
-   **Donegal:** Both clauses can be introduced by `mar is` + comparative, without using an abstract noun.
    -   *Mar is mó atá ann mar is fearr é.* - The more there are, the better.
-   **Older forms:**
    -   `is móide` (comparative + `-de`) can replace `is ea is mó`.
        -   *Dá neartmhaire a shéideas an ghaoth an tine is móide a lasas sí é.* - The stronger the wind blows the fire, the more it ignites it.
    -   A double negative structure `Níl dá ... nach ...` was also used.
        -   *Níl dá mhéad a thaithí nach móide a spéis.* - The greater his familiarity, the greater his appetite.
-   **Other constructions:**
    -   `de réir mar a` ('according as'): *De réir mar a théann siad in aois is amhlaidh is measa a bhíonn siad.* (The older they get, the worse they are.)
    -   `nuair is` ('when... is'): *Nuair is mó an chruachás is é is mó an cuidiú.* (When the dilemma is greatest, the help is also greatest.)

### `téigh i` + Abstract Noun: "it gets better"

To describe a gradual change in a quality, the verb `téigh` (to go) is used with the preposition `i` (in) and the gradual abstract noun.

| Structure | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Form of `téigh` + `i` + gradual abstract noun | *Tá sé ag dul i bhfeabhas.* (It is getting better.) |

**Notes on this construction:**

-   The literal meaning is "it is going into improvement".
-   The preposition `chun` (to) can be used instead of `i`.
    -   *Tá sé ag dul chun donachta.* - It is getting worse.
