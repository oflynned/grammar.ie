In Irish, adjectives can be descriptive (`aidiachtaí tuairisciúla`) or pronominal (`aidiachtaí forainmneacha`). Descriptive adjectives have three main functions in a sentence, each with its own grammatical rules.

- **Predicative:** The adjective describes the subject and is linked by a verb, most commonly `bí` (to be).
  - *Tá an bhean tinn.* (The woman is ill.)
- **Attributive:** The adjective directly modifies a noun, following it and agreeing with it in gender, number, and case.
  - *an bhean thinn* (the ill woman)
- **Adverbial:** The adjective is used to describe a verb, usually by adding the particle `go`.
  - *Tá an bhean ag obair go maith.* (The woman works well.)

## Predicative Adjectives

A predicative adjective (`an aidiacht fhaisnéiseach`) follows a verb and describes the subject of the sentence. The most common verb used is `bí`. In this role, the adjective is in its base form and does not change to agree with the noun.

- *Tá an fear ard.* (The man is tall.)
- *Tá na mná sásta.* (The women are happy.)

### Predicative Use with `go`

Some subjectively evaluative adjectives use the adverbial particle `go` when used predicatively with the verb `bí`. This is especially common when describing health, weather, or personal states.

- *Tá mé go maith.* (I am well.)
- *Tá an aimsir go hálainn.* (The weather is beautiful.)
- *Tá an teach go dona.* (The house is bad.)

The use of `go` in this context can be nuanced and vary by dialect. It often distinguishes a temporary state (health, weather) from an inherent quality. For example, *Tá sé uafásach* means "It (an animal, etc.) is hideous," while *Tá sé go huafásach* means "It (the weather) is terrible." Similarly, *Tá sé lag* means "It is weak," while *Tá sé go lag* means "He is weak (in health)."

However, many adjectives, even those describing weather, never take `go`: *Tá sé fuar* (It is cold).

### Secondary Predicates

Predicative adjectives can also appear with verbs other than `bí` to describe the state of the subject or object during the action. These are known as depictive predicatives (`faisnéisí léiritheacha`). They do not take the adverbial particle `go`.

- *Shiúil sé abhaile tinn.* (He walked home ill.)
- *D'ith sí an t-úll amh.* (She ate the apple raw.)

This can also be expressed using a small clause with `agus` (and), where the verb `bí` is omitted.

- *D'fhill sé agus é tinn.* (He returned, and he [was] ill.)

### Resultative Predicates

Resultative predicatives (`faisnéisí críochnaitheacha`) describe the result of an action.

The verb `fan` (to remain, stay) can be used with an adjective to express the continuation of a state. The adjective can appear with or without `go`.

- *D'fhan sé ciúin.* (He remained calm.)
- *Fan go socair.* (Stay calm.)

The verb `déan` (to make, do) can express the causing of a property, though this is not very common.

- *Rinne sin an teach níos uaigní.* (That made the house lonelier.)

More frequently, this idea is expressed by "putting" a property on someone, using the verb `cuir` and the preposition `ar`.

- *Chuir sé áthas orm.* (It made me happy.)
- *Chuirfeadh sé tinneas ort.* (It would make you ill.)

## Attributive Adjectives

An attributive adjective (`an aidiacht aitreabúideach`) directly modifies a noun. In Irish, it almost always comes **after** the noun. It must agree with the noun in gender, number, and case, which means it is declined and undergoes initial mutation (usually lenition).

- *fear mór* (a big man)
- *bean mhór* (a big woman)
- *hata an fhir mhóir* (the big man's hat)

When multiple adjectives modify a single noun, they all follow the noun and are each declined and mutated as required. They are not usually separated by `agus` (and).

- *an teach mór bán* (the big white house)

### Adjectives Before the Noun

A small number of adjectives appear **before** the noun, often as prefixes. These are not declined.

- `droch-`: *an drochbhád* (the bad boat)
- `dea-`: *an dea-chlú* (the good reputation)
- `sean-`: *an seanfhear* (the old man)

Certain pronominal adjectives also precede the noun, such as `gach` (every).

### Order of Attributes

When a noun has multiple modifiers, they follow a specific order:

1.  **Noun**
2.  **Descriptive Adjectives**: These follow a typical sequence: size, quality, colour, origin.
    - *an cailín beag óg deas rua Éireannach* (the small, young, nice, red-haired Irish girl)
3.  **Genitive Attributes**: A noun in the genitive case follows any descriptive adjectives.
    - *bean mhaith an tí* (the good woman of the house)
    - *balla ard cloiche* (a high stone wall)
    - **Exception**: When a noun and its genitive attribute form a single concept (like `scian phóca` for "pocketknife"), the descriptive adjective comes after the entire unit.
      - *scian phóca bheag* (a small pocketknife)
      - *mac léinn cliste* (a clever student)
      - Compare: *fear oibre maith* (a good worker) vs. *fear maith oibre* (a good man for working).
4.  **Pronominal Adjectives**: Words like `eile` (other) and `seo` (this) follow descriptive adjectives.
    - *an bhean mhaith seo eile* (this other good woman)

### Exceptions to Declension

In some specific contexts, attributive adjectives are not declined:

- After feminine personal names in the genitive: *mac Bhríd bheag* (the son of little Bríd), but *mac na mná bige* (the son of the little woman).
- After adverbs like `cuibheasach` (quite) or `réasúnta` (reasonably). The adjective is also not lenited.
  - *an tsrón cuibheasach mór* (the quite big nose), but *an tsrón mhór* (the big nose).
- After the numbers 3–10, the adjective is in the plural form, even though the noun is singular.
  - *trí bhád mhóra* (three big boats)
- After the noun `muintir` (people, family), which is singular, the adjective is usually plural.
  - *an mhuintir mhóra* (the adults)
- Adjectives with the prefixes `an-` (very) and `ró-` (too) are not declined in copular sentences.
  - *Is daoine an-mhaith iad.* (They are very good people.)

### Comparatives and Superlatives

Comparative and superlative forms of adjectives cannot be used attributively. Instead, a relative clause with the copula is used.

- *an fear is mó* (the bigger/biggest man; literally "the man who is bigger")
- *gach rud is féidir* (everything possible; literally "every thing that is possible")

## Adjectives as Adverbs

To use a descriptive adjective as an adverb (`dobhriathar`), the particle `go` is placed before its base form.

| Context | Formation | Examples |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Consonant initial | `go` + Adjective | `go maith`, `go cliste`, `go sciobtha` |
| Vowel initial | `go` + h-prefix + Adjective | `go hiontach`, `go hard` |

- *Tá mé sciobtha.* (I am fast.) — predicative
- *D’oibrigh mé go sciobtha.* (I worked fast.) — adverbial

### Special Cases

- The particle `go` is omitted if another adverb modifies the adjective: *go dona* (badly), but *réasúnta dona* (quite badly).
- In cleft sentences where the adverb is fronted, `go` is usually dropped because the adjective functions predicatively with the copula.
  - *Is cúramach a rinne sé é.* (It is carefully that he did it.)
- For the comparative form of an adverb, `níos` (or `ní ba` in the past) is used instead of `go`.
  - *Déanfaidh sé níos cúramaí é.* (He will do it more carefully.)

## Pronominal Adjectives

Pronominal adjectives (`aidiachtaí forainmneacha`) specify or generalise a noun rather than describing it. This category includes the article (`an`), numbers (`dhá`), possessive pronouns (`mo`), and demonstratives (`seo`).

They are always used attributively but are not declined. Some come before the noun (`gach fear` - every man), while others come after (`fear éigin` - some man).

- **`gach`**: every. *gach fear* (every man).
  - Also `gach uile` (*gach uile fhear*) and `gach aon` (*gach aon fhear*).
- **`uile`**: all, whole. Its position and meaning vary.
  - After plural noun: *na naoimh uile* (all saints).
  - Before singular noun: *an uile mhaitheas* (all goodness).
  - After singular noun: *an scéal uile* (the whole story).
- **`ar fad`, `go léir`**: all, the whole.
  - *na leabhair ar fad* (all the books), *na daoine go léir* (all the people).
- **`éigin`**: some, any. *fear éigin* (some man).
- **`ar bith`**: any (in questions), no (in negative sentences). *fear ar bith* (any man/no man).
- **`aon`**: any, no. Precedes the noun. *aon fhear* (any man/no man).
- **`eile`**: other, another. *fear eile* (another man).
- **`céanna`**: the same (used with the article). *an fear céanna* (the same man).
- **`áirithe`**: certain, particular. *lá áirithe* (on a certain day).

### Usage of `aon` and `ar bith`

Like "any" in English, `aon` and `ar bith` are "negative polarity items" and appear mainly in specific contexts:

- **Negative sentences**: *Ní haon amadán mé.* (I am no fool.) *Níl carr ar bith agam.* (I don't have a car at all.)
- **Questions**: *An bhfuil aon charr agat?* (Do you have any car?)
- **Conditional sentences**: *má tá aon eolas agat faoi* (if you have any information about it).
- **Comparisons with `ná`**: *Is fearr é ná aon duine agaibhse.* (He is better than any of you.)
- **After superlatives**: *an méadú is mó in aon tír* (the biggest increase in any country).

### Special Meanings of `aon`

- **As a numeral**: When meaning the number "one" in a positive statement, it is usually followed by `amháin`. *aon lá amháin* (one day).
- **"The only"**: With the article, it means "the only". *an t-aon lá amháin a bhí mé saor* (the only day I was free).
- **"The same"**: It can mean "the same as", with the comparison introduced by `le`. *Tá mé ar aon tuairim leat.* (I am of the same opinion as you.)
