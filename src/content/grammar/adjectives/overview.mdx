Adjectives are words that describe nouns, such as "big", "small", "good", or "bad". In Irish, adjectives are a core part of the language, but they behave differently from their English counterparts. Most notably, they usually come *after* the noun they describe and must change their form to agree with it.

## Placement of Adjectives

The most fundamental rule for Irish adjectives is that they almost always follow the noun they modify.

- *fear **mór*** - a **big** man
- *bean **chineálta*** - a **kind** woman
- *leabhar **suimiúil*** - an **interesting** book

## Agreement with the Noun

An adjective must "agree" with its noun in three ways: gender, number, and case. This means the adjective's form can change depending on the noun it is describing.

1.  **Gender:** It must match whether the noun is masculine or feminine.
2.  **Number:** It must match whether the noun is singular or plural.
3.  **Case:** It must match the noun's grammatical role (nominative, genitive, etc.).

This agreement often involves adding endings to the adjective or causing an initial mutation.

## Initial Mutations

Adjectives undergo initial mutations (lenition or eclipsis) depending on the noun they follow. This is one of the most important aspects of using them correctly.

For example, an adjective is lenited after a feminine singular noun in the nominative case:

- *fear **mór*** (man is masculine, so no mutation)
- *bean **mhór*** (woman is feminine, so `mór` becomes `mhór`)

It is also lenited after a masculine singular noun in the genitive case:

- *hata an fhir **mhóir*** - the hat of the big man

<Note>
These mutation rules are complex and depend on the gender, number, and case of the noun. They will be covered in detail on subsequent pages.
</Note>

## Attributive vs. Predicative Adjectives

It is important to distinguish between two ways adjectives can be used.

**Attributive adjectives** directly modify a noun and are part of the noun phrase. They follow the noun and agree with it.

- *Chonaic mé an **fear mór**.* - I saw the **big man**.
- *Is bean **chineálta** í.* - She is a **kind** woman.

**Predicative adjectives** describe the subject of a sentence, usually linked by the verb *bí* ("to be"). They do not follow the noun directly, do not agree with it in form, and are never mutated by it.

- *Tá an fear **mór**.* - The man **is big**.
- *Tá an bhean **cineálta**.* - The woman **is kind**.

<Tip>
A simple way to tell the difference: if the adjective is part of a "the big man" type of phrase, it's attributive. If it follows a verb like "is" or "are" to describe the subject, it's predicative.
</Tip>

| Attributive | Predicative |
| :--- | :--- |
| *an cailín **beag*** (the small girl) | *Tá an cailín **beag*** (The girl is small) |
| *an teach **nua*** (the new house) | *Tá an teach **nua*** (The house is new) |

## Grading Adjectives

Like in English, Irish adjectives can be graded to make comparisons. This involves using special forms or particles.

- **Positive:** *mór* (big)
- **Comparative:** *níos mó* (bigger)
- **Superlative:** *is mó* (biggest)

These forms are used to compare two or more things and have their own set of grammatical rules.

## Verbal Adjectives

A verbal adjective (*aidiacht bhriathartha*) is a form derived from a verb that functions as an adjective. They often correspond to English past participles ending in "-ed" or "-en".

- *dóigh* (to burn) → *dóite* (burnt)
- *bris* (to break) → *briste* (broken)

They are used just like regular adjectives:

- *arán **dóite*** - burnt bread
- *fuinneog **bhriste*** - a broken window
