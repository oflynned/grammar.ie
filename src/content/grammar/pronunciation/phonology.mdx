Phonology is the study of the sounds of a language, distinct from orthography, which is the study of its spelling. This page provides an overview of the sound system of Irish, which is essential for understanding its pronunciation and spelling rules.

## The Sounds of Irish

The transcription system used here is a "modified IPA" (International Phonetic Alphabet) common in Irish linguistics. It is a phonological representation, meaning it captures the essential sound categories rather than every fine phonetic detail, making it more practical for learners and for representing different dialects with a single system.

### Transcription Conventions

**Slender (Palatalised) Consonants**
Slender consonants are marked with a prime symbol (`′`). This is a simplified alternative to the various specific symbols used in the full IPA system.
*   `[k′]` (IPA: `[c]`)
*   `[x′]` (IPA: `[ç]`)
*   `[g′]` (IPA: `[ɟ]`)
*   `[s′]` (IPA: `[ʃ]` or `[ɕ]`)

**Broad (Velarised) Consonants**
Broad consonants are phonetically neutral or slightly velarised (like sounds made towards the back of the mouth). In this transcription, they are left unmarked for simplicity.
*   `[b]` (IPA: `[bˠ]`)
*   `[t]` (IPA: `[t̪]`, a dental 't')
*   `[d]` (IPA: `[d̪]`, a dental 'd')

**Aspiration**
The puff of air (aspiration) that follows voiceless plosives like `p`, `t`, and `k` in English and German is also present in Irish but is not marked in this transcription. So, `[k]` is used instead of `[kʰ]`.

**Tense L, N, and R**
Irish has a distinction between "tense" and "lax" versions of `l`, `n`, and `r`. In this system:
*   **Tense** sounds are shown with uppercase letters: `[ʟ]`, `[ʟ′]`, `[ɴ]`, `[ɴ′]`, `[ʀ]`.
*   **Lax** sounds are shown with lowercase letters: `[l]`, `[l′]`, `[n]`, `[n′]`, `[r]`, `[r′]`.

**Other Consonants**
*   `[x]` represents a sound similar to the "ch" in Scottish "loch" or German "ach".
*   `[f]` and `[v]` were originally bilabial (using both lips) but are now often labiodental (bottom lip and top teeth), like in English. The symbols `[f]` and `[v]` are used for both.

**Vowels**
The transcription simplifies vowel representation. Short vowels are typically more open than their long counterparts, but the same base symbol is used for both.
*   `[o]` represents a short `o` sound (phonetically closer to `[ɔ]`).
*   `[oː]` represents a long `o` sound.
*   `[ə]` represents the unstressed "schwa" sound, like the 'a' in "about".

## The Consonants

In Irish, consonants are arguably more central to the phonological system than vowels. The most important feature is the consistent contrast between "broad" and "slender" consonants. Vowels tend to adapt to the quality of the consonants around them.

Consonants are classified by their place and manner of articulation. All Irish consonants except `[h]` exist in both broad and slender forms.

### Labials
Labials are sounds formed with the lips.
*   **Plosives**: `[p]`, `[p′]`, `[b]`, `[b′]`
*   **Fricatives**: `[f]`, `[f′]`, `[v]`, `[v′]`
*   **Nasals**: `[m]`, `[m′]`

### Dentals
Dentals are formed with the tongue touching the upper teeth. The broad sounds `[t]`, `[d]`, `[ʟ]`, and `[ɴ]` are dental.
*   **Plosives**: `[t]`, `[d]`
*   **Liquids**: `[ʟ]`
*   **Nasals**: `[ɴ]`

<Details summary="Historical note">
The Old Irish dental fricatives `[θ]` and `[ð]` (like the English `th` in `thing` and `that`) no longer exist in Modern Irish. They were replaced over time by sounds like `[h]` and `[γ]`/`[γ′]`.
</Details>

### Alveolars
Alveolars are formed with the tongue-tip near the alveolar ridge (the gum ridge behind the teeth).
*   **Plosives**: `[ṭ]`, `[ḍ]` (rare, mostly in loanwords)
*   **Fricatives**: `[s]`, `[z]` (The voiced `[z]` only occurs in loanwords like `zú` for "zoo", or as an eclipsis variant of `s` in a few dialects).
*   **Liquids**: `[l]`, `[l′]`, `[r]`, `[ʀ]`
*   **Nasals**: `[n]`, `[n′]`

The broad `[r]` is a tapped or trilled 'r', similar to Spanish or Italian.

### Alveolopalatals
These sounds are formed at the transition point between the alveolar ridge and the hard palate.
*   **Plosives**: `[t′]`, `[d′]`
*   **Fricatives**: `[s′]`, `[z′]` (The slender `[s′]` sounds like English "sh" `[ʃ]`. The voiced `[z′]` `[ʒ]` is rare, found in loanwords like `jab` [d′z′ab] for "job").
*   **Liquids**: `[ʟ′]`
*   **Nasals**: `[ɴ′]`

### Palatals
Palatals are formed with the body of the tongue against the hard palate.
*   **Plosives**: `[k′]`, `[g′]`
*   **Fricatives**: `[x′]`, `[γ′]` (Slender `[x′]` is like the "ch" in German "ich". Slender `[γ′]` is similar to the 'y' in "yes").
*   **Liquids**: `[r′]` (A complex sound made with the blade of the tongue, not the tip. It never occurs at the beginning of a word).
*   **Nasals**: `[ŋ′]`

### Velars
Velars are formed with the back of the tongue against the soft palate (velum).
*   **Plosives**: `[k]`, `[g]`
*   **Fricatives**: `[x]`, `[γ]` (Broad `[x]` is like the "ch" in Scottish "loch". Broad `[γ]` is a voiced version of this, like the `g` in Spanish `lago`).
*   **Nasals**: `[ŋ]` (like the "ng" in "sing")
*   **Approximants**: `[w]` (like English "w". In Connacht and Ulster, this sound often replaces broad `[v]`).

### Glottals
Glottals are formed in the larynx.
*   **Fricatives**: `[h]` (Occurs from lenition of `t` and `s`, as the `h`-prefix, and in some other specific cases).
*   **Plosives**: `[ʔ]` (The glottal stop, common in English at the start of vowel-initial words like "apple", is almost absent in modern spoken Irish. Its absence allows vowels to merge where a consonant has been lost, e.g., `athair` [ahər′] can become [æːr′] in Cois Fharraige).

## Broad and Slender Consonants

Every Irish consonant except `[h]` has two distinct forms: a **broad** (velarised) form and a **slender** (palatalised) form. This distinction is phonemic, meaning it can change the meaning of a word. The terms `leathan` ("broad") and `caol` ("slender") are the traditional Irish names for this contrast.

| **Broad Consonants** | [p] | [b] | [v] | [f] | [m] | [t] | [d] | [n] | [ɴ] | [l] | [ʟ] | [r] | [s] | [z] | [k] | [g] | [x] | [γ] | [ŋ] |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Slender Consonants** | [p′] | [b′] | [v′] | [f′] | [m′] | [t′] | [d′] | [n′] | [ɴ′] | [l′] | [ʟ′] | [r′] | [s′] | [z′] | [k′] | [g′] | [x′] | [γ′] | [ŋ′] |

The difference is very audible. For example, broad `[s]` is like an English 's', while slender `[s′]` is like an English 'sh'. The difference is less obvious for labial sounds like `[p]` and `[p′]`.

<Dialect region="Ulster">
In Ulster, the slender plosives `[t′]` and `[d′]` are often pronounced as affricates, sounding like the "ch" in "church" (`[t͡ʃ]`) and the "j" in "judge" (`[d͡ʒ]`).
</Dialect>

This broad/slender quality is so fundamental that it governs the entire spelling system through the rule **`Caol le caol agus leathan le leathan`** ("Slender with slender and broad with broad"). This means that a consonant or consonant cluster must be surrounded by vowels of the same quality (slender vowels `e, i` or broad vowels `a, o, u`).

*   *sean* `[s′an]` (The `e` makes the `s` slender)
*   *san* `[san]` (The `a` makes the `s` broad)
*   *airgead* `[ar′əg′əd]` (The `ea` makes the `g` slender, the final `a` makes the `d` broad)
*   *airgid* `[ar′əg′əd′]` (The final `i` makes the `d` slender)

<Details summary="Linguistic comparison">
The broad/slender distinction in Irish is analogous to the "hard" and "soft" consonant distinction in Slavic languages like Russian. In Russian, this is indicated by special letters (ь, ъ) or by different vowel characters.
</Details>

## The Tense Consonants

The consonants `l`, `n`, and `r` historically had four variants each, based on a combination of the broad/slender distinction and a **tense/lax** distinction.

*   **Tense (Fortis)**: `[ʟ]`, `[ʟ′]`, `[ɴ]`, `[ɴ′]`, `[ʀ]`, `[ʀ′]`
*   **Lax (Lenis)**: `[l]`, `[l′]`, `[n]`, `[n′]`, `[r]`, `[r′]`

Tense variants occur at the beginning of unlenited words and are written with a single letter (`l, n, r`). Inside or at the end of a word, they are written with a double letter (`ll, nn, rr`). The lax variants are the lenited forms of these sounds.

The phonetic difference lies in the force and precision of articulation. For example, tense `[ʀ]` is a strongly rolled 'r', while lax `[r]` is a single tap.

### Dialectal Variations
The preservation of these distinctions varies significantly by dialect:
*   **Ulster**: Preserves most of the distinctions, except for `[ʀ′]`.
*   **Connacht**: Has three variants for `l` and `n`, and two for `r`. The lax `[l]`, `[n]` and tense `[ʀ]`, `[ʀ′]` have been lost.
*   **Munster**: Generally only has two variants for each (`[ʟ]`/`[l′]`, `[ɴ]`/`[n′]`, `[r]`/`[r′]`), merging the tense/lax distinction into the broad/slender one.

### Influence on Vowels
In dialects that have lost the phonetic distinction in the consonants themselves (especially Munster and parts of Connacht), the "tenseness" is often transferred to the preceding vowel in a stressed syllable. This causes the vowel to become long or to turn into a diphthong. This happens before syllable-final `ll, nn, rr` and clusters like `rd, rn, nt`.

*   *donn* ("brown"): `[doɴ]` (Ulster) vs. `[daun]` (Munster)
*   *ceann* ("head"): `[k′aɴ]` (Ulster) vs. `[k′aun]` (Munster) or `[k′ɑːɴ]` (Connemara)
*   *barr* ("top"): `[baʀ]` (Ulster) vs. `[baur]` (Munster) or `[bɑːr]` (Connemara)

This lengthening does not happen if a vowel follows, because the consonant is no longer at the end of the syllable.
*   *barr* `[bɑːr]` but *barra* `[bɑ.rə]`
*   *ceann* `[k′aun]` but *ceanna* `[k′a.ɴə]`

However, if a consonant-initial ending is added, the vowel remains long.
*   *fill* ("return") `[f′iːl′]` -> *fillte* ("returned") `[f′iːʟ′.t′ə]`
*   *coill* ("forest") `[kiːʟ′]` -> *coillte* ("forests") `[kiːʟ′.t′ə]`

### Tense M and NG
The consonants `m` and `ng` are always considered tense. They have a similar lengthening effect on preceding vowels in southern dialects.
*   *am* ("time") `[ɑːm]` but *ama* `[ɑ.mə]`
*   *im* ("butter") `[iːm]` but *ime* `[i.mə]`

<Note>
This vowel lengthening does not occur in unstressed syllables, such as the common verb endings `-ann` and `-inn`, or in pronouns like `sinn`.
</Note>

## Consonant Clusters

Irish allows for various consonant clusters at the beginning (onset) and end (coda) of a syllable.

### Syllable Onsets
Common initial clusters include:
*   `s` + plosive: `sp-`, `st-`, `sc-`
*   `s` + liquid/nasal: `sl-`, `sr-`, `sn-`, `sm-`
*   Plosive + `l` or `r`: `pl-`, `pr-`, `bl-`, `br-`, `cl-`, `cr-`, `gl-`, `gr-`, `tl-`, `tr-`, `dl-`, `dr-`
*   Other initial clusters: `cn-`, `gn-`, `mn-`, `tn-`

<Dialect region="Ulster/Connacht">
In northern dialects, clusters like `cn-`, `gn-`, and `mn-` are often pronounced with an 'r' sound: `[kr-]`, `[gr-]`, `[mr-]`.
*   *cnoc* ("hill") is pronounced `[kruk]`
*   *mná* ("women") is pronounced `[mrɑː]`
</Dialect>

### Syllable Codas
Common final clusters include:
*   Liquid/nasal + plosive: `-lt`, `-nt`, `-rt`, `-mp`, `-rc`
*   Other final clusters: `-cht`, `-rd`, `-rn`, `-st`, `-sc`, `-ng`

<Dialect region="Donegal">
In parts of Donegal, the final cluster `-cht` is pronounced as `[rt]`.
</Dialect>

In some clusters, the quality of the first consonant is fixed. For example, in `sp-` and `-cht`, the first consonant (`s` and `ch`) is always broad, even if the following consonant is slender.
*   *spléach* `[sp′l′eːx]` (The `s` is broad, but the `pl` is slender)
*   *boichte* `[boxt′ə]` (The `ch` is broad, but the `t` is slender)

## The Vowels

Irish vowels can be long or short, and are also classified as broad or slender.

*   **Long Vowels**: `[aː]`, `[eː]`, `[iː]`, `[oː]`, `[uː]`. These are written with an acute accent (`á, é, í, ó, ú`) or as digraphs (`ao`, `ae`).
*   **Short Vowels**: `[a]`, `[e]`, `[i]`, `[o]`, `[u]`, and the neutral vowel `[ə]` (schwa).

Vowels are also categorised by quality, which must match adjacent consonants according to spelling rules.
*   **Broad Vowels**: `a, o, u` (and the digraphs `ao`, `ae`). These are back vowels.
*   **Slender Vowels**: `e, i`. These are front vowels.

The exact pronunciation of a vowel can vary depending on the surrounding consonants and the dialect. For example, `á` is pronounced as a back `[ɑː]` in Connemara but as a front `[æː]` in Ulster. The digraph `ao` is pronounced `[iː]` in Connacht and Ulster, but `[ɛː]` in Munster.

### Nasalisation
In some dialects, vowels can be nasalised, particularly when adjacent to `m`, `n`, `ng`, or where an `mh` appears in the spelling. The nasalisation is often subtle. It can be meaning-distinguishing:
*   *ádh* `[ɑː]` - luck
*   *áth* `[ɑ̃ː]` - ford
*   *amhras* `[ãurəs]` - doubt
*   *abhras* `[aurəs]` - handiwork

### The Neutral Vowel (Schwa)
The schwa `[ə]` is a very common, short, unstressed vowel sound, like the 'a' in "sofa".
*   **Reduction**: In unstressed syllables, all short vowels are typically reduced to schwa.
    *   *baile* ("town") `[bal′ə]`
    *   *leathan* ("broad") `[l′ahən]`
*   **Elision**: The schwa is often dropped (elided) in fast speech when it comes next to another vowel.
    *   *Tá sé ag ól* ("He is drinking") is pronounced `[tɑː s′eːgoːl]`, not `[tɑː s′eː əg oːl]`.
    *   *baile álainn* ("beautiful town") is pronounced `[bal′ɑːlən′]`, not `[bal′ə ɑːlən′]`.

## The Epenthetic Vowel

In all dialects, an unwritten schwa sound is inserted to break up certain consonant clusters. This is called an epenthetic or "helping" vowel. It occurs after a **short vowel** between a liquid (`l, n, r`) and one of the following consonants:

| | **b** | **bh** | **ch** | **g** | **m** | **mh** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **l** | lb | lbh | lch | lg | lm | lmh |
| **n** | nb | nbh | nch | (ng)* | nm | nmh |
| **r** | rb | rbh | rch | rg | rm | rmh |

*   *gorm* ("blue") is pronounced `[gorəm]`
*   *ainm* ("name") is pronounced `[an′əm′]`
*   *dearg* ("red") is pronounced `[d′arəg]`

This does not happen after a long vowel or diphthong.
*   *téarma* `[t′eːrmə]` (no epenthetic vowel)
*   *dualgas* `[duəlgəs]` (no epenthetic vowel)

<Dialect region="Munster">
Epenthesis is more widespread in Munster. It can occur in compounds (`an-mhaith` pronounced `[anəvah]`) and in other clusters like `rn` (`dorn` pronounced `[dorən]`).
</Dialect>

## Diphthongs

Diphthongs are gliding vowels where two vowel sounds are combined in a single syllable. The main Irish diphthongs are:
*   **[au]**: as in *abhainn* `[aun′]` ("river")
*   **[ai]**: as in *radharc* `[rairk]` ("sight")
*   **[uə]**: as in *fuar* `[fuər]` ("cold")
*   **[iə]**: as in *ciall* `[k′iəl]` ("sense")

## Glides

Glides are transitional sounds that occur between consonants and vowels of different qualities (i.e., between broad and slender). They are a natural result of moving the tongue from one position to another and are not usually written in transcription.

*   **Palatal Offglide (`[j]`)**: Occurs after a slender consonant and before a broad vowel.
    *   *beo* ("alive") is phonetically `[b′joː]`
*   **Velar Offglide (`[ɰ]` or `[w]`)**: Occurs after a broad consonant and before a slender vowel.
    *   *buí* ("yellow") is phonetically `[bwiː]`
*   **Palatal Onglide (`[ĭ]`)**: Occurs after a broad vowel and before a slender consonant.
    *   *áit* ("place") is phonetically `[ɑːĭt′]`
*   **Velar Onglide (`[ə̆]`)**: Occurs after a slender vowel and before a broad consonant.
    *   *díol* ("to sell") is phonetically `[d′iːə̆l]`

## The Syllable

An Irish syllable consists of a vowel (nucleus), an optional beginning (onset), and an optional end (coda). The quality of the consonants in the onset and coda determines the quality of a short vowel in the nucleus, following the `Caol le caol...` rule.

A unique feature is the "zero-onset". A word that begins with a vowel orthographically still has an inherent broad or slender quality. This becomes apparent when a prefix is added.
*   *eochair* ("key") `[oxər′]` has a slender onset. When `d'` ("your") is added, it becomes slender: *d'eochair* `[d′oxər′]`.
*   *oíche* ("night") `[iːx′ə]` has a broad onset. When `d'` is added, it becomes broad: *d'oíche* `[diːx′ə]`.

## Prosody

Prosody covers features like stress, rhythm, and intonation.

### Word Stress
Word stress patterns are a major point of difference between dialects.

**Connacht and Ulster**
Stress is almost always on the **first syllable** of a word.
*   *fuinneog* ("window") `[ˈfin′oːg]`
*   *cailín* ("girl") `[ˈkal′iːn′]`

A few adverbs and loanwords are exceptions, with stress on the second syllable (e.g., *aréir* `[əˈreːr′]`, *tobac* `[toˈbac]`).

**Munster**
The stress rules are more complex. Stress falls on the:
*   **First syllable** if all syllables have short vowels.
*   **Second syllable** if it contains a long vowel or diphthong.
    *   *cailín* `[kaˈl′iːn′]`
    *   *fuinneog* `[fiˈn′oːg]`
*   **Second syllable** if it ends in `-ach(t)` and the first syllable is short.
    *   *salach* ("dirty") `[səˈlax]`

This stress shift can cause the first syllable's vowel to be reduced to schwa or even disappear entirely.
*   *fuinneog* `[fəˈn′oːg]` can become `[f′n′oːg]`

### Sentence Stress
In a sentence, certain words are always unstressed, including:
*   Personal and possessive pronouns (`mé`, `mo`, `sé`, `a`)
*   Prepositions (`ag`, `ar`, `le`)
*   The article (`an`)
*   Verbal particles (`an`, `ní`, `go`)
*   The copula (`is`, `ba`)

Generally, nouns are stressed more than verbs, and objects are stressed more than subjects. Adjectives and adverbs are stressed more strongly than the words they modify.

### Intonation
Irish typically has a falling intonation at the end of a sentence, even for questions. A question is marked by a particle at the beginning (`An...?`), not by rising intonation at the end.

### Speech Tempo
Irish is often spoken very quickly, with words running together. This fluent connection between words is the source of the initial mutations (lenition and eclipsis) that are a core feature of the grammar.
