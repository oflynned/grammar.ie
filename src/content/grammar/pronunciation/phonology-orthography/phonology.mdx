Phonology describes the sound system of a language. It is distinct from orthography, which is the system of spelling used to represent those sounds. This page focuses on the sounds of Irish (`foghair na Gaeilge`), while spelling conventions are covered in the Orthography section.

## Transcription of Irish Sounds

The sounds of Irish can be represented using the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA). However, a precise phonetic transcription would be overly complex and vary significantly between dialects. A more practical approach is a phonological representation that allows for dialectal variants of a single phoneme.

For this purpose, a cross-dialectal transcription system, often called "modified IPA," is used. It employs few special characters and has the following key features:

*   **Slender (palatalised) consonants** are marked with a prime (`′`). In standard IPA, this is often shown with a superscript `j` (`[ʲ]`), but this modified system uses the prime for consistency. For example, `[k′]` is used instead of IPA `[c]`, and `[x′]` instead of IPA `[ç]`.
*   **Broad (velarised) consonants** are unmarked. In standard IPA, they might be marked with a superscript gamma (`[ˠ]`), but in this system, `[b]` is used instead of `[bˠ]`. It is worth noting that Irish broad consonants are often phonetically neutral rather than truly velarised. The broad dental sounds `t` and `d` are represented simply as `[t]` and `[d]`, not the more specific IPA `[t̪]` and `[d̪]`.
*   **Aspiration** of voiceless plosives (`k`, `p`, `t`), which is common in Irish (similar to English), is not marked. So, `[k]` is used instead of `[kʰ]`.
*   **Tense and lax consonants** `l`, `n`, and `r` have special symbols. Tense forms are `[ʟ]`, `[ʟ′]`, `[ɴ]`, `[ɴ′]`, `[ʀ]`, while lax forms are `[l]`, `[l′]`, `[n]`, `[n′]`, `[r]`, `[r′]`. These symbols have different meanings in standard IPA.
*   **Fricatives** like `[x]` resemble the `ch` in Scottish "loch" and are sometimes closer to a uvular fricative `[χ]`. The sounds `[f]` and `[v]`, originally bilabial (using both lips), are now often labiodental (lower lip and upper teeth), but the symbols `[f]` and `[v]` are used for both.
*   **Vowels** are transcribed simply, even though their realisation varies. Short vowels are generally more open than their long counterparts. For example, short `o` is transcribed as `[o]` even though it is phonetically closer to `[ɔ]`. The same applies to other short vowels: `[u]` for `[ʊ]`, `[e]` for `[ɛ]`, and `[i]` for `[ɪ]`. The schwa sound `[ə]` is used for the neutral vowel in unstressed syllables.

Audio examples of Irish sounds are available from external resources such as [Fios Feasa: The Sounds of Irish](http://www.fiosfeasa.com/feasa/more.php?id=10_0_3_0_M) (Munster Dialect) and [www.phouka.com](http://www.phouka.com/fraoch/pronunciation.htm) (Ulster Dialect).

## The Consonants (`na consain`)

Consonants are arguably more significant than vowels in Irish phonology, as vowels often adapt to the quality of adjacent consonants. The most important feature is the strict distinction between broad and slender consonants.

Consonants are classified by their place and manner of articulation. All consonants except `[h]` exist in two phonemic variants: broad (velarised) and slender (palatalised).

### Place of Articulation

*   **Labials (`consain liopacha`)**: Formed with the lips.
    *   Plosives: `[p]`, `[p′]`, `[b]`, `[b′]`
    *   Fricatives: `[f]`, `[f′]`, `[v]`, `[v′]`
    *   Nasals: `[m]`, `[m′]`
*   **Dentals (`consain déadacha`)**: Formed at the teeth. Broad `[t]`, `[d]`, `[ʟ]`, `[ɴ]` are dental.
    *   Plosives: `[t]`, `[d]`
    *   Liquids: `[ʟ]`
    *   Nasals: `[ɴ]`
*   **Alveolars (`consain ailbheolacha`)**: Formed at the alveolar ridge.
    *   Fricatives: `[s]`, `[z]` (The voiced `[z]` only occurs in loanwords like `zú` or as an eclipsis of `s` in some dialects).
    *   Liquids: `[l]`, `[l′]`, `[r]`, `[ʀ]`
    *   Nasals: `[n]`, `[n′]`
*   **Alveolopalatals (`consain iarailbheolacha`)**: Formed between the alveolar ridge and the hard palate.
    *   Plosives: `[t′]`, `[d′]`
    *   Fricatives: `[s′]` (like English "sh"), `[z′]` (like the 's' in "pleasure")
    *   Liquids: `[ʟ′]`
    *   Nasals: `[ɴ′]`
*   **Palatals (`consain charballacha`)**: Formed at the hard palate.
    *   Plosives: `[k′]`, `[g′]`
    *   Fricatives: `[x′]` (like German `ch` in `ich`), `[γ′]` (similar to `[j]`, the 'y' in "yes")
    *   Liquids: `[r′]`
    *   Nasals: `[ŋ′]`
*   **Velars (`consain choguasacha`)**: Formed at the soft palate (velum).
    *   Plosives: `[k]`, `[g]`
    *   Fricatives: `[x]` (like Scottish `ch` in `loch`), `[γ]` (a voiced version of `[x]`)
    *   Nasals: `[ŋ]` (like `ng` in "sing")
    *   Approximants: `[w]` (in Connacht and Ulster as the broad form of `v`)
*   **Glottals (`consain ghlotasacha`)**: Formed in the glottis.
    *   Fricatives: `[h]`
    *   Plosives: `[ʔ]` (the glottal stop, rare in modern spoken Irish but found in traditional singing)

### Broad and Slender Consonants (`consain leathana agus consain chaola`)

All Irish consonants except `[h]` have two contrasting forms: a neutral-to-velarised **broad** form and a palatalised **slender** form. This distinction is phonemic, meaning it can change the meaning of a word. In transcription, slender consonants are marked with a prime (`′`).

| **Broad Consonants** | `[p]` | `[b]` | `[v]` | `[f]` | `[m]` | `[t]` | `[d]` | `[n]` | `[ɴ]` | `[l]` | `[ʟ]` | `[r]` | `[s]` | `[z]` | `[k]` | `[g]` | `[x]` | `[γ]` | `[ŋ]` |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Slender Consonants** | `[p′]` | `[b′]` | `[v′]` | `[f′]` | `[m′]` | `[t′]` | `[d′]` | `[n′]` | `[ɴ′]` | `[l′]` | `[ʟ′]` | `[r′]` | `[s′]` | `[z′]` | `[k′]` | `[g′]` | `[x′]` | `[γ′]` | `[ŋ′]` |

The difference between broad and slender pairs is very audible. For example, broad `[s]` is a standard 's' sound, while slender `[s′]` is like the English 'sh' sound (`[ʃ]`). The distinction is less pronounced for labial consonants like `[p]` and `[p′]`.

This phonological rule has a direct impact on spelling, governed by the principle `caol le caol agus leathan le leathan` ("slender with slender and broad with broad"). This means a consonant's quality (broad or slender) is indicated by the vowels written next to it.
*   *sean* `[s′an]` (old) - `s` is slender, flanked by the slender vowel `e`.
*   *san* `[san]` (in the) - `s` is broad, flanked by the broad vowel `a`.

This system is similar to the "hard" and "soft" consonant distinction in Slavic languages like Russian.

### Tense Consonants (`na consain theanna`)

The consonants `l`, `n`, and `r` historically had a four-way distinction based on both the broad/slender quality and a tense/lax quality. The tense forms (fortis) are more precisely articulated than the lax forms (lenis).

| | **Tense** | **Lax** | | |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **l** | `[ʟ]` | `[ʟ′]` | `[l]` | `[l′]` |
| **n** | `[ɴ]` | `[ɴ′]` | `[n]` | `[n′]` |
| **r** | `[ʀ]` | `[ʀ′]` | `[r]` | `[r′]` |

In writing, tense `l, n, r` are indicated by a double letter (`ll, nn, rr`) or when followed by certain other consonants (`rt, rd, nt`). At the beginning of a word, a single letter represents the unlenited (tense) form.

The preservation of these distinctions varies by dialect:
*   **Ulster**: Preserves most distinctions, except for `[ʀ′]`.
*   **Connacht**: Has three variants for `l` and `n`, and two for `r`.
*   **Munster**: Generally only has a two-way broad/slender distinction, with the formerly tense variants becoming the standard broad forms.
*   **Lárchanúint (Standard)**: Also simplifies to a two-way distinction.

#### Influence on Vowels

Tense consonants at the end of a stressed syllable can lengthen or diphthongise the preceding vowel. This is especially prominent in dialects that have lost the consonant distinction itself.
*   **Munster**: Vowels are often diphthongised. *donn* (brown) is `[daun]`; *ceann* (head) is `[k′aun]`.
*   **Connemara**: Vowels are often lengthened. *barr* (top) is `[bɑːr]`.
*   **Ulster & Mayo**: Vowels generally remain short, as the tense consonant sound is preserved. *donn* is `[doɴ]`.

This lengthening does not occur if a vowel follows the tense consonant, as the consonant then starts the next syllable: *barr* `[bɑːr]` but *barra* `[bɑ.rə]`. However, if a consonant follows, the vowel remains long: *fillte* `[f′iːʟ′.t′ə]`.

The consonants `m` and `ng` are always considered "tense" and have a similar lengthening effect on preceding vowels in southern dialects.

### Consonant Clusters (`na cairn chonsan`)

Irish allows various consonant clusters at the beginning (onset) and end (coda) of a syllable.

**Syllable Onsets** can have up to three consonants:
*   Two consonants: `sp-`, `st-`, `sc-`, `sl-`, `sn-`, `sm-`, `pl-`, `pr-`, `bl-`, `br-`, `cl-`, `cr-`, `gl-`, `gr-`, `tl-`, `tr-`, `dl-`, `dr-`, `cn-`, `gn-`, `mn-`.
*   Three consonants: `spl-`, `spr-`, `scl-`, `scr-`, `str-`.

In northern dialects, `cn-`, `gn-`, `mn-` are pronounced `[kr-]`, `[gr-]`, `[mr-]`.

**Syllable Codas** typically have up to two consonants:
*   Examples: `-lt`, `-nt`, `-rt`, `-mp`, `-lc`, `-rc`, `-rd`, `-rn`, `-st`, `-sc`, `-cht`.
*   Groups like `-lb`, `-rb`, `-lg`, `-rm` are broken up by an epenthetic vowel.

The quality (broad or slender) generally applies to the entire cluster. However, in clusters like `sp-`, `sm-`, `-cht`, and `-rd`, the first consonant is always broad, even if the following one is slender. For example, *boichte* `[boxt′ə]`.

## The Vowels (`na gutaí`)

*   **Long Vowels (`gutaí fada`)**: `[aː]`, `[eː]`, `[iː]`, `[oː]`, `[uː]`. These are written with an acute accent (`á, é, í, ó, ú`) or as digraphs (`ao`, `ae`).
*   **Short Vowels (`gutaí gairide`)**: `[a]`, `[e]`, `[i]`, `[o]`, `[u]`, and the neutral vowel `[ə]`. Short vowels are phonetically more open than their long counterparts.

Vowels are also classified as broad or slender:
*   **Broad Vowels (`gutaí leathana`)**: The back vowels `a, o, u`. The digraph `ao` is considered broad for spelling purposes, though it is pronounced as a front vowel (`[iː]` or `[ɛː]`).
*   **Slender Vowels (`gutaí caola`)**: The front vowels `e, i`.

Vowel pronunciation is heavily influenced by surrounding consonants and varies significantly by dialect. For instance, `á` is pronounced `[ɑː]` in Connemara but `[æː]` in Ulster.

### Nasalisation (`an tsrónaíl`)

In some dialects, vowels can be nasalised, especially when adjacent to `mh` or other nasal consonants (`m, n, ng`). This nasalisation is less pronounced than in French and is not always consistent. It can be phonemic, distinguishing words that are otherwise identical.
*   *cóir* `[coːr′]` (right) vs. *os comhair* `[os kõːr′]` (opposite)
*   *ádh* `[ɑː]` (luck) vs. *áth* `[ɑ̃ː]` (ford)

### The Neutral Vowel or Schwa (`an guta neodrach`)

The schwa `[ə]` is a short, unstressed, mid-central vowel, similar to the 'a' in "about".
*   **Reduction**: In unstressed syllables, all short vowels are typically reduced to `[ə]`. For example, *baile* (town) is `[bal′ə]`.
*   **Elision**: A schwa at the beginning or end of a word is often dropped in connected speech, especially when it meets another vowel. *Tá sé ag ól* (He is drinking) becomes `[tɑː s′eːgoːl]`.

### The Epenthetic Vowel (`an guta cúnta`)

An unwritten schwa-like vowel is inserted between certain consonant combinations to ease pronunciation. This occurs after a short vowel between a liquid (`l, n, r`) and a following `b, bh, ch, g, m, mh`.

| | **b** | **bh** | **ch** | **g** | **m** | **mh** |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **l** | `lb` | `lbh` | `lch` | `lg` | `lm` | `lmh` |
| **n** | `nb` | `nbh` | `nch` | (`ng`)* | `nm` | `nmh` |
| **r** | `rb` | `rbh` | `rch` | `rg` | `rm` | `rmh` |

Examples:
*   *gorm* (blue) is pronounced `[gorəm]`
*   *ainm* (name) is pronounced `[an′əm′]`
*   *dearg* (red) is pronounced `[d′arəg]`

This epenthesis does not occur after long vowels or diphthongs. Munster dialects use epenthesis more frequently, for instance in compounds (`an-mhaith` -> `[anəvah]`) and with the cluster `rn` (`dorn` -> `[dorən]`).

### The Diphthongs (`na défhogair`)

Irish has four main diphthongs (gliding vowels):
*   `[au]`, as in *abhainn* `[aun′]` (river)
*   `[ai]`, as in *radharc* `[rairk]` (sight)
*   `[uə]`, as in *fuar* `[fuər]` (cold)
*   `[iə]`, as in *ciall* `[k′iəl]` (sense)

## The Glides (`na sleamhnóga`)

Glides are transitional sounds that occur between consonants and vowels of opposite quality (i.e., between broad and slender). They are not explicitly marked in transcription but are an automatic feature of pronunciation.

*   **Palatal Offglide (`[j]`)**: Occurs after a slender consonant and before a broad vowel.
    *   *beo* `[b′oː]` is phonetically `[b′joː]`
*   **Velar Offglide (`[ɰ]` or `[w]`)**: Occurs after a broad consonant and before a slender vowel.
    *   *buí* `[biː]` is phonetically `[bwiː]`
*   **Palatal Onglide (`[ĭ]`)**: Occurs after a broad vowel and before a slender consonant.
    *   *áit* `[ɑːt′]` is phonetically `[ɑːĭt′]`
*   **Velar Onglide (`[ə̆]`)**: Occurs after a slender vowel and before a broad consonant.
    *   *díol* `[d′iːl]` is phonetically `[d′iːə̆l]`

## The Syllable (`an siolla`)

An Irish syllable consists of a vowel (nucleus), an optional consonant onset, and an optional consonant coda. A key rule is that within a syllable with a short vowel, the onset and coda must have the same quality: `C′V′C′` (slender) or `CVC` (broad). This constraint does not apply to long vowels.

Even words that begin with a vowel have an inherent broad or slender quality at their start (a "zero-onset"), which becomes apparent when a prefix is added.
*   *eochair* `[oxər′]` (key, slender start) -> *d'eochair* `[d′oxər′]` (your key)
*   *oíche* `[iːx′ə]` (night, broad start) -> *d'oíche* `[diːx′ə]` (your night)

## Prosody (`an phrosóid`)

Prosody covers features like stress, intonation, and rhythm.

### Word Stress (`an bhéim ghutha`)

*   **Connacht and Ulster**: Stress is almost always on the **first syllable**.
*   **Munster**: The rules are more complex. Stress falls on the **second syllable** if it contains a long vowel or diphthong (*cailín* `[kaˈl′iːn′]`) or ends in `-ach(t)` (*salach* `[səˈlax]`). Otherwise, it is on the first syllable. This can lead to the reduction or loss of the first vowel.

### Sentence Stress (`an bhéim abairte`)

Certain word classes are always unstressed in Irish, including personal pronouns, prepositions, verbal particles, conjunctions, the article, and the copula. Emphasis is achieved not through stress but by using emphatic suffixes (*mise*, *eisean*) or by changing the sentence structure.

In general:
*   Nouns are stressed more than verbs.
*   Objects are stressed more than subjects.
*   Adjectives and adverbs are stressed more than the words they modify.

### Intonation (`an thúin`)

Irish typically has a falling intonation, even in questions. Unlike English, where a rising pitch indicates a question, Irish uses an interrogative particle at the beginning of the sentence.

### Speech Tempo (`an luas cainte`)

Irish is often spoken very quickly, with words blending together. This fluent connection between words (sandhi) is the historical origin of the initial mutations like lenition and eclipsis.
