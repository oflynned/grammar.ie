Irish spelling can seem confusing at first. Its orthography is etymological, meaning it often reflects how words were pronounced centuries ago, similar to English or French. This system must also account for significant pronunciation differences between dialects.

Despite this, Irish spelling follows consistent rules. The most important of these is the distinction between "slender" and "broad" sounds, which leads to the use of extra, often silent, vowel letters. Once you understand these rules, the connection between spelling and pronunciation becomes much clearer than in English or French.

## The Alphabet (`an Aibítir`)

The modern Irish alphabet uses the Latin script and consists of 18 native letters.

-   **Vowels (`gutaí`)**: `a, e, i, o, u`.
-   **Consonants (`consain`)**: `b, c, d, f, g, h, l, m, n, p, r, s, t`.

The acute accent, or `síneadh fada`, marks a vowel as long: `á, é, í, ó, ú`.

The letters `j, k, q, v, w, x, y, z` appear only in loanwords and names. `k` is otherwise replaced by `c`, and `q` only appears as `qu`. The letter `h` is primarily used to mark lenition (e.g., `bh`, `ch`) or as a prefix before vowels (`h-aon`).

### Traditional Script (`an Cló Gaelach`)

Until the mid-20th century, Irish was commonly written in a distinct insular script known as `an cló Gaelach`. In this script, lenition was marked with an overdot (`ponc séimhithe`) instead of a following `h`, for example, `ċ` instead of `ch`. This practice originated in Old Irish manuscripts where a dot (`punctum delens`) was used to mark letters for deletion. Since lenited `f` is silent and lenited `s` is pronounced as `h`, the dot was a natural fit. This was later extended to all lenitable consonants.

The Gaelic script also omits the dot over the letter `i`, which makes it easier to distinguish between `i` and `í`.

### Letter Names

The official names for the letters are: `á, bé, cé, dé, é, eif, gé, héis, í, jé, cá, eil, eim, ein, ó, pé, cú, ear, eas, té, ú, vé, wae, ex, yé, zae`. However, in everyday speech, the English names are more common.

### Abbreviations

A common abbreviation still in use is the Tironian 'et' (`⁊`), which stands for `agus` (and). As the character is not always available, it is sometimes replaced with a `7`. For example, `7rl.` is used for `srl.` (`agus araile`), meaning "and so on". Another common abbreviation is `.i.`, from the Latin `id est`, meaning "that is" (Irish: `eadhon`).

## `Caol le caol agus leathan le leathan`

This phrase, meaning "slender with slender and broad with broad," is the fundamental principle of Irish spelling. It governs the relationship between consonants and their surrounding vowels.

### Vowel and Consonant Quality

-   **Slender vowels (`gutaí caola`)** are the front vowels: `e, é, i, í`.
-   **Broad vowels (`gutaí leathana`)** are the back vowels: `a, á, o, ó, u, ú`.
-   The digraphs `ae` and `ao` are pronounced as front vowels but are grammatically **broad**.

A consonant's quality (slender or broad) is determined by the vowels next to it.
-   **Slender consonants (`consain chaola`)** are surrounded by slender vowels.
-   **Broad consonants (`consain leathana`)** are surrounded by broad vowels.

The quality of a consonant is often crucial for a word's meaning. For example, `bán` [baːn] (white) has a broad `n`, while `báin` [baːn′] (of white) has a slender `n'`. To make this distinction clear in writing, a consonant must be flanked by vowels of the same quality.

| Consonant Quality | Preceding Vowel | Following Vowel |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Slender | Slender | Slender |
| Broad | Broad | Broad |

This rule means that a letter combination like `abe` is impossible because the `b` would be between a broad `a` and a slender `e`. To resolve this, a silent vowel is inserted to ensure the consonant is flanked by vowels of the same quality. These silent vowels act as markers for consonant quality.

### Inserting Silent Vowels

-   An `i` is inserted between a broad vowel and a slender consonant.
    -   *báid* [baːd′] (boats)
-   An `e` is inserted between a slender consonant and a broad `a` or `o`.
    -   *fear* [f′ar] (man)
    -   *teo* [t′oː] (warmer)
-   After a slender consonant, `ó` is written as `eo` (without the `fada`).
    -   *éireoidh mé* [eːr′oːiː meː] (I will rise)
-   An `a` is inserted between `é` and a broad consonant.
    -   *féar* [f′eːr] (grass)
-   An `o` is inserted between `í` and a broad consonant.
    -   *bíodh* [b′iːx] (let it be)
-   An `i` is inserted between a slender consonant and `u`.
    -   *fliuch* [f′l′ux] (wet)

### Examples of Meaning Change

The distinction between broad and slender consonants can change a word's meaning, number, or grammatical case.

| Broad Form | Meaning | Slender Form | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `bán` | white | `báin` | of white (gen.) |
| `bó` | cow | `bóin` | cow (dat.) |
| `ceann` | head | `cinn` | heads |
| `glas` | green | `glais` | of green (gen.) |
| `poll` | hole | `poill` | holes |
| `cat` | cat | `cait` | cats |
| `mac` | son | `mic` | sons |
| `fear` | man | `fir` | men |

### Vowel Marking at the Start of a Word

Some words that begin with a vowel have a silent prefixed letter, such as the `e` in `eochair` (key) or the `o` in `oíche` (night). This letter seems redundant until a preceding word, like the article `an`, attaches to it. The silent vowel then determines the quality of the final consonant of the preceding word: *an eochair* [ən′oxər′], *an oíche* [əniːx′ə].

### Exceptions to the Rule

The `caol le caol` rule is not strictly applied in compound words (e.g., `neamhbheo` = `neamh` + `beo`). Adverbs like `ansin` and `anseo`, and prepositional pronouns like `ina`, are historical compounds and also do not follow the rule. Another exception is `ospidéal` (hospital), where the `s` in `sp` is always broad, even if the `p` is slender.

## Pronunciation and Spelling (`Fuaimniú agus Litriú`)

While the `caol le caol` rule provides a framework, the exact pronunciation of letter combinations varies significantly between dialects.

### Vowel Digraphs

-   **`ao`**: Pronounced [iː] in Connacht, Ulster, and the Standard, but [ɛː] in Munster. Despite its sound, it is a **broad** vowel group. When a slender consonant follows, it is written `aoi` and pronounced [iː] in all dialects.
-   **`ae`**: Always pronounced [eː]. It is also a **broad** vowel group. When a slender consonant follows, it is written `aei`, as in `Gaeilge` [geːl′g′ə].

### Other Vowel Combinations

-   **`ei`, `éi`**: A silent `i` is conventionally written after `e` or `é` before a slender consonant, e.g., `Éire` [eːr′ə].
-   **`ea`**: The `e` marks the preceding consonant as slender. The standard pronunciation is [a], as in `fear` [f′ar]. In Connacht, it is often pronounced [æː].
-   **`éa`**: Pronounced [eː] in Connacht and Ulster, but often [iə] in Munster.
-   **`ai`**: The `i` marks the following consonant as slender. The standard pronunciation is [a]. In Connacht, it is often [æː].
-   **`eo`**: Usually a long [oː], as in the future ending `-eoidh`. A few words have a short [o], such as `deoch` (drink) and `seo` (this).
-   **`io`**: Can be pronounced [i] or [o], and sometimes [u] in Connacht and Munster.
-   **`oi`**: Can be pronounced [o], [i], or [e] depending on the word and dialect, e.g., `oifig` (office).

### Vowel and Consonant Combinations

-   **`abh, odh, ogh`**: At the start or middle of a word, often [au] in Connacht/Munster and [oː] in Ulster.
-   **`amh`**: Similar to `abh`, but can also be [av] in parts of Connemara.
-   **`adh, agh`**: At the start or middle of a word, often [ai] in Munster/Connacht and [eː] in Ulster.
-   **`aidh, aigh`**: Pronounced [ai].
-   **`-th-`**: Pronounced [h], but often becomes silent between vowels in Cois Fharraige, causing the vowels to merge and lengthen: `athair` [ahər′] > [æːr′].
-   **`-ch-`**: Slender `ch` [x′] is often pronounced [h] between vowels. In Cois Fharraige, this `h` can also become silent: `oíche` [iːx′ə] > [iːhə] > [iː].

### Nasalisation (`an t-éirí sronach`)

In Connacht especially, a preceding nasal consonant (`n`, `m`) can raise a vowel's pronunciation.
-   `seomra` (room) is pronounced [s′uːmrə] (from [s′oːmrə]).
-   `déanamh` (doing) is pronounced [d′iːnə] (from [d′eːnə]).

### Pronunciation of Word Endings

The pronunciation of common endings varies greatly by dialect.

#### Endings `-(a)idh` and `-(a)igh`

These endings appear on verbs, nouns, and prepositional pronouns.

| Context | Munster | Connemara | Donegal | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Verbs before a pronoun | [ə] | [ə] | [ə] | *Shalaigh sé na bróga.* (He dirtied the shoes.) |
| Verbs before a noun | [ig′] | [ə] | [iː] | *Shalaigh an fear na bróga.* (The man dirtied the shoes.) |
| Nouns (genitive/plural) | [ig′] | [ə] | [iː] | *na marcaigh* (the riders) |

#### Ending `-(e)adh`

This ending has several grammatical functions with distinct pronunciations.

| Function | Munster | Connemara | Donegal | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Imperfect (analytic) | [əx] | [əx] / [əṭ]* | [uː] / [əṭ]* | *Ghlanadh sé.* (He used to clean.) |
| Preterite (autonomous) | [əx] / [əg] | [uː] | [uː] | *Glanadh an bord.* (The table was cleaned.) |
| Verbal Noun | [ə] | [ə] | [uː] | *Tá sé ag glanadh.* (He is cleaning.) |

*\*The [əṭ] pronunciation occurs before pronouns beginning with `s-`.*

#### Ending `-(e)abh` and `-(e)amh`

This is a common noun ending.

| Munster | Connemara | Donegal | Standard | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| [əv] | [ə] | [uː] | [əv] | `talamh` (ground), `ollamh` (professor) |

#### Ending `-(a)ibh` and `-(a)imh`

`-(a)ibh` is the old dative plural ending for nouns.

| Munster | Connacht | Donegal | Standard | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| [iv′] | [ə] or [iː] | [iv′] | [iv′] | `do na fearaibh` (to the men), `Gaillimh` (Galway) |

#### Pronunciation of `-f-` in Future and Conditional

The future marker `-f-` is rarely pronounced as [f]. Its sound depends on the preceding consonant.

-   **After `b, d, g`**: The `-f-` is pronounced [h], which devoices the preceding consonant to [p], [t], or [k]. The [h] sound itself is then dropped.
    -   *tuigfidh mé* (I will understand) sounds like *tuicidh mé* [tik′ə m′eː].
-   **After `bh, mh`**: The `-f-` devoices the preceding [v] sound to [f].
    -   *scríobhfaidh mé* (I will write) is pronounced [s′k′r′iːfə m′eː].
-   **After `c, ch, p, s, t`**: The `-f-` is completely silent.
    -   *bacfaidh mé* (I will prevent) sounds like *bacaidh mé* [bakə m′eː].
-   **After `l, n, r, m`**: The `-f-` is pronounced [h]. In Cois Fharraige, it is silent.
    -   *glanfaidh mé* (I will clean) is pronounced [glanhə m′e:].
-   **After a vowel**: The `-f-` is pronounced [h] in Connemara and often [f] in Munster and South Donegal.
    -   *léifidh mé* (I will read) is [l′eːhə m′eː] in Connemara.

In synthetic endings like `-fá` (you would) and `-far` (one will), the `-f-` is more often pronounced [f].

## Capitalisation with Prefixes

When a word requiring a capital letter takes a prefix (`t-`, `n-`, `h-`) or is eclipsed, the prefix or eclipsing letter is written in lowercase, followed by the capital letter.

-   *Poblacht na **hÉ**ireann* (Republic of Ireland)
-   *Tír na **nÓ**g* (Land of Youth)
-   *i **gC**onnemara* (in Connemara)

When the entire word is in uppercase, the prefix is often written as a small capital.
-   POBLACHT NA HÉIREANN

## The Spelling Reform (`An Caighdeán Oifigiúil`)

A major spelling reform, finalised in 1957, aimed to simplify the orthography and bring it closer to the most common pronunciations. This standardised spelling is known as `An Caighdeán Oifigiúil` (The Official Standard).

The reform primarily involved removing letters that had become silent in most dialects and standardising certain letter combinations.

| Old Spelling | New Spelling | English |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `oidhche` | `oíche` | night |
| `meadhon` | `meán` | middle |
| `timcheall` | `timpeall` | around |
| `Gaedhilge` | `Gaeilge` | Irish |
| `Beurla` | `Béarla` | English |
| `siubhail` | `siúil` | to walk |
| `cosamhail` | `cosúil` | similar |

The reform has been criticised for obscuring etymology and dialectal pronunciations. For example, the old spelling `tráigh` (beach) reflects the [g] sound still heard at the end of the word in Munster and Ulster, whereas the modern `trá` only reflects the pronunciation in parts of Connemara. It also created new complexities, such as the verb ending `-(a)íonn`, which replaced the universal `-(e)ann` for a certain class of verbs.

## Punctuation (`an Phoncaíocht`)

-   **Comma (`an chamóg`)**: Used to set off non-essential relative clauses and fronted subordinate clauses. It is not used before `agus` (and) or `nó` (or) in a list.
-   **Semicolon (`an leathstad`)**: Used to connect two closely related main clauses.
-   **Exclamation Mark (`an comhartha uaillbhreasa`)**: Used less frequently than in some other languages. Imperative sentences can end with a full stop.
