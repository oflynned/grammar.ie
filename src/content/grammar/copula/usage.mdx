In Irish, there are two different words for the verb "to be": `bí` and `is`. The word `is` is known as the copula. It is used to link a subject with a predicate that is a noun or a pronoun, essentially defining or classifying what the subject *is*. Understanding the difference between `bí` and `is` is a fundamental step in forming correct Irish sentences.

## The Role of the Copula

The main job of the copula is to connect a subject to a noun or pronoun that describes it. This is different from the verb `bí` (which appears as `tá` in the present tense), which typically describes a state, condition, or location.

For example, to say "I am ill", you use `tá`:
*   *Tá mé tinn.* - I am ill.

However, to say "I am a doctor", you must use the copula `is`.

<Warning>
You cannot use `tá` to link a subject to a noun predicate. The following sentence is incorrect:

| Avoid |
| :--- |
| *Tá mé dochtúir.* |

Instead, you must use the copula `is`:

| Prefer |
| :--- |
| *Is dochtúir mé.* - I am a doctor. |

</Warning>

A common alternative that uses the verb `bí` involves turning the predicate into a prepositional phrase:
*   *Tá mé i mo dhochtúir.* - I am a doctor. (Literally: "I am in my doctor-ness.")

<Note>
The Irish copula is not considered a true verb in the same way `bí` is. It is better understood as a particle or a defective verb. It does not conjugate for person and has a very limited number of tense forms.
</Note>

## Uses of the Copula

The copula is used in several common and important sentence structures.

### Classification and Identification
This is the most common use of the copula, stating what something or someone is.
*   *Is dochtúir é.* - He is a doctor. (Classification)
*   *Is é an dochtúir é.* - He is the doctor. (Identification)

### Adjectival Phrases
The copula is used with certain adjectives, particularly for subjective evaluations, comparisons, and in modal-like phrases.
*   *Is maith sin!* - That is good!
*   *Is ionann iad.* - They are the same.
*   *Ba mhaith le Pól an carr a cheannach.* - Paul would like to buy the car.
*   *an fear is mó* - the bigger/biggest man (literally, "the man who is bigger")

### Statements of Origin and Possession
It is used with prepositions like `as`, `de`, and `le` to show where something is from or who owns it.
*   *Is as Corcaigh é.* - He is from Cork.
*   *Is le Pól an carr.* - The car belongs to Paul.

### Emphasis (Cleft Sentences)
The copula is essential for forming cleft sentences, which are used to emphasise a particular part of the sentence.
*   *Is é an fear a dhéanann na bróga.* - It is the man who makes the shoes.

### Interrogative Questions
Questions that use an interrogative word (like "who?", "what?", "where?") are formed using a copula construction, even if the copula itself is not visible.
*   *Cé a rinne sin?* - Who did that? (Literally: "Who is it that did that?")

## Key Grammatical Features

The copula follows a unique set of grammatical rules that distinguish it from regular verbs.

*   **Limited Forms:** It only has two basic forms: `is` for the present and future, and `ba` for the past and conditional.
*   **Missing Forms:** The copula has no imperative or verbal noun. To express these ideas, you must use a construction with `bí` and the preposition `i`.
    *   *Bí i d’fhear!* - Be a man!
    *   *bheith ina fhear* - to be a man
*   **Invariable Forms:** Copula forms do not change for person or number. `is` and `ba` are used for all persons (I, you, he, she, etc.).
*   **Disjunctive Pronouns:** The 3rd person personal pronouns `sé`, `sí`, and `siad` are not used directly after the copula. Instead, their disjunctive forms `é`, `í`, and `iad` are required.
*   **Fused Forms:** The copula merges with negative and interrogative particles. For example, `ní` is a fusion of `ní` + `is` ("is not"), and `an` is a fusion of `an` + `is` ("is it?"). Many interrogative words also contain an implied copula, e.g., `cé` ("who is?").
*   **Mutations:** In the present tense, copula forms cause no mutation. In the past/conditional, the form `ba` always causes lenition.
*   **Word Order:** The word order in a simple copula sentence is **Copula - Predicate - Subject**.
*   **Definite Nouns:** A definite noun cannot come directly after the copula. It must be separated by a pronoun (`é`, `í`, or `iad`).
    *   *Is **é** an múinteoir an fear sin.* - That man is the teacher.

## Tenses of the Copula

*   **`is` (Present/Future):** This is the present tense form. While it can be used for the future (*Is gearr gurb é* - It will soon be), this is rare. For future classification, constructions with `bí` are more common. `is` is also used to introduce cleft sentences regardless of the tense of the main verb.
*   **`ba` (Past/Conditional):** This form is used for past tenses and the conditional. With noun predicates, it usually indicates the past. With adjective predicates, it can be past or conditional. In modal phrases, it is typically conditional.
    *   *Ba mhaith liom.* - I would like.
*   **`gura`/`nára` (Subjunctive):** The form `gura` (negative `nára`) is used for the subjunctive mood, but this is uncommon in modern Irish.

<Details summary="Linguistic note: Fused forms and 'dropped' particles">
  The idea that forms like `ní` are "fusions" is a simplification. In reality, they are often the normal verbal particles, after which the copula `is` or `ba` is simply omitted. For example, `ní` is used instead of a theoretical `*ní is*`. This omission is why these particles do not cause lenition or eclipsis in the present tense (e.g., `ní maith liom`, not `*ní mhaith liom*`). In the past tense, although the form `ba` is also dropped, the lenition it would have caused is retained (e.g., `níor mhaith liom`). The trigger for lenition here is the "missing" `ba`, not the particle `níor` itself. True fusions include forms like `más` (from `má` + `is`) and those ending in `-b` or `-bh`, which are abbreviations of `ba` or `bha` (e.g., `níorbh é an fear`).
</Details>

<Details summary="Historical note: The pronouns 'sé', 'sí', and 'siad'">
  From a historical perspective, the subject pronouns `sé`, `sí`, and `siad` are themselves considered to be combinations of the copula `is` and the older pronouns `é`, `í`, and `iad`. Following this logic, every sentence using these pronouns technically contains a copula linking the predicate and the subject.
</Details>
