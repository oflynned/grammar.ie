The Irish copula, `is`, is a special verb used to link a subject to a predicate, much like "to be" in English. However, unlike the main verb `bí` (which describes states, locations, and actions), the copula is used for definitions, classifications, and identifications. It has a unique and strict grammar that is very different from other verbs.

In Irish, all copula sentences can be divided into two main types: identification sentences and classification sentences.
*   **Identification sentences** state that the subject and predicate are the same thing (X = Y).
*   **Classification sentences** state that the subject belongs to a particular class or group (X is a member of Y).

These two sentence types have different grammatical structures.

## Identification Sentences

An identification sentence, or `an abairt ionannais`, is typically a copula sentence with a **definite** predicative noun. It expresses the idea "X is *the* Y", where the subject and predicate are identical.

### With a Definite Noun as Subject After the Predicate

When the predicate is a definite noun (e.g., a noun with the article `an`), a pronoun `é`, `í`, or `iad` must be placed between the copula and the predicate. This pronoun is called a **sub-predicate** (`fofhaisnéis`) and acts as a placeholder, because a definite noun cannot directly follow the copula.

The choice of sub-predicate depends on the subject:
*   `é` for masculine singular subjects: `é mo theach` (my house)
*   `í` for feminine singular subjects: `í an bhean` (the woman)
*   `iad` for plural subjects or multiple items: `iad na fir` (the men), `iad an fear agus an bhean` (the man and the woman)

The basic structure is: **Copula + Sub-predicate + Predicate + Subject**.

| Tense | Form | Structure | Example | Translation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Present** | Positive | `Is + é/í/iad + P + S` | *Is é an sagart an fear.* | The man is the priest. |
| | Negative | `Ní + hé/hí/hiad + P + S` | *Ní hé an dochtúir an fear.* | The man is not the doctor. |
| **Past / Conditional** | Positive | `Ba + é/í/iad + P + S` | *Ba í an dochtúir an bhean.* | The woman was the doctor. |
| | Negative | `Níorbh + é/í/iad + P + S` | *Níorbh é an dochtúir an bhean.* | The woman was not the doctor. |

### With a Definite Noun as Subject Before the Predicate

In some identification sentences, especially for emphasis, the subject can appear before the main predicate. However, the sub-predicate `é/í/iad` still comes before the subject, maintaining the core structure.

The structure is: **Copula + Sub-predicate + Subject + Predicate**.

| Tense | Form | Structure | Example | Translation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Present** | Positive | `Is + é/í/iad + S + P` | *Is é mo bharúil go …* | It is my opinion that… |
| | Negative | `Ní + hé/hí/hiad + S + P` | *Ní hé mo bharúil go …* | It is not my opinion that… |
| **Past / Conditional** | Positive | `Ba + é/í/iad + S + P` | *Ba é mo bharúil go …* | It was my opinion that… |
| | Negative | `Níorbh + é/í/iad + S + P` | *Níorbh é mo bharúil go …* | It was not my opinion that… |

#### The Sub-predicate

The agreement of the sub-predicate in this structure follows specific rules:

*   The sub-predicate is always `é` when the main predicate is a `go`-clause or a verbal noun phrase. It agrees with the clause, not the grammatical gender of the subject noun.
    *   *Is é mo bharúil go bhfuil sé anseo.* - It is my opinion that he is here.
    (Even though `barúil` is feminine, `é` is used because the predicate is the `go`-clause).

*   When the predicate is a noun, the sub-predicate (`é/í/iad`) agrees with the gender and number of the **subject**.
    *   *Is é an chéad chrann a tharla dó saileach mhór.* - The first tree he encountered was a large willow.
    (The sub-predicate `é` is masculine because the subject `an chéad chrann` is masculine, even though the predicate `saileach mhór` is feminine).

*   When referring to people, the sub-predicate agrees with the **natural gender** of the person, regardless of the grammatical gender of the nouns involved.
    *   *Is í Máire an múinteoir.* - Máire is the teacher.
    (The sub-predicate `í` is feminine for Máire, even though `múinteoir` is a masculine noun).
    *   *Is í an múinteoir a bhí agam ná Bríd.* - The teacher I had was Bríd.
    (The sub-predicate `í` is feminine for Bríd, even though the subject `an múinteoir` is masculine).

#### Usage of This Structure

This sentence structure is used in several specific situations, most notably for emphasis in what are known as **cleft sentences**.

##### To Emphasise the Predicate (Cleft Sentences)

This is the most common use. The part of the sentence being emphasised becomes the predicate and is moved to the end.

*   The subject is usually a definite noun, often followed by a relative clause. The article `an` of the subject can be omitted, especially with the word `rud` (thing).
*   The noun `rud` is often used as a general-purpose subject, meaning "that which" or "what".
*   In Munster, the particle `ná` (or sometimes `ach`) is often placed before the main predicate. In other dialects, a slight pause is used instead.

*   *Is é an rud a chonaic mé ná capall.* - What I saw was a horse.
*   *Is é an t-ainm a bhí air ná Seán.* - The name he had was Seán.
*   *Is é an fear a bhí ann ná Seán.* - The man who was there was Seán.

<Dialect region="Connacht">
The sub-predicate `é` often merges with the subject `rud` to form `éard`. This can further merge with the copula `is` to become `séard`.

- *Séard a chonaic mé capall.* - What I saw was a horse.
</Dialect>

<Note>
Even when the predicate is an indefinite noun (like `capall` in the example), this structure is still considered an identification sentence. The subject (`an rud a chonaic mé`) is identified with the sub-predicate `é`, and the indefinite noun at the end provides the new, emphasised information.
</Note>

##### For Definitions

Cleft sentences are the standard way to form definitions in Irish.

*   *Is é rud is crann ann ná planda ard adhmadach.* - A tree is a tall, woody plant. (Literally: "What a tree is, is a tall, woody plant.")
*   *Is é atá sa bhéaloideas ná scéalta a chuirtear ar aghaidh ó ghlúin go glúin.* - What folklore is, are stories that are passed on from generation to generation.

##### When the Predicate is a Clause or Verbal Noun

This structure is used when the predicate is a `go`-clause or a verbal noun phrase.

*   *Is é mo bharúil go bhfuil ceart agat.* - It is my opinion that you are right.
*   *Is é m’aidhm Gaeilge a thuiscint.* - My goal is to understand Irish.
*   *Ba é mo chomhairle dó an capall a dhíol.* - My advice to him was to sell the horse.

##### When the Subject is a Proper Noun or Demonstrative

Proper nouns and demonstrative pronouns usually appear as the subject before the predicate.

*   *Is é Seán an dochtúir.* - Seán is the doctor.
*   *Is é sin an dochtúir.* - That is the doctor.
*   *Is é seo Seán Ó Néill.* - This is Seán Ó Néill.

<Note>
Placing a proper noun or demonstrative at the end of the sentence is possible but rare. It adds a strong, unusual emphasis to the predicate, suggesting something like "That is *the* famous doctor". For normal statements, the proper noun or demonstrative comes first.
</Note>

### With 1st/2nd Person Pronouns (mé, tú, muid, sinn, sibh) as Subject

When the subject is a 1st or 2nd person pronoun, the word order changes to **Copula + Subject + Predicate**.

| Tense | Form | Structure | Example | Translation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Present** | Positive | `Is + pers. pron. + P` | *Is mé an dochtúir.* | I am the doctor. |
| | Negative | `Ní + pers. pron. + P` | *Ní mé an dochtúir.* | I am not the doctor. |
| **Past / Conditional** | Positive | `Ba + pers. pron. + P` | *Ba mé an dochtúir.* | I was the doctor. |
| | Negative | `Níor + pers. pron. + P` | *Níor mé an dochtúir.* | I was not the doctor. |

This structure focuses on the predicate (`an dochtúir`). It is the standard form for questions (*An tú Seán?* - Are you Seán?) but is less common in declarative statements.

In exclamations, the pronoun can come at the end and the copula `is` is omitted.
*   *Mo cheol thú!* - Bravo! (Literally: "[You are] my music!")
*   *Mo Dhia thú!* - You are my God!

### With 1st/2nd Person Pronouns (mé, tú, muid, sinn, sibh) as Predicate

To emphasise the pronoun, it is placed in the predicate position, immediately after the copula. In this case, the **emphatic forms** of the pronouns (`mise`, `tusa`, etc.) are used.

| Tense | Form | Structure | Example | Translation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Present** | Positive | `Is + pers. pron. + S` | *Is mise an dochtúir.* | **I** am the doctor. |
| | Negative | `Ní + pers. pron. + S` | *Ní mise an dochtúir.* | **I** am not the doctor. |
| **Past / Conditional** | Positive | `Ba + pers. pron. + S` | *Ba mise an dochtúir.* | **I** was the doctor. |
| | Negative | `Níor + pers. pron. + S` | *Níor mise an dochtúir.* | **I** was not the doctor. |

This is the more common structure for declarative sentences.

When two pronouns are involved, the first person comes before the second or third. The use of emphatic forms helps clarify which is the subject and which is the predicate.
*   *dá mba mise thusa* - if you were me
*   *dá mba mé thú* - if I were you

### With 3rd Person Pronouns (é, í, iad) as Subject

When the subject is a 3rd person pronoun, the sentence uses both a sub-predicate and a subject pronoun.

The structure is: **Copula + Sub-predicate + Predicate + Subject**.

| Tense | Form | Structure | Example | Translation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Present** | Positive | `Is + é/í/iad + P + é/í/iad` | *Is é an dochtúir é.* | He is the doctor. |
| | Negative | `Ní + hé/hí/hiad + P + é/í/iad` | *Ní hé an dochtúir é.* | He is not the doctor. |
| **Past / Conditional** | Positive | `Ba + é/í/iad + P + é/í/iad` | *Ba é an dochtúir é.* | He was the doctor. |
| | Negative | `Níorbh + é/í/iad + P + é/í/iad` | *Níorbh é an dochtúir é.* | He was not the doctor. |

The first pronoun (`é/í/iad`) is the sub-predicate, required before the definite predicate. The second pronoun is the actual subject. The two pronouns must always agree in gender and number (e.g., `é...é`, `í...í`, `iad...iad`).

<Dialect region="Ulster">
In some Ulster dialects, the final subject pronoun is omitted, making the structure identical to that for 1st and 2nd person pronouns.

- *Is é an fear.* - He is the man. (Standard: *Is é an fear é.*)
</Dialect>

### With 3rd Person Pronouns (é, í, iad) as Predicate

To emphasise a 3rd person pronoun, it is placed in the predicate position using its emphatic form (`eisean`, `ise`, `iadsan`) or with `féin` or a demonstrative (`é sin`).

| Tense | Form | Structure | Example | Translation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Present** | Positive | `Is + pers. pron. + S` | *Is eisean an dochtúir.* | **He** is the doctor. |
| | Negative | `Ní + pers. pron. + S` | *Ní heisean an dochtúir.* | **He** is not the doctor. |
| **Past / Conditional** | Positive | `Ba + pers. pron. + S` | *Ba eisean an dochtúir.* | **He** was the doctor. |
| | Negative | `Níorbh + pers. pron. + S` | *Níorbh eisean an dochtúir.* | **He** was not the doctor. |

This form is less common than its 1st and 2nd person counterparts.

## Classification Sentences

A classification sentence, or `an abairt aicme`, uses an **indefinite** predicative noun. It expresses the idea "X is *a* Y", assigning the subject to a class or group. For example, in "It is a horse," the subject "it" is assigned to the class of things called "horse".

### With a Definite Subject

#### Normal Classification Sentence

The basic structure includes a pronoun called a **sub-subject** (`fo-ainmní`) between the predicate and the definite subject.

| Tense | Structure | Example | Translation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Present** | `Is + P + é/í/iad + S` | *Is dochtúir é an fear.* | The man is a doctor. |
| **Past / Conditional** | `Ba + P + é/í/iad + S` | *Ba dhochtúir é an fear.* | The man was a doctor. |

The sub-subject `é/í/iad` is often omitted, especially in Ulster.
*   *Is dochtúir (é) an fear.* - The man is a doctor.

<Note>
In proverbs, the subject and predicate can be swapped and the copula omitted.
- *An chuairt is fearr cuairt ghearr.* - The best visit is a short visit. (Normal order: *Is cuairt ghearr í an chuairt is fearr.*)
</Note>

#### Left-Shift of the Predicate

To add emphasis, the predicate can be moved to the front of the sentence. The pronoun `ea` ("it") then takes the predicate's original position. This is called left-dislocation or fronting.

| Tense | Structure | Example | Translation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Present** | `P + is + ea + S` | *Dochtúir is ea an fear.* | The man is a doctor. |
| **Past / Conditional** | `P + ba + ea + S` | *Dochtúir ba ea an fear.* | The man was a doctor. |

This structure is only used in positive statements. It is the standard, non-emphatic way to form classification sentences in Munster.

<Dialect region="Munster">
In Munster, `ab ea` is often used instead of `ba ea` in the past tense. This `ab` comes from the older form `dob ea` (`do` + `ba`).

- *Dochtúir ab ea an fear.* - The man was a doctor.
</Dialect>

### With Personal Pronouns as Subject

#### Normal Classification Sentence

| Tense | Structure | Example | Translation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Present** | `Is + P + pers. pron.` | *Is dochtúir mé.* | I am a doctor. |
| **Past / Conditional** | `Ba + P + pers. pron.` | *Ba dhochtúir mé.* | I was a doctor. |

<Dialect region="Connacht, Ulster">
In the past tense, this structure is uncommon. It is more natural to use the verb `bí` with the preposition `i`.

- *Bhí mé i mo dhochtúir.* or *Is dochtúir a bhí ionam.* - I was a doctor.
</Dialect>

#### Left-Shift of the Predicate

This emphatic structure is also used with personal pronouns, and is the standard form in Munster.

| Tense | Structure | Example | Translation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Present** | `P + is + ea + pers. pron.` | *Dochtúir is ea mé.* | I am a doctor. |
| **Past / Conditional** | `P + ba + ea + pers. pron.` | *Dochtúir ba ea mé.* | I was a doctor. |

### With an Indefinite Subject

When both the subject and predicate are indefinite, the structure is simple.

| Tense | Structure | Example | Translation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Present** | `Is + P + S` | *Is iasc bradán.* | A salmon is a fish. |
| **Past / Conditional** | `Ba + P + S` | *B’iasc bradán.* | A salmon was a fish. |

<Note>
In some proverbs, the subject comes first and the copula is omitted.
- *Tír gan teanga tír gan anam.* - A country without a language is a country without a soul.
- *Tosach maith leath na hoibre.* - A good beginning is half the work.
</Note>

## Copula and Adjectives

### With an Attributive Adjective

Adjectives can modify any noun in a copula sentence without changing the basic structure.
*   *Is duine deas é an fear mór.* - The big man is a nice person. (Classification)
*   *Is é an múinteoir nua an fear mór.* - The big man is the new teacher. (Identification)

There is also a special structure for classification sentences where an adjective modifies an indefinite predicate noun.

#### Special Form of Classification Sentence

This structure emphasises the adjective by moving it to the front of the sentence. It uses the article `an` with the noun, even though the meaning is indefinite ("a nice person," not "the nice person").

| Tense | Structure | Example | Translation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Present** | `Is + Adj. + an + P + S` | *Is deas an duine é.* | He is a *nice* person. |
| **Past / Conditional** | `Ba + Adj. + an + P + S` | *Ba deas an duine é.* | He was a *nice* person. |

The adjective in this structure is invariable and cannot be modified by adverbs like `an-` (very) or `ró-` (too).
*   *Is maith an fear é Dia.* - God is a good man.
*   *Is ait an mac an saol.* - Life is a strange son. (Proverb: "Life is strange.")

This structure is often used in rhetorical questions to add emphasis.
*   *Nach fuar an lá é?* - Isn't it a cold day?

It is also used in exclamations, often with the copula omitted.
*   *Maith an buachaill!* - Good boy! (from *Is maith an buachaill thú.*)

### With a Predicative Adjective

A copula sentence with a predicative adjective is a type of classification sentence, as the adjective describes a class or quality.

| Tense | Structure | Example | Translation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Present** | `Is + Adj. + (é/í/iad) + S` | *Is deas é an duine.* | The person is nice. |
| **Past / Conditional** | `Ba + Adj. + (é/í/iad) + S` | *Ba deas é an duine.* | The person was nice. |

The presence or absence of the sub-subject `é/í/iad` can add a subtle change in meaning:
*   *Is geal an solas.* - The light is bright. (Describes an inherent, general quality of light.)
*   *Is geal é an solas.* - The light is bright! (Describes the specific brightness of a particular light.)

#### Usage

Today, the verb `bí` is more commonly used with predicative adjectives (*Tá an fear tinn.* - The man is ill). However, the copula is still regularly used in several specific contexts:

*   **Subjective evaluation** in exclamatory sentences:
    *   *Is deas é sin!* - That's nice!
    *   *Ní furasta í an teanga seo!* - This language is not easy!

*   **Adjectives of equality** like `ionann` (same) and `cosúil` (similar):
    *   *Is ionann iad.* - They are the same.
    *   *Is cosúil go...* - It seems that...

*   **Adjectives like `deacair` (difficult) and `furasta` (easy)** followed by a verbal noun phrase:
    *   *Is deacair a rá go...* - It is difficult to say that...

*   **Adjectives of indefinite quantity** like `iomaí` (many) and `beag` (few):
    *   *Is iomaí fear a bhí ann.* - Many a man was there.
    *   *Is beag duine a thuigeann é.* - Few people understand it.

*   **Phrases with adjectives and prepositions** (see next section):
    *   *Is maith liom é.* - I like it.

*   **All comparatives and superlatives**:
    *   *Is fearr Gaeilge bhriste ná Béarla cliste.* - Broken Irish is better than clever English.

*   **Emphasis (cleft sentences)**, where an adverb is moved to the front:
    *   *Rinne sé an obair go cúramach.* → *Is cúramach a rinne sé an obair.* - He did the work carefully.

### With a Predicative Adjective and Prepositions

Many common expressions, especially those equivalent to modal verbs in English (should, can, like), are formed with the copula, an adjective, and a preposition.

| Tense | Structure | Example | Translation | Literal Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Present** | `Is + Adj. + Prep. + S` | *Is maith liom fuisce a ól.* | I like to drink whiskey. | "Is good with me, whiskey to drink" |
| **Past / Conditional** | `Ba + Adj. + Prep. + S` | *Ba mhaith liom fuisce a ól.* | I would like to drink whiskey. | "Would be good with me, whiskey to drink" |

The logical subject (the person who likes, should, can, etc.) is expressed by the prepositional pronoun (e.g., `liom`). The grammatical subject is the thing being discussed (e.g., `fuisce a ól`).

The choice of preposition is key:
*   **`le`** indicates a **subjective** assessment ("I find...").
    *   *Is maith liom é.* - I like it. (I find it good.)
    *   *Is fada liom an lá.* - I find the day long. (The day feels long to me.)
*   **`do`** indicates an **objective** assessment ("good for...").
    *   *Is maith bainne duit.* - Milk is good for you.
    *   *Ba cheart dom imeacht.* - I should leave. (It would be right for me to leave.)

Some nouns can also be used in this structure, such as `dóigh` (opinion), `oth` (regret), `fuath` (hatred), and `féidir` (possible).
*   *Is dóigh liom go bhfuil sé ceart.* - I think he is right.
*   *Is fuath liom é.* - I hate it.
*   *Is féidir liom snámh.* - I can swim.

Some words appear in a special form in these constructions: `eol` (known), `cuimhin` (remembered), `eagal` (afraid).
*   *mar is eol duit* - as you know
*   *Is maith is cuimhin liom é.* - I remember it well.
*   *Is eagal liom go bhfuil sé déanach.* - I'm afraid it's late.

## Dependent Forms of the Copula

The copula has special forms when used in dependent clauses, such as subordinate clauses, relative clauses, and conditional clauses.

### In a go/nach-clause

| Tense | Form | Structure | Example | Translation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Present** | Positive | `gur` | *Deir sé gur maith leis é.* | He says that he likes it. |
| | Negative | `nach` | *Deir sé nach maith leis é.* | He says that he doesn't like it. |
| **Past / Conditional** | Positive | `gur` | *Dúirt sé gur mhaith leis é.* | He said that he would like it. |
| | Negative | `nár` | *Dúirt sé nár mhaith leis é.* | He said that he would not like it. |

Before a vowel, `gur` becomes `gurb` (present) or `gurbh` (past/conditional). This form is mainly used before pronouns and adjectives.

### In a Direct Relative Clause

A direct relative clause refers to the subject of the clause.

| Tense | Form | Structure | Example | Translation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Present** | Positive | `is` | *an rud is féidir liom a dhéanamh* | the thing that I can do |
| | Negative | `nach` | *an rud nach féidir liom a dhéanamh* | the thing that I cannot do |
| **Past / Conditional** | Positive | `ba` | *an rud ab fhéidir liom a dhéanamh* | the thing that I could do |
| | Negative | `nár` | *an rud nárbh fhéidir liom a dhéanamh* | the thing that I could not do |

In the past/conditional, `ba` becomes `ab` before a vowel or `fh-`. The negative `nár` becomes `nárbh`.

A special form `is ... ann` is used for definitions.
*   *Cad is crann ann?* - What is a tree?
*   *Séard is crann ann ná planda ard.* - A tree is a tall plant.

### In an Indirect Relative Clause

An indirect relative clause refers to something other than the subject, often the object or an element governed by a preposition.

| Tense | Form | Structure | Example | Translation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Present** | Positive | `ar` | *an bhean ar maith léi an leabhar* | the woman who likes the book |
| | Negative | `nach` | *an bhean nach maith léi an leabhar* | the woman who does not like the book |
| **Past / Conditional** | Positive | `ar` | *an bhean ar mhaith léi an leabhar* | the woman who would like the book |
| | Negative | `nár` | *an bhean nár mhaith léi an leabhar* | the woman who would not like the book |

Before a vowel or `fh-`, `ar` becomes `arb` (present) or `arbh` (past/conditional).

<Dialect region="Munster">
In Munster, `gur(b/bh)` is used instead of `ar(b/bh)`.
</Dialect>

This structure is common for classification and identification.
*   *an fear ar múinteoir é* - the man who is a teacher
*   *an fear arb é an múinteoir é* - the man who is the teacher

When a preposition is involved, it can combine with the relative particle, e.g., `do + ar` → `dar`.
*   *an fear darb ainm Seán* - the man whose name is Seán

### In a Real Conditional Clause (If...)

| Tense | Form | Structure | Example | Translation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Present** | Positive | `más` | *Más é an dochtúir é* | If he is the doctor |
| | Negative | `mura` | *Murab é an dochtúir é* | If he is not the doctor |
| **Past** | Positive | `má ba` | *Má ba é an dochtúir é* | If he was the doctor |
| | Negative | `murar` | *Murarbh é an dochtúir é* | If he was not the doctor |

### In an Unreal Conditional Clause (If... were...)

| Tense | Form | Structure | Example | Translation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Conditional** | Positive | `dá mba` | *Dá mba é an dochtúir é* | If he were the doctor |
| | Negative | `murar` | *Murarbh é an dochtúir é* | If he were not the doctor |

### In an Optative Clause (Wishes)

These forms are used for wishes and blessings and are mostly found in fixed expressions.

| Tense | Form | Structure | Example | Translation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Subjunctive Present** | Positive | `gura` | *Gura maith agat!* | Thank you! (May it be good at you!) |
| | Negative | `nára` | *Nára fada uainn an lá!* | May the day not be far from us! |

## Special Uses of the Copula

### Indicating Possession

The construction `is le` is used to state that something belongs to someone. It is only used with definite nouns.

| Tense | Structure | Example | Translation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Present** | `is + le + Owner + Item` | *Is le Pól an carr.* | The car belongs to Paul. |
| **Past / Conditional** | `ba + le + Owner + Item` | *Ba le Pól an carr.* | The car belonged to Paul. |

The question form is *Cé leis é?* (To whom does it belong?).

### Indicating Origin

The construction `is as` is used to state where someone is from.

| Structure | Example | Translation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `is + as + Place + Person` | *Is as an nGearmáin mé.* | I am from Germany. |
| `is + as + Place + do + Person` | *Is as an nGearmáin dom.* | I am from Germany. |

The question form is *Cad as duit?* or *Cárb as duit?* (Where are you from?).

### Naming

#### Stating a Full Name

The standard way to state a name is with a cleft sentence structure. The name itself is treated as an indefinite noun.

*   *(Is) Seán is ainm dom.* - My name is Seán.
*   *Nach Seán is ainm dó?* - Isn't his name Seán?
*   *Deir sé gur Seán is ainm dó.* - He says his name is Seán.

The question form is *Cad is ainm duit?* (What is your name?).

#### Stating a Surname

To give just a surname, the construction `is de` is used.

| Structure | Example | Translation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `is + de + Surname-ach` | *Is de Bhrianach mé.* | I am an O'Brien. |
| `is + de mhuintir + Surname` | *Is de mhuintir Bhriain mé.* | I am of the O'Brien family. |

The question form is *Cér díobh thú?* (What is your surname?).

## Interrogative Sentences

### Yes/No Questions

Yes/no questions are formed with special interrogative particles instead of `is` or `ba`.

| Tense | Positive | Before Vowel | Negative | Before Vowel | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Present** | `an` | `an` | `nach` | `nach` | *An eisean an dochtúir?* (Is he the doctor?) |
| **Past / Conditional** | `ar` | `arbh` | `nár` | `nárbh` | *Ar dhochtúir é?* (Was he a doctor?) |

<Dialect region="Connacht">
In Connemara, `ar` can be used for the present tense before `mé, tú, muid, sibh`, and `ab` before `é, í, iad`. The past tense can be formed with `an mba`. This can lead to ambiguity: *Ar mise an dochtúir?* could mean "Am I the doctor?" (Connemara) or "Was I the doctor?" (Standard).
</Dialect>

### Wh-questions with Cé, Cad, Céard

Questions with `cé` (who), `cad` (what), and `céard` (what) are inherently copula constructions. `Cé` means "who is," and `cad` means "what is."

#### Cé (Who is...?)

*   *Cé hí an bhean sin?* - Who is that woman?
*   *Cé hé tusa?* - Who are you?
*   *Cérbh í an bhean sin?* - Who was that woman?

#### Cén / Cé na (Which...?)

`Cén` (`cé` + `an`) and `cé na` are used before a noun to ask "which?".
*   *Cén leabhar é sin?* - Which book is that?
*   *Cé na leabhair iad sin?* - Which books are those?

#### Cad é / Céard (What is...?)

These are used to ask for definitions or identifications.
*   *Cad é an t-am?* - What time is it?
*   *Céard é do bharúil?* - What is your opinion?
*   *Cad é an rud é leabhar?* - What is a book?
*   *Cad is leictreachas ann?* - What is electricity?

<Details summary="Dialectal forms of 'cad é'">
The phrase `cad é` is pronounced differently across the dialects, leading to various written forms. The stress is on `é`, causing the `cad` to be reduced.
- Munster: `god é` [gə'deː]
- Ulster: `goidé` [gə'd′eː]
- Connacht: `céard` (from an older `go céard` < `cad é rud`)
- Ring, Co. Waterford: `déard`
</Details>

### Answers to Copula Questions

Irish has no single words for "yes" and "no". To answer a yes/no question, you repeat the verb from the question. For the copula, this means repeating the predicate with the appropriate positive or negative copula form.

*   For **identification** questions ("Is X *the* Y?"), the answer uses the pronoun `é`, `í`, or `iad`.
*   For **classification** questions ("Is X *a* Y?"), the answer uses the pronoun `ea`.

| Question Type | Positive Answer | Negative Answer |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Identification** (*An é Pól an dochtúir?*) | `Is é.` (Yes) | `Ní hé.` (No) |
| **Classification** (*An dochtúir é Pól?*) | `Sea.` (`Is ea`) (Yes) | `Ní hea.` (No) |
| **With Adjective** (*An deas an duine é?*) | `Is deas.` (Yes) | `Ní deas.` (No) |
| **With Adjective + Preposition** (*An maith leat é?*) | `Is maith (liom).` (Yes) | `Ní maith (liom).` (No) |
| **Possession** (*An leatsa an leabhar?*) | `Is liom.` (Yes) | `Ní liom.` (No) |
| **Cleft Sentence** (*An inné a rinne tú é?*) | `Sea.` (`Is ea`) (Yes) | `Ní hea.` (No) |

The past/conditional forms are used when answering a past/conditional question.
*   *Ar mhúinteoir í?* - *Ba ea.* (Was she a teacher? - Yes.)
*   *Arbh é an dochtúir é?* - *Níorbh é.* (Was he the doctor? - No.)

## Demonstrative Pronouns in Copula Sentences

The demonstrative pronouns `seo` (this), `sin` (that), and `siúd` (that over there) have special behaviour in copula sentences. There are two main structures: a **long form** and a **short form**.

*   **Long Form**: A standard copula sentence where the demonstrative follows a pronoun (`é seo`, `í sin`). This form has a determining meaning ("*This one* is the teacher").
    *   *Is é seo an múinteoir.* - This is the teacher.
    *   *Is múinteoir é seo.* - This is a teacher.

*   **Short Form**: The demonstrative pronoun comes first, and the copula `is` is omitted. This form has a presenting, deictic meaning ("*Here* is the teacher").
    *   *Seo é an múinteoir.* - Here is the teacher.
    *   *Seo múinteoir.* - Here is a teacher.

<Details summary="Historical note on 'seo'">
The short form originates from an older construction `ag seo` (literally "see this!"), similar to French *voici*. The `ag` was an imperative verb, not the preposition. Over time, the `ag` was dropped, and the initial `s` of `seo/sin/siúd` was reinterpreted by speakers as the copula `is`. This led to the creation of "s-less" forms like `b'eo` (`ba` + `eo`) and `an in` (`an` + `in`) in dialects.
</Details>

The short form is very common in speech. In dialects outside Ulster, when a copula form other than `is` is used, the `s` of the demonstrative is dropped.

| Form | Short Form (Munster/Connacht) | Long Form (Standard) | Translation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Present Positive** | *Seo é an fear.* | *Is é seo an fear.* | This/Here is the man. |
| **Past Positive** | *B’eo é an fear.* | *Ba é seo an fear.* | This was the man. |
| **Present Negative** | *Ní heo é an fear.* | *Ní hé seo an fear.* | This is not the man. |
| **Present Question** | *An eo é an fear?* | *An é seo an fear?* | Is this the man? |
| **Subordinate Clause** | *...gurb eo é an fear.* | *...gurb é seo an fear.* | ...that this is the man. |

## Emphasis in Copula Sentences

The copula is the primary tool in Irish for emphasising different parts of a sentence, a process known as clefting.

### Cleft Sentence

To emphasise a part of a sentence, you move it to the front after the copula `is`. The rest of the sentence becomes a direct relative clause.

*   *Is maith liom í.* (I like her.) → *Is ise is maith liom.* (**She** is the one I like.)
*   *Ba cheart dóibh imeacht.* (They should leave.) → *Is dóibh ba cheart imeacht.* (**They** are the ones who should leave.)

### Pseudo-Cleft Sentence

This structure moves the emphasised element to the very end of the sentence for maximum focus. It uses a placeholder subject like `is é an rud` or `séard` (`is` + `éard`).

*   *Ba mhaith liom carr mór.* (I would like a big car.) → *Séard ba mhaith liom ná carr mór.* (What I would like is a **big car**.)
*   *Is duine uasal tú.* (You are a gentleman.) → *Is é rud tú ná duine uasal.* (What you are is a **gentleman**.)

### Left-Dislocation of the Predicate

In classification sentences, the predicate can be moved to the front for emphasis, with `ea` taking its place. This is the standard structure in Munster.

*   *Is dochtúir é an fear.* (The man is a doctor.) → *Dochtúir is ea an fear.* (A **doctor** is what the man is.)

### Emphasis with bí

Unusually, to emphasise the predicate of a classification sentence, you can use a cleft sentence with the verb `bí` and the preposition `i`. This is common in Connacht and Ulster and is equivalent to the Munster left-dislocation.

*   *Is dochtúir í.* (She is a doctor.) → *Dochtúir atá inti.* (A **doctor** is what she is.)

## Miscellaneous Constructions

### Ní ... go ...

This fixed proverbial structure means "There is no X without Y" or "It isn't X until Y".

*   *Ní féasta go rósta.* - It's not a feast without a roast.
*   *Ní neart go cur le chéile.* - There is no strength without unity. (Unity is strength.)

### Ní ... go dtí ...

This productive structure means "There is no X like Y" or "There was nothing to match Y".

*   *Ní haoibhneas go dtí é.* - There is no joy like it.
*   *Níor bhean go dtí í.* - There was no woman like her.
