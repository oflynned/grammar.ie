Irish nouns are either masculine or feminine. Gender matters because it affects articles, initial mutations, adjective agreement, pronouns, and some declension patterns. You will meet it whenever you use a noun with `an`, `na`, an adjective, or a pronoun such as `sé` or `sí`.

## Gender and Biological Sex

Irish distinguishes between the natural, biological sex of living beings, called sex (`gnéas`), and the grammatical gender of nouns, called gender (`inscne`).

Irish has two grammatical genders: masculine (`firinscneach`) and feminine (`baininscneach`). Male living beings are usually named by masculine nouns, and female living beings are usually named by feminine nouns.

Not every noun follows biological sex. For example, `stail` means "stallion" but is grammatically feminine, while `cailín` means "girl" but is grammatically masculine.

<Warning>
  Do not assume that biological sex always decides grammatical gender. `stail` is feminine, and `cailín` is masculine.
</Warning>

For inanimate things, gender is usually arbitrary from a learner's point of view, although it can often be connected with the noun's origin or ending. Irish is therefore similar to German in having grammatical gender, but present-day Irish no longer has a neuter gender.

## Where Gender Matters

Gender affects several parts of Irish grammar.

- The declension of the noun and of following adjectives
- The use of the definite article in the nominative and genitive
- The initial mutation after the article: `an` or `na`, lenition or no lenition, and t-prefix before `s` or a vowel
- The lenition of following adjectives and genitive attributes
- The choice of personal pronoun: `sé` for "he" or `sí` for "she"
- The choice of possessive pronoun: `a` with lenition for "his", and `a` without lenition for "her"
- The choice of prepositional pronoun, for example `aige` "at him", `aici` "at her", `as` "out of him", and `aisti` "out of her"

<Note>
  Masculine nouns that refer to people of female biological sex can still take feminine pronouns, for example `an cailín` may be referred to with `sí` or `í`. The reverse can also happen. Some nouns and pronouns therefore do not agree strictly in grammatical gender.
</Note>

## Recognising Masculine Nouns

Some patterns are useful clues that a noun is masculine.

### Declension Clues

The following nouns are usually masculine:

- All nouns of the 1st declension
- Most nouns of the 4th declension
- No nouns of the 2nd declension, except the three masculine nouns `im`, `sliabh`, and `teach`
- Only a few nouns of the 5th declension
- Most occupational nouns

### Masculine Endings

Nouns with the following endings are masculine:

- `-adh`
- `-amh`
- `-aí`
- `-án`
- `-ar`
- `-as`
- `-éad`
- `-éal`
- `-éara`
- `-aeir`
- `-éir`
- `-úir`
- `-ú`
- `-ún`
- `-ire`

Nouns ending in `-éar` are almost all masculine.

| Ending | Masculine pattern | Exceptions |
|---|---|---|
| `-éar` | almost all masculine | `méar` "finger", `sméar` "berry" |
| `-eoir` | almost all masculine | `beoir` "beer", `deoir` "drop", `treoir` "guidance" |
| `-óir` | almost all masculine | `altóir` "altar", `cóir` "justice, right", `éagóir` "injustice, wrong", `glóir` "glory", `onóir` "honour" |
| `-aire` | almost all masculine | `aire` "care", `trócaire` "mercy" |
| `-ste` | almost all masculine | `aiste` "essay", `timpiste` "accident", `tubaiste` "disaster" |
| `-úr` | almost all masculine | `deirfiúr`, `siúr` "sister" |

Nouns ending in `-ín` are normally masculine because they belong to the 4th declension.

`mala` "bag" and `muga` "mug" show the common masculine pattern for disyllabic nouns ending in `-a`.

Monosyllabic nouns ending in `-cht` are masculine, for example `lucht` "load, people", `reacht` "law, right", and `bocht` "poor person". The exception is `léacht` "lecture". Polysyllabic nouns ending in `-cht` are masculine only in compounds such as `bunreacht` "constitution" and `comhlacht` "company".

Derivations in `-ach` are masculine when they are formed from other nouns.

- `Éire` "Ireland" > `Éireannach` "Irishman, Irishwoman"
- `Gearmánach` "German"
- `Sasanach` "English person"
- `Caitliceach` "Catholic"

<Note>
  Words of Latin origin ending in `-ún` or `-úr` are feminine in Latin but masculine in Irish: `an náisiún` "the nation", `an nadúr` "nature".
</Note>

## Recognising Feminine Nouns

Some patterns are useful clues that a noun is feminine.

### Declension Clues

The following nouns are usually feminine:

- All nouns of the 2nd declension, except `im`, `sliabh`, and `teach`
- Most nouns of the 5th declension
- No nouns of the 1st declension
- Only a few nouns of the 4th declension

### Name Clues

The following types of names are usually feminine:

- Country names, except `Sasana` "England", `Ceannada` "Canada", and `Meiricea` "America"
- Language names, except `Béarla` "English"
- River names, except `An Feabhal` "Foyle"

### Feminine Endings

Nouns with the following endings are feminine:

- `-ailt`
- `-áil`
- `-aíl`
- `-eog`
- `-óg`
- `-seach`
- `-lann`
- `-íocht`
- `-úil`
- `-áint`
- `-úint`
- `-int`, except `sáirsint` "sergeant"
- `-irt`
- `-is`
- `ís`

Polysyllabic nouns ending in `-acht` are feminine, for example `Gaeltacht`.

Nouns ending in `-ach` are feminine when they are not derivations from other nouns.

`báisteach` "rain" is feminine.

## Gender-Specific Personal Nouns

Irish less often marks personal nouns, including occupational nouns, by natural sex. A `múinteoir` can be a male teacher or a female teacher, depending on context, although the noun is always grammatically masculine.

When Irish does need to mark sex explicitly, it can do so in several ways.

### The Prefix `ban-`

The prefix `ban-` marks women and is followed by lenition except before `t` and `d`.

- `bantiarna` "lady"
- `banrinceoir` "female dancer"
- `banphríosúnach` "a female prisoner"
- `bandia` "a goddess"

The prefix `fear-` for men is rare.

### Genitive Apposition

The genitive appositions `mná` and `fir` can also mark sex. Both forms are always unlenited.

- `baintreach fir` "widower"
- `dochtúir mná` "female doctor"

Compare `dochtúir ban`, which means "gynaecologist".

### Adjectives

Adjectives can mark sex directly.

- `fireann` "male"
- `baineann` "female"

### Endings

Irish only rarely uses gender-specific endings. This differs from German, where such endings are common.

- `banríon` "queen", with an ending in addition to the prefix `ban-`; compare `rí` "king"
- `máistreás` "mistress"; compare `máistir` "lord, master"

## Remnants of the Neuter Gender

The neuter gender, `an neodar`, has disappeared completely from present-day Irish. Nouns that were neuter in Old Irish were reassigned to masculine or feminine gender. They often belong to the 3rd declension, so 3rd declension nouns today may be either masculine or feminine without their form showing which one they are.

Some vague remnants of the neuter remain.

- Eclipsis and n-prefix appear in older geographical proper names after formerly neuter elements, for example `Loch nEathach` "Loch Neagh" and `Muir nIocht` "English Channel", although `loch` "lake" is now masculine and `muir` "sea" is now feminine.
- Some formerly neuter nouns vary dialectally between masculine and feminine.
- The neuter pronoun `ea` survives, but its use is now very limited.
- `trian` "third" was originally neuter, so `dhá` "two" often causes eclipsis instead of the usual lenition: `dhá dtrian` "two thirds".

<Details summary="Historical note on the neuter">
  Old Irish had a neuter gender, but modern Irish distributed those nouns between the masculine and feminine classes. Some older place names and fixed expressions preserve traces of the old neuter pattern.
</Details>

## The Diminutive Ending `-ín`

A diminutive is a small or affectionate form. The Irish ending `-ín` corresponds roughly to German diminutive endings such as "-chen" or "-lein".

The ending is usually added after palatalisation.

- `fear` "man" > `firín` "little man"

There is no palatalisation after `-ch` or `-cht`.

- `luch` "mouse" > `luchín` "little mouse"
- `teach` "house" > `teachín` "little house"
- `dréacht` "literary or musical work, piece" > `dréachtín` "little work, stanza"

Words ending in `-ín` belong to the 4th declension and are in principle masculine, though not always.

### Masculine Diminutives with New Meanings

Words are generally masculine when speakers no longer understand them as diminutives of a base word, or when they have developed a new meaning.

`cailín` "girl" is no longer understood as a diminutive of the obsolete `caile`, just as German "Mädchen" is no longer understood as a diminutive of "Maid, Magd". For that reason, `cailín` is masculine.

### Genuine Diminutives

With a masculine base word, the diminutive is naturally also masculine.

- `an firín` "the little man"
- `an leabhairín` "the little book"

With a feminine base word, the gender depends on case.

In the nominative singular, the diminutive is treated as feminine.

- `an pháirc bheag` "the small park" > `an pháircín bheag` "the little park"

In the genitive singular, the diminutive is treated as masculine.

- `cranna na páirce bige` "the trees of the small park" > `cranna an pháircín bhig` "the trees of the little park"

In the plural, gender is not visible.

- `na páirceanna beaga` "the small parks" > `na páircíní beaga` "the little parks"

With female personal names, the diminutive is always feminine.

- `Brídín`
- `Nóirín`

<Warning>
  Learners often expect every word ending in `-ín` to behave like an ordinary masculine noun. Genuine diminutives of feminine nouns can still be feminine in the nominative singular.
</Warning>

### Other Diminutive Suffixes

Today, only `-ín` is still productive for forming diminutives. Earlier Irish also used other suffixes, especially `-óg` for feminine nouns and `-án` for masculine nouns. These survive in many nouns.

- `cnocán` "a little hill"
- `eitleog` "kite", literally "little wing"
- `ordóg` "thumb", literally "little hammer"

### Diminutives Beyond Nouns

Unlike in German, the Irish diminutive ending `-ín` is not restricted to nouns. It can sometimes attach to adjectives or to adverbs derived from nouns.

- `ar baillín` "in or after a little while"
- `go fóillín` "still a little"
- `álainnín` "pretty-ish"
- `bídeachaín` "tiny-ish"
