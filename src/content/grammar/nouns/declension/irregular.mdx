While most Irish nouns can be categorised into one of the five declensions based on their genitive singular form, a small number of common nouns are highly irregular and do not follow any standard pattern.

This page provides a reference for the declension of these nouns.

The table below shows the principal parts of the most frequent irregular nouns. For detailed notes on dative forms and historical variations, see the sections further down the page.

| `deirfiúr` (sister) | f | `deirféar` | `deirfiúracha` | `ndeirfiúracha` |

## Other Notable Nouns

Some nouns, while sometimes classified within the standard declensions, have irregular forms that are worth noting separately.

### `teach` (house)

Though sometimes considered a 2nd declension noun, `teach` has a highly irregular pattern.

| Case | Singular | Plural |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Nominative** | `an teach` | `na tithe` |
| **Genitive** | `an tí` | `na dtithe` |
| **Dative** | `don tigh` / `teach` | `do na tithe` |

### `deoch` (drink)

Similarly to `teach`, `deoch` can be assigned to the 2nd declension but has irregular forms.

| Case | Singular | Plural |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Nominative** | `an deoch` | `na deochanna` |
| **Genitive** | `na dí` | `na ndeochanna` |
| **Dative** | `don digh` / `deoch` | `do na deochanna` |

### `bia` (food)

In the official standard, `bia` is a regular 4th declension noun (genitive `bia`). However, older forms, which are still common in Munster Irish, are irregular.

| Case | Standard Singular | Munster Singular | Plural |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Nominative** | `an bia` | `an biadh` | `na bianna` / `biadha` |
| **Genitive** | `an bhia` | `an bhídh` | `na mbianna` / `mbiadha` |
| **Dative** | `don bhia` | `don bhiadh` | `do na bianna` / `biadha` |

### `bó` (cow)

`bó` is now mostly a regular 4th declension noun, but its plural `ba` is unusually weak. It also has an old dative form `boin`.

| Case | Singular | Plural |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Nominative** | `an bhó` | `na ba` |
| **Genitive** | `na bó` | `na mbó` |
| **Dative** | `don bhó` / `boin` | `do na ba` |

The old dative plural was `buaibh`.

## Detailed Notes on Irregular Nouns

### `bean` (woman)

- **Dative Singular:** `don mhnaoi` or `(a) bhean`.
- **Dative Plural:** The historical dative plural was `mnáibh`.

### `deirfiúr` (sister)

The word `deirfiúr` is highly irregular. It was formed with the prefix `deirbh-` to distinguish a biological sister from a religious one (`siúr`).
- **Etymology:** The original form was `deirbh-fiúr`. The `f` comes from a lenited `s` in the Proto-Indo-European root *swesor- (compare German *Schwester*).
- **Dative Singular:** `don deirfír`. The old spelling was `deirbh-fiair`.
- **Dialect Forms:** Pronunciation varies significantly, e.g., [dr′əf′u:r] in Munster and [dr′aur] in Cois Fharraige. Other declension forms also exist, such as the genitive `drifíre` in Cois Fharraige.

### `siúr` (sister, nun)

This noun is largely declined like a 5th declension noun, but the `-r` in the nominative singular is broad, which is atypical.

### `lá` (day)

- **Dative Singular:** `don ló` or `(a) lá`.

### `leaba` (bed)

The genitive singular `leapa` likely arose from an older form `leabtha`.
- **Genitive variants:** Other historical or dialectal genitive forms include `leapan`, `leaban`, `leapthan`, and `leabadh`.
- **Dative variants:** Corresponding dative forms include `leapain` and `leabaidh`.

### `olann` (wool)

Technically, `olann` is a regular 3rd declension noun. Its genitive `olla` is formed by syncope (losing the `a` from `olann` to get `oln-`) and adding the 3rd declension ending `-a`. In modern Irish, the sound cluster `ln` becomes `ll`, resulting in the spelling `olla`.

### `talamh` (land)

- **5th Declension Pattern:** The genitive `talún` is a modern spelling of the old 5th declension genitive `talmhan`. The old dative was `talmhain`.
- **1st Declension Pattern:** A regular masculine 1st declension variant also exists: `talamh` (nom. sg.), `talaimh` (gen. sg.).
- **Historical Note:** Other nouns ending in `-mh` once belonged to the 5th declension, such as `breitheamh` (judge), which had the old genitive `breitheamhan` (modern `breithiún`). This is the origin of the English term "Brehon Laws" for the old Irish legal system. Today, these nouns are mostly treated as 1st declension, e.g., `breitheamh` (gen. `breithimh`).

### `Ó` / `Ua` (descendant)

These forms are used in surnames. The common noun `ó` (grandson) is a regular 4th declension noun.
- **Vocative:** `a Uí`.
- **Dative Singular:** The dative `Ú` is obsolete.
- **Dative Plural:** The form `Uibh` is found in some place names, such as `Uibh Ráthach` (Iveragh).
- **Usage:** `Ua` is also a common nominative singular form, and `Ó` can be a genitive plural.
