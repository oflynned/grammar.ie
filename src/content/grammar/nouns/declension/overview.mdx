In Irish, nouns are grouped into five main categories called declensions (*díochlaontaí*). This classification is based on how a noun forms its genitive singular case. The form of the dative singular also often follows a predictable pattern within each declension.

While there are some regular patterns for forming the plural, these are highly variable and are not used to classify nouns into declensions.

This page provides a high-level overview of the five declensions, along with irregular nouns and verbal nouns.

## The Five Declensions

The following table summarises the core patterns for the five main noun declensions in Irish.

| Declension | Common Gender | Genitive Singular Formation | Example (Nom. → Gen.) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1st | Masculine | Final consonant becomes slender | `fear` → `fir` |
| 2nd | Feminine | Final consonant becomes slender + `-e` | `long` → `loinge` |
| 3rd | m. or f. | Final consonant becomes broad + `-a` | `leann` → `leanna` |
| 4th | m. or f. | No change | `cailín` → `cailín` |
| 5th | m. or f. | Various patterns, often ending in a broad consonant | `athair` → `athar` |

### First Declension

- **Common Gender:** Masculine
- **Genitive Singular:** The final consonant becomes slender (palatalisation).
- **Dative Singular:** Same as the nominative singular.
- **Example:** `fear` (man) → Gen. Sg. `fir`, Dat. Sg. `fear`.

### Second Declension

- **Common Gender:** Feminine
- **Genitive Singular:** The final consonant becomes slender and the ending `-e` is added.
- **Dative Singular:** The final consonant becomes slender.
- **Example:** `long` (ship) → Gen. Sg. `loinge`, Dat. Sg. `loing`.

### Third Declension

- **Common Gender:** Masculine or Feminine
- **Genitive Singular:** The ending `-a` is added. If the final consonant was slender, it becomes broad.
- **Dative Singular:** Same as the nominative singular.
- **Example:** `leann` (ale) → Gen. Sg. `leanna`, Dat. Sg. `leann`.

### Fourth Declension

- **Common Gender:** Masculine or Feminine
- **Genitive Singular:** Same as the nominative singular.
- **Dative Singular:** Same as the nominative singular.
- **Example:** `cailín` (girl) → Gen. Sg. `cailín`, Dat. Sg. `cailín`.

### Fifth Declension

- **Common Gender:** Masculine and Feminine
- **Genitive Singular:** This is a varied group with several patterns. The final consonant is often broad.
- **Common Patterns:**
    - **Pattern A (m):** The final consonant becomes broad.
        - Example: `athair` (father) → Gen. Sg. `athar`
    - **Pattern B (f):** The genitive ends in a broad `-(e)ach` and the dative in a slender `-(a)igh`.
        - Example: `beoir` (beer) → Gen. Sg. `beorach`, Dat. Sg. `beoraigh`
    - **Pattern C (f):** The genitive adds a broad `-n` or `-nn`, and the dative adds a slender `-n` or `-nn`.
        - Example: `Éire` (Ireland) → Gen. Sg. `Éireann`, Dat. Sg. `Éirinn`
    - **Pattern D (m):** The genitive adds a broad `-d`, and the dative adds a slender `-d`.
        - Example: `cara` (friend) → Gen. Sg. `carad`, Dat. Sg. `caraid`

## Other Noun Categories

### Irregular Nouns

A small number of common nouns have highly irregular forms that do not fit into any of the five declensions.
- **Example:** `bean` (woman) → Gen. Sg. `mná`, Dat. Sg. `mnaoi`.

### Verbal Nouns

Regularly formed verbal nouns ending in `-adh` or `-ú` follow a special pattern.
- **Genitive Singular:** The genitive form is the same as the verbal adjective.
- **Example:** `bualadh` (hitting) → Gen. Sg. `buailte`.

## Dialectal Variation

In some dialects, certain nouns may shift from one declension to another. This is a natural part of language variation.
- `guala` (shoulder): Standard 5th declension (gen. `gualann`), but also found in the 2nd declension (gen. `gualainne`).
- `athair` (father): Standard 5th declension (gen. `athar`), but also found in the 3rd declension (gen. `athara`).
- `teanga` (tongue/language): Standard 4th declension (gen. `teanga`), but also found in the 5th declension (gen. `teangan`).

## Historical Context

The Irish declension system has historical roots shared with other Indo-European languages like Latin.
- **1st Declension:** Compare Irish `fear` (gen. `fir`) with Latin *vir* (gen. *viri*). The Latin `-i` ending is reflected in Irish as the palatalisation (slender quality) of the final consonant.
- **2nd Declension:** Compare Irish `long` (gen. `loinge`) with Latin *longa* (gen. *longae*). The Latin `-ae` ending corresponds to the Irish `-e` and palatalisation.
