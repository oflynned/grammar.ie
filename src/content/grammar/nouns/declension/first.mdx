The first declension (`An Chéad Díochlaonadh`) consists exclusively of masculine nouns that end in a broad consonant in their nominative singular form.

This includes many common nouns, masculine first names, and words with typical endings like `-ach` and `-adh` (excluding verbal nouns in `-adh`).

## Declension Pattern

The core principle of the first declension is palatalisation (`caolú`), where a broad consonant at the end of the stem is made slender to form the genitive singular. The plural forms vary.

### Weak Plural Pattern

Most first declension nouns form a "weak" plural. There are two common patterns:

| Case | Singular | Plural (Pattern A) | Plural (Pattern B) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Nominative** | Ends in a broad consonant | Ends in a slender consonant | Ends in a broad consonant + `-a` |
| **Genitive** | Ends in a slender consonant | Same as Nominative Singular | Same as Nominative Singular |

## Case Formation Rules

### Genitive Singular

The genitive singular is formed by making the final broad consonant slender. This process, known as palatalisation (`caolú`), is shown in the spelling in several ways:

-   **Adding `-i-`**: An `i` is inserted before the final consonant or consonant group.
    -   `bord` → `boird`
    -   `cat` → `cait`
    -   `Seán` → `Seáin`
-   **Changing endings**:
    -   `-(e)ach` becomes `-(a)igh`. Note the change from `ch` to `gh`.
        -   `marcach` → `marcaigh`
    -   `-(e)adh` becomes `-(a)idh`.
        -   `samhradh` → `samhraidh`
-   **Vowel changes**: Sometimes, the stem vowel also changes.
    -   `fear` → `fir`
    -   `mac` → `mic`
-   **Exceptions for `ia`**: Nouns with `ia` in the stem usually change it to `iai`, not `éi`.
    -   `Brian` → `Briain`
    -   `fiach` → `fiaigh`
    -   `rian` → `riain`

### Vocative Singular

The vocative singular has the same form as the genitive singular. It is preceded by the vocative particle `a`, which causes lenition.

-   *A fhir!* (O man!)
-   *A Sheáin!* (O Seán!)

### Nominative Plural

Plural formation is the most varied aspect of the first declension. Plurals can be "weak" or "strong".

#### Weak Plurals

A plural is considered weak if its genitive plural form is the same as the nominative singular.

1.  **By Palatalisation**: The nominative plural is identical to the genitive singular.
    -   `bord` (table) → `boird` (tables)
    -   `cat` (cat) → `cait` (cats)
2.  **By adding `-a`**: The ending `-a` is added to the nominative singular form.
    -   `cleas` (trick) → `cleasa` (tricks)
    -   `ceart` (right) → `cearta` (rights)

#### Strong Plurals

A plural is considered strong if its genitive plural form is the same as its nominative plural form.

-   **Ending `-aí`**: Common for nouns ending in `-ach` or `-adh`.
    -   `bealach` (way) → `bealaí`
    -   `cogadh` (war) → `cogaí`
-   **Ending `-ta` or `-tha`**: Found in monosyllabic words with a long vowel ending in `l`, `n`, or `r`.
    -   `scéal` (story) → `scéalta`
    -   `glór` (voice) → `glórtha`
-   **Ending `-nna` or `-anna`**:
    -   `bás` (death) → `básanna`
    -   `nós` (custom) → `nósanna`
-   **Syncope and `-e`**: Polysyllabic nouns can form a plural by palatalising the stem, dropping the vowel of the last syllable (syncope), and adding `-e`.
    -   `solas` (light) → `soilse`
    -   `uasal` (noble) → `uaisle`

### Genitive Plural

The form of the genitive plural depends on whether the nominative plural is weak or strong.

-   For **weak plurals**, the genitive plural is the same as the **nominative singular**.
    -   `na cait` (the cats, nom. pl.) → `na gcat` (of the cats, gen. pl.)
-   For **strong plurals**, the genitive plural is the same as the **nominative plural**.
    -   `na bealaí` (the ways, nom. pl.) → `na mbealaí` (of the ways, gen. pl.)

### Vocative Plural

The vocative plural always adds the ending `-a` to the nominative singular stem.

-   *A fheara!* (O men!)
