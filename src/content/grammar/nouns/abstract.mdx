Abstract nouns describe qualities or concepts rather than concrete objects. In Irish, many abstract nouns are formed from adjectives, such as turning 'good' (`maith`) into 'goodness' (`maitheas`). They are essential for expressing degrees and comparisons and are divided into two main types: ordinary and gradual.

## Ordinary Abstract Nouns

An ordinary abstract noun (`an t-ainmfhocal teibí gnách`) expresses a quality in a general sense, without reference to a specific person or thing possessing it.

### Formation

Ordinary abstract nouns are typically formed from adjectives in several ways:

| Formation Rule | Gender | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Comparative form of the adjective | f | `minice` (frequency), `bacaí` (lameness) |
| Comparative + `-(e)acht` or `-(a)íocht` | f | `deirgeacht` (redness), `clistíocht` (cleverness) |
| Adjective + `-(a)íl` (esp. for adjectives in `-ach`) | f | `bacaíl` (lameness) |
| Adjective + `-(e)as` or `-(e)adas` | m | `maitheas` (goodness), `beos` (liveliness), `dorchadas` (darkness) |
| Adjective + `-achar` (esp. for adjectives in `-ach`) | m | `lagachar` (weakness), `salachar` (dirt) |
| The adjective itself | m/f | `maith` (the good) |

<Note>
Some irregular forms end in `-ad`, such as `leithead` (width) from `leathan` (wide), `raimhead` (fatness) from `ramhar` (fat), and `laghad` (smallness) from `beag` (small). The noun `oiread` (amount) also belongs to this group, though it has no corresponding adjective.
</Note>

Many adjectives can form more than one abstract noun. For example, from `maith` (good), you can have `maith`, `maithe`, and `maitheas`.

<Note>
The comparative form of certain adjectives **cannot** be used as an ordinary abstract noun. This applies to adjectives ending in `-mhar` (`grianmhar` -> `grianmhaire`), a vowel (`beo`, `crua`), or `-a` (`fearúil` -> `fearúla`), as well as the irregular `dócha` (`dóichí`). These forms are used only as gradual abstract nouns.
</Note>

### Usage

These nouns express a general quality and are often used with the definite article.

- *Ní maith liom an dorchadas.* - I don't like darkness.

## Gradual Abstract Nouns

The gradual abstract noun (`an t-ainmfhocal teibí céime`) is used to express a specific degree or extent of a quality. It is closely related to the comparative form of the adjective and is sometimes called the "abstract comparative".

### Formation

- For adjectives ending in `-ach`, `-mhar`, or a vowel, the gradual form is the same as the **comparative**.
    - *dá chrua é* (from `crua`)
    - *dá aistí é* (from `aisteach`)
    - *dá ghreannmhaire é* (from `greannmhar`)
- This also applies to adjectives whose comparative ends in `-a`.
    - *dá dheacra é* - however difficult it may be
    - *dá fhearúla é* - however manly he may be
- Other adjectives use either the **comparative** form or the comparative with `-(e)acht` or `-íocht` added.
    - `minic` -> `minice` or `minicíocht`
    - `moch` -> `moiche` or `moicheacht`
    - `ard` -> `airde` or `airdeacht`
- Some adjectives use a special form, such as `maith` (good), which uses `feabhas` (improvement, excellence) as its gradual abstract noun instead of `maitheas` or `maithe`.

<Dialect region="Munster">
In Munster, `méid` is often used instead of the standard `méad` for "size" or "amount", for example, *dá mhéid*.
</Dialect>

### Usage

The gradual abstract noun is used when indicating the extent or degree of a quality, especially in the specific grammatical structures outlined below.

- *méid na donachta* - the extent of the wickedness

## Syntax of Gradual Abstract Nouns

Gradual abstract nouns appear in several important constructions to express comparison and degree. These often involve the particles `a`, `cá`, and `dá`.

### A + Abstract Noun: Expressing 'How...'

This structure is used to make a statement about the degree of a quality, equivalent to English phrases like "how good he is" or "how little money you have".

| Structure | Example |
|---|---|
| `a` + **gradual abstract noun** + relative clause | *Is iontach a fheabhas atá sé.* - It is wonderful how good he is. |
| `a` + **gradual abstract noun** + noun + relative clause | *Inis dom a laghad airgead atá agat.* - Tell me how little money you have. |

The particle `a` causes lenition.

<Details summary="Grammatical note on 'a'">
This `a` is historically the masculine 3rd person singular possessive pronoun ("his"). A literal translation of *a fheabhas atá sé* would be "his goodness that he is". In modern Irish, it functions as a particle indicating degree rather than possession. It is always the leniting masculine form, never the form for "her".
</Details>

This construction can be combined with prepositions:

* *Fuair sé ardmholadh trina fheabhas a rinne sé an obair.* - He received great praise for how well he did the work. (`trina` = `trí` + `a`)

Certain prepositions create fixed phrases:
- `ar a laghad` - at least
- `ar a mhéad` - at most
- `ar a luaithe` - at the earliest
- `ar a dhéanaí` - at the latest
- `ar a fheabhas` - excellently

With the conjunction `agus`, it can describe a circumstance:

* *Tá sé go han-mhaith agus a óige atá sé.* - He is very good, considering how young he is.

With `ach`, it has a restrictive sense:

* *Sin é an bealach is fearr ach a dheacracht atá sé.* - That is the best way, even if it is difficult.

This structure can also be translated as "as ... as":

* *Labhramar a laghad Béarla agus ab fhéidir.* - We spoke as little English as possible.

<Note>
A common alternative for this meaning is to use `chomh` + adjective: *chomh maith agus atá sé* (as good as he is / how good he is).
</Note>

<Details summary="Note on following nouns">
A noun following the abstract noun in this structure can be in the genitive (especially for uncountable nouns like *a laghad údair*, "how little cause") or, more commonly, the nominative (*a laghad airgead*, "how little money"). Some grammars prescribe the nominative.
</Details>

### Cá + Abstract Noun: Asking 'How...?'

To form a direct or indirect question about the degree of a quality, `cá` is used.

| Structure | Example |
|---|---|
| `cá` + **gradual abstract noun** + relative clause | *Cá fheabhas atá sé?* - How good is he? |
| `cad é` + `a` + **gradual abstract noun** + relative clause | *Cad é a mhinice a bhí sí anseo?* - How often was she here? |

This `cá` is a combination of the interrogative word `cá` and the particle `a` (`cá` < `cá` + `a`), so it causes lenition.

<Dialect>
In Connacht and Munster, `cé` (from `cé` + `a`) is often used instead of `cá`, also with lenition: *cé fhad?* (how long?). Other regional forms include `cén` (*cén fhad?*) and `cad é a` or `cad é an` (*cad é an fhaid?*). In Munster, `méid` is often not lenited after `cé`: *cé méid?* (how many?).
</Dialect>

When forming an indirect question, you must use `cá` or its equivalent.

* *Níl a fhios agam cá fheabhas atá sé.* - I don't know how good he is. (Indirect question)
* *Is iontach a fheabhas atá sé.* - It is astonishing how good he is. (Not an indirect question)

<Note>
Alternatives include `cá` + adjective (without `a`, so no lenition, followed by an indirect relative clause) or `cé chomh` + adjective.
* *Cá fada a raibh tú ansin?* - How long were you there?
* *Cé chomh maith agus a bhí sé?* - How good was it?
</Note>

### Dá + Abstract Noun: Expressing 'However...'

The particle `dá` is used to mean "however..." (e.g., "however good he is"). It is formed from the preposition `de` and the particle `a` (`dá` < `de` + `a`) and causes lenition.

This construction has several variations depending on the verb and sentence structure.

#### With Verbs

- `dá` + noun + direct relative clause: *dá fheabhas a rinne sé é* - however well he did it
- `dá` + noun + indirect relative clause: *dá fheabhas a ndearna sé é* - however well he did it
- `dá` + noun + `dá` + indirect relative clause: *dá fheabhas dá ndearna sé é* - however well he did it

#### With the Verb Bí

- `dá` + noun + direct relative clause: *dá fheabhas atá sé* - however good he is
- `dá` + noun + `dá` + indirect relative clause: *dá fheabhas dá bhfuil sé* - however good he is
- `dá` + noun + subject (verb `bí` omitted): *dá dheacracht é* - however difficult it may be

#### With Nouns

- `dá` + noun + noun + relative clause: *dá mhéad dúil a bhí aige ann* - however much he liked it
- `dá` + noun + `de` + noun: *dá laghad de dhuine* - however insignificant a person
- noun + `dá` + noun (attributive): *amhras dá laghad* - the slightest doubt (literally, "a doubt, however small")

This attributive use is common in negative sentences:

* *Níl amhras dá laghad faoi.* - There is not the slightest doubt about it.

<Dialect region="Connemara">
In Connemara, `dhá` is sometimes used instead of `dá`.
</Dialect>

<Dialect region="Donegal">
An alternative construction is used in Donegal: Adjective + `agus (eile) mar` + direct relative clause.
* *maith agus eile mar atá siad* - however good they are
* *óg agus uile mar a bhí sé ag an am* - however young he was at the time
</Dialect>

### The Bigger, The Better

To express a proportional relationship like "the bigger, the better," Irish uses the `dá` construction in the first clause.

| Structure | Example |
|---|---|
| `dá` + **gradual abstract noun** ... `is ea is` + comparative | *Dá mhéad é is ea is fearr.* - The bigger it is, the better. |
| `dá` + **gradual abstract noun** ... `is amhlaidh is` + comparative | *Dá mhéad é is amhlaidh is fearr.* - The bigger it is, the better. |

This structure links the degree of one quality to the degree of another.

* *Dá laghad a gcím díot is amhlaidh is fearr dom.* - The less I see of you, the better for me.
* *Dá mhéad dá smaoiním is ea is measctha a bhíonn mo smaointe.* - The more I think, the more confused my thoughts are.

<Note>
Be careful to distinguish this proportional structure from a simple concessive clause.
- *Dá laghad é **is ea is fearr** é.* - The smaller he is, the better he is. (Proportional)
- *Dá laghad é **is fearr** é.* - However small he is, he is the better one. (Concessive)
</Note>

<Dialect region="Ulster">
In Ulster, the second part of the clause can be introduced with `sin mar is`.
* *Dá mhéad é sin mar is fearr é.* - The bigger he is, the better he is.
</Dialect>

<Dialect region="Donegal">
In Donegal, `mar is` can introduce both clauses, without using an abstract noun.
* *Mar is mó atá ann mar is fearr é.* - The more there are, the better.
* *Más gaiste a dhéanfas tú é, más mó a gheobhas muid.* - The faster you do it, the more we get. (`más` is a contraction of `mar is`)
</Dialect>

<Details summary="Other and older forms">
Several other constructions can express this proportional relationship, some of which are more common in older or literary Irish.
- **`is móide`**: The comparative with `-de` appended. *Dá neartmhaire a shéideas an ghaoth an tine is móide a lasas sí é.* (The stronger the wind blows the fire, the more it ignites it.)
- **`de réir mar a`**: Meaning "according as". *De réir mar a théann siad in aois is amhlaidh is measa a bhíonn siad.* (The older they get, the worse they are.)
- **`nuair is`**: Meaning "[always then] when...". *Nuair is mó an chruachás is é is mó an cuidiú.* (When the dilemma is greatest, the help is also greatest.)
</Details>

### Expressing Gradual Change: 'Getting Better'

To describe a process of change, such as something "getting better" or "getting worse", Irish uses the verb `téigh` (to go) with the preposition `i` (in) and a gradual abstract noun.

| Structure | Example |
|---|---|
| Form of `téigh` + `i` + **gradual abstract noun** | *Tá sé ag dul i bhfeabhas.* - It is getting better. |

* *Rachaidh sé i bhfeabhas amárach.* - It will get better tomorrow.

The preposition `chun` (to) can also be used.

* *Tá sé ag dul chun donachta.* - It is getting worse.
