Irish names have a rich history and a grammatical structure that changes depending on who is being referred to. First names are declined, and surnames change their form to indicate gender and marital status. This guide covers the main types of Irish names and how they are used.

## Personal Names

### First Names

Irish first names can be divided into two main groups: those of Gaelic origin and those from other languages.

#### Gaelic and Gaelicised Names

These names have deep roots in Irish history and mythology. Many were later adapted to sound like English names, even if they have different origins.

*   **Gaelic names:** `Tadhg`, `Niall`, `Eoghan`, `Domhnall`, `Brian`, `Conchubhar` (or `Connór`), `Diarmaid`, `Donncha`, `Cathal`.
*   **Phonetic "translations":** `Áine` became Anne, `Aoife` became Eva, `Tadhg` became Timothy, and `Eoghan` became Eugene or Owen.

Many names from Norman and Christian traditions were also Gaelicised.
*   `Seán` (from Jehan/Jean)
*   `Séamas` (from James)
*   `Searlas` (from Charles)
*   `Gearailt` (from Gérard)
*   `Annraoi` (from Henry)
*   `Micheál` (from Michael)
*   `Sinéad` (from Jeannette)
*   `Liam` (from Guilliaume/William)
*   `Peadar` (from Peter)
*   `Pól` (from Paul)
*   `Eoin` (from Johannes)

<Note>
Sometimes, the common name differs from the name of the saint it's associated with. For example, `Eoin` is the name for St. John, but the common first name is `Seán`. Similarly, the mother of Jesus is `Muire`, but the common first name is `Máire`.
</Note>

**Declension of First Names**

Male names often follow the first declension.
*   **Seán:** genitive `Sheáin`, vocative `a Sheáin`
*   **Niall:** genitive `Néill`, vocative `a Néill`
*   **Tomás:** genitive `Thomáis`, vocative `a Thomáis`

Other declensions are also common.
*   **3rd declension:** `Diarmaid` (gen. `Dhiarmada`), `Mathúin` (gen. `Mhathúna`)
*   **4th declension:** `Donncha` (gen. `Dhonncha`)

Some names can be declined in more than one way. For example, `Aonghus` can be 3rd declension (gen. `Aonghusa`) or 1st declension (gen. `Aonghuis`).

Female first names mostly belong to the 2nd or 4th declension.
*   `Niamh` (gen. `Néimhe`)
*   `Bríd` (gen. `Bhríde`)
*   `Sinéad` (gen. `Shinéad`)
*   `Síle` (gen. `Shíle`)

Female names can be formed from male names using the diminutive suffix `-ín`, as in `Póilín` (from `Pól`). This suffix can also be used for male names, like `Seáinín` for "Johnny".

If a person has multiple first names, historically the second name was put in the genitive and lenited, e.g., `Seán Pheadair`. Today, this is mainly seen in bynames that include a father's or grandfather's name.

#### English and Foreign Names

Names like Tom, Donald, John, and Janet are also common in Ireland. These names are typically not declined, lenited, or eclipsed. Many Irish people use both an English and an Irish version of their name, depending on the context.

### Bynames

Bynames, or `leasainmneacha`, are used to provide more detail about a person.

*   **Adjectives:** Adjectives like `mór` (big) and `óg` (young) function like "senior" and "junior".
    *   *Séamas Óg Ó Néill* - James O’Neill, Jr.
*   **Hair Colour:** Adjectives describing hair colour are common.
    *   *Bríd Rua* - Red-haired Bríd
    *   *Áine Bhán* - Blonde Áine
*   **Patronymics:** In the Gaeltacht, where a few surnames can be very common, people often use the names of their father and grandfather as bynames instead. These names are in the genitive and are lenited.
    *   *Seán Mhairtin Phóil* - John, son of Martin, grandson of Paul.

## Surnames

Irish surnames, or `sloinnte`, have distinct forms for men and women. They fall into several categories.

### Gaelic and Gaelicised Surnames

This is the largest group, mostly comprising patronymic names beginning with `Ó` (descendant/grandson) or `Mac` (son).

#### Names with Ó and Mac

The prefixes `Ó` and `Mac` refer to a distant ancestor, not one's immediate father or grandfather. These fixed surnames have been in use since the 11th and 12th centuries. `Ó` is more common, while `Mac` is found more frequently in the north of Ireland and is the standard in Scotland.

<Details summary="Historical Note: `mac` vs. `Mac`">
  Before `Ó` and `Mac` became fixed surnames, they were used literally to mean "son of" or "grandson of". In these cases, the word was written in lowercase. For example, the legendary hero *Fionn mac Cumhaill* means "Fionn, son of Cumhall".
</Details>

The name of the ancestor follows the prefix in the genitive case.
*   `Ó Braonáin` (descendant of Braonán) - Anglicised as (O')Brennan
*   `Ó Dónaill` (descendant of Domhnall) - Anglicised as O'Donnell
*   `Mac Cárthaigh` (son of Cárthach) - Anglicised as MacCarthy
*   `Mac Diarmada` (son of Diarmaid) - Anglicised as MacDermot

Some names include the elements `Maoil` (follower, devotee) or `Giolla` (servant, youth), often followed by a saint's name.
*   `Mac Giolla Phádraig` (son of the servant of Patrick) - MacGilpatrick
*   `Ó Maoil Eoin` (descendant of the follower of John) - O'Malone

The element `Con-` (from `cú`, dog/warrior) is part of the following name and is not lenited.
*   `Mac Conmara` (son of the hound of the sea) - MacNamara

Some surnames derive from a profession or origin, often using `Mac` and the article `an`.
*   `Mac an Ghabhann` (son of the smith) - MacGowan
*   `Mac an Bhaird` (son of the bard) - Ward
*   `Mac an tSaoir` (son of the craftsman) - MacAteer/McIntyre

The spelling of many surnames was updated during the spelling reform of the 1940s.
*   `Ó Seaghdha` became `Ó Sé`
*   `Ó Murchadha` became `Ó Murchú`
*   `Mac Mathghamhna` became `Mac Mathúna`

#### Forms for Men

A man's surname begins with `Ó` or `Mac`. `Mag` is a variant of `Mac` used before vowels and certain consonants.
*   After `Ó`, a following vowel takes an h-prefix: `Seán Ó hAonghusa`.
*   After `Mac`, the following name may be lenited, especially in younger names: `Liam Mac Phaidín`.
*   In the genitive case, `Ó` becomes `Uí` and `Mac` becomes `Mhic`. Both cause lenition.
    *   *teach Sheáin Uí Mhurchú* - Seán Murphy's house
    *   *teach Thomáis Mhic Mhathúna* - Thomas MacMahon's house

#### Forms for Unmarried Women

For an unmarried woman, `Ó` is replaced by `Ní` (from `Iníon Uí`, daughter of the descendant) and `Mac` is replaced by `Nic` (from `Iníon Mhic`, daughter of the son). Both `Ní` and `Nic` cause lenition.
*   `Máire Ní Cheallaigh` (Mary O'Kelly)
*   `Áine Nic Mhathúna` (Ann MacMahon)

With some `Mac-` names, `Ní` is used instead of `Nic`, where the `Mhic` component is dropped entirely.
*   `Mairghréad Ní tSuibhne` (Margret MacSweeny)

#### Forms for Married Women

When a woman takes her husband's surname, the prefix `Bean` (woman/wife) is used. The husband's surname is put into the genitive: `Ó` becomes `Uí`, and `Mac` becomes `Mhic`. The word `Bean` is often omitted in speech.
*   `Máire (Bean) Uí Cheallaigh` (Mrs. Mary O'Kelly)
*   `Áine (Bean) Mhic Mhathúna` (Mrs. Ann MacMahon)

A woman can also use a double-barrelled name, combining her maiden name and married name.
*   *Máire Nic Eochagáin Uí Chuinn* - Máire Geoghegan-Quinn

<Dialect region="Ulster">
In Ulster, it is common for married women to use the same forms as for their maiden name (`Ní` and `Nic`) instead of `Uí` and `Mhic`.
</Dialect>

#### The -(e)ach Form

Surnames with `Ó` and `Mac` can be turned into substantivised adjectives ending in `-(e)ach`. This form is always used with the definite article and without a first name. It is used to refer to someone by their surname alone, similar to saying "O'Neill" in English.

*   `Ó Néill` → `an Niallach`
*   `Mac Cárthaigh` → `an Cárthach`

This form is used in several contexts:
*   **Referring to a man:** *Tháinig an Niallach isteach.* - O'Neill came in.
*   **Addressing a man (vocative):** *a Cheallaigh!* - Mr. O'Kelly!
*   **For "Mrs.":** *Bean an Cheallaigh* - Mrs. O'Kelly.
*   **For a family (plural):** *na Ceallaigh* - the O'Kellys.

<Details summary="Cultural Note: Using Surnames as Titles">
  In older Gaelic culture, using a surname like `Ó Néill` on its own, without a first name, was reserved as a title for the clan chief. To refer to an ordinary person, you would use the `-(e)ach` form (`an Niallach`) or their full name (`Pádaí Ó Néill`). Today, using the surname alone still implies a reference to a particularly famous bearer of that name.
</Details>

#### Formal Titles and Family Names

Here is a summary of how to form common titles.

*   **Mr.:** Use the `-(e)ach` form with the article (`an Mathúnach`) or `Mac Uí` (`Mac Uí Mhathúna`). For formal address, `an tUasal` is used: `an tUasal Seán Ó Sé`.
*   **Mrs.:** `Bean Uí` (for `Ó` names) or `Bean Mhic` (for `Mac` names). `Bean an` + the genitive of the `-(e)ach` form is also used: `Bean an Chadhnaigh`.
*   **Miss:** `Iníon Uí` or `Iníon Mhic`. The newer title "Ms." can be translated as `An Iníon Ní Cheallaigh`.
*   **The ... Family:** `Muintir` + the ancestor's name for `Ó` names (`Muintir Cheallaigh`). `Clann Mhic` for `Mac` names (`Clann Mhic Mhathúna`). The plural of the `-(e)ach` form is also common: `na Ceallaigh`.

#### Anglicised Forms

In English, `Ó` becomes `O'` and `Mac` is often shortened to `Mc`. These prefixes are attached without a space, and the following name is capitalised (e.g., O'Sullivan, MacCarthy). Over time, many families dropped the `O'` or `Mac` entirely (e.g., Kelly, Byrne).

Some names of Norman origin use `Fitz-` (from French *fils*, son) instead of `Mac`, as in `FitzGerald` (`Mac Gearailt`).

### Surnames Ending in -(e)ach

A small number of common surnames are substantivised adjectives ending in `-(e)ach` and do not have an `Ó` or `Mac` form. They often indicate origin.
*   `Breathnach` (Welsh) - Anglicised as Walsh, Wallace
*   `Caomhánach` (follower of St. Caomhán) - Kavanagh
*   `Cinnseallach` - Kinsella

For women, these surnames are lenited: `Áine Bhreathnach`. They are declined like first-declension nouns for male bearers.

### Surnames with de

Surnames beginning with `de` are of Anglo-Norman origin, corresponding to the French *de* (from). It is not a sign of nobility.
*   `de Búrca` - Burke
*   `de Buitléir` - Butler
*   `de Paor` - Power

The form is the same for men and women (`Seán de Paor`, `Máire de Paor`) and does not change in the genitive. These names can also form an `-(e)ach` version, such as `an Búrcach` for (Mr.) Burke.

### Other Gaelicised Surnames

Many names of early Anglo-Norman, English, and Welsh settlers were Gaelicised without adding `Ó`, `Mac`, or `de`.
*   `Céitinn` - Keating
*   `Daltún` - Dalton
*   `Seoigh` - Joyce

These names are generally not lenited after a first name. They can also form `-(e)ach` versions, like `an Céitinneach` for (Mr.) Keating.

### Summary of Surname Forms

Here are examples showing the different forms for common surnames.

#### Ó Briain (O'Brian)
*   **Man:** `Seán Ó Briain`
*   **Unmarried Woman:** `Síle Ní Bhriain`
*   **Married Woman:** `Úna Uí Bhriain`
*   **Mr. O'Brian:** `an Brianach`
*   **The O'Brians:** `Muintir Bhriain` or `na Brianaigh`

#### Mac Mathúna (MacMahon)
*   **Man:** `Seán Mac Mathúna`
*   **Unmarried Woman:** `Síle Nic Mhathúna`
*   **Married Woman:** `Úna Mhic Mhathúna`
*   **Mr. MacMahon:** `an Mathúnach`
*   **The MacMahons:** `Clann Mhic Mhathúna` or `na Mathúnaigh`

#### Breathnach (Walsh)
*   **Man:** `Seán Breathnach`
*   **Unmarried/Married Woman:** `Úna Bhreathnach`
*   **Mr. Walsh:** `an Breathnach`
*   **The Walshes:** `na Breathnaigh`

#### de Búrca (Burke)
*   **Man:** `Seán de Búrca`
*   **Unmarried/Married Woman:** `Úna de Búrca`
*   **Mr. Burke:** `an Búrcach`
*   **The Burkes:** `Muintir de Búrca` or `na Búrcaigh`

#### Céitinn (Keating)
*   **Man:** `Seán Céitinn`
*   **Unmarried/Married Woman:** `Úna Céitinn`
*   **Mr. Keating:** `an Céitinneach`
*   **The Keatings:** `Muintir Chéitinn` or `na Céitinnigh`

## Forms of Address

When addressing someone directly, Irish uses the vocative case, which is signalled by the particle `a`. This particle causes lenition.

*   First names in the first declension change their ending: `Seán` becomes `a Sheáin!`.
*   Surnames also change:
    *   `Ó` becomes `Uí`: *a Sheáin Uí Cheallaigh!* (John O'Kelly!)
    *   `Mac` becomes `Mhic`: *a Phóil Mhic Mhathúna!* (Paul MacMahon!)
    *   `-(e)ach` becomes `-(a)igh`: *a Phóil Bhreathnaigh!* (Paul Walsh!)
    *   `Bean` becomes `Bhean`: *a Mháire Bhean Uí Cheallaigh!* (Mrs. Mary O'Kelly!)

General forms of address are:
*   **Sir:** `a dhuine uasail!`
*   **Madam:** `a bhean uasal!`
*   **Ladies and Gentlemen:** `a dhaoine uaisle!`

## Place Names

Most places in Ireland have both an Irish and an English name. The Irish name is usually the original, with the English name often being a phonetic approximation. In Gaeltacht (Irish-speaking) areas, only the Irish names are official.

Irish place names are often descriptive, containing elements like `cill` (church), `baile` (town), `cnoc` (hill), and `áth` (ford).

<Note>
Some place names retain older grammatical features, such as eclipsis after what were once neuter nouns. You might see `Loch nEathach` (Lough Neagh) or `An Muileann gCearr` (Mullingar).
</Note>

The names of three provinces—Ulster (`Ulaidh`), Leinster (`Laighin`), and Connacht (`Connachta`)—are historically plural nouns, originally referring to the peoples of those regions. Munster (`an Mhumhain`) is a singular noun. Today, the province names are most often seen in the genitive case with the word `Cúige` (province), as in `Cúige Uladh` (Province of Ulster).

Many river and country names use the definite article, such as `an Life` (the Liffey), `an Ghearmáin` (Germany), and `an Fhrainc` (France). Exceptions include `Éire` (Ireland) and `Sasana` (England).
