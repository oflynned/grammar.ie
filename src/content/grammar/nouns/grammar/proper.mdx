Proper nouns (`ainmneacha dílse`) in Irish, such as personal names and place names, follow specific grammatical rules for declension, mutation, and forms of address.

## Personal Names

### First Names (`na réamhainmneacha`)

Irish first names can be divided into two main groups.

#### Gaelic and Gaelicised Names

These names are of Irish origin or have been adapted into the Irish language over centuries.

*   **Gaelic names:** Examples include `Tadhg`, `Niall`, `Eoghan`, `Domhnall`, `Brian`, `Conchubhar`, `Diarmaid`, and `Donncha`. Many have been given English "translations" based on phonetic similarity, such as `Áine` (Anne), `Aoife` (Eva), `Tadhg` (Timothy), and `Eoghan` (Eugene/Owen).
*   **Gaelicised names:** Norman and Christian names were widely adapted, such as `Seán` (from Jean), `Séamas` (James), `Liam` (William), `Peadar` (Peter), and `Sinéad` (Jeannette). The name for the Virgin Mary is `Muire`, but the common first name is `Máire`.

**Declension of First Names**

*   **Male names** often follow the 1st declension.
    *   `Seán` (genitive `Sheáin`, vocative `a Sheáin`)
    *   `Niall` (genitive `Néill`, vocative `a Néill`)
    *   `Tomás` (genitive `Thomáis`, vocative `a Thomáis`)
*   Other declensions are also common.
    *   **3rd declension:** `Diarmaid` (genitive `Dhiarmada`)
    *   **4th declension:** `Donncha` (genitive `Dhonncha`)
*   **Female names** mostly belong to the 2nd or 4th declension.
    *   `Niamh` (genitive `Néimhe`)
    *   `Bríd` (genitive `Bhríde`)
    *   `Síle` (genitive `Shíle`)
*   The diminutive suffix `-ín` can form female names (`Nóirín`, `Róisín`) or affectionate male names (`Seáinín` for Johnny).

#### English and Foreign Names

Names like Tom, Donald, or Janet are typically not declined or mutated in Irish. Many Irish people use both an English and an Irish form of their name, depending on the context.

### Bynames (`na leasainmneacha`)

Bynames are used to distinguish between people with the same name.

*   **Adjectives:** `mór` (big/senior) and `óg` (young/junior) are common.
    *   *Séamas Óg Ó Néill* - James O’Neill, Jr.
*   **Hair colour:** Adjectives like `rua` (red) and `bán` (fair/blonde) are also used.
    *   *Bríd Rua* - Red-haired Bríd
    *   *Aodh Rua Ó Domhnaill* - Red Hugh O'Donnell
*   **Patronymics:** In the Gaeltacht, it is common to use the genitive, lenited names of the father and grandfather instead of a surname.
    *   *Seán Mhairtin Phóil* - John, son of Martin, grandson of Paul.

## Surnames (`na sloinnte`)

Irish surnames have distinct forms for men and women. They fall into several categories.

### Gaelic and Gaelicised Surnames

This is the largest group, mostly comprising patronymic names beginning with `Ó` or `Mac`.

#### Names with `Ó` and `Mac`

These are the most common types of Irish surname.

*   `Ó` (or the older spelling `Ua`) means "descendant" or "grandson".
*   `Mac` (sometimes written `’ac` and pronounced /ək/) means "son".

These prefixes refer to a distant ancestor, not the immediate father or grandfather. The name following the prefix is in the genitive case.

*   `Ó Braonáin` (descendant of Braonán) - (O')Brennan
*   `Ó Dónaill` (descendant of Domhnall) - O'Donnell
*   `Mac Cárthaigh` (son of Cárthach) - MacCarthy
*   `Mac Diarmada` (son of Diarmaid) - MacDermot

**Common Components in Surnames**

*   **`Maoil` or `Giolla`**: These mean "follower" and "servant" respectively, and are usually followed by the name of a saint.
    *   `Mac Giolla Phádraig` - son of the servant of Patrick (Fitzpatrick)
    *   `Ó Maoil Eoin` - descendant of the follower of John (O'Malone)
*   **`Con-`**: This is the genitive of `cú` (dog/hound) and is used metaphorically to mean "warrior" or "champion". It is written as part of the following word and is not lenited.
    *   `Mac Conmara` - son of the hound of the sea (MacNamara)
*   **Professions**: Some names derive from an ancestor's profession, often using `Mac` and the article `an`.
    *   `Mac an Ghabhann` - son of the smith (MacGowan)
    *   `Mac an Bhaird` - son of the bard (Ward)

> **Note on Historical Usage**
> Before `Ó` and `Mac` became fixed surnames, they referred to the immediate father or grandfather and were written in lowercase, e.g., *Fionn mac Cumhaill* (Fionn, son of Cumhall).

#### Forms for Men

A man's surname begins with `Ó` or `Mac` (or `Mag` before a vowel).

*   After `Ó`, a following vowel takes an h-prefix: `Ó hÓgáin`.
*   After `Mac`, the following consonant may be lenited in younger names: `Mac Phaidín`.
*   **Genitive:** `Ó` becomes `Uí`, and `Mac` becomes `Mhic`. Both cause lenition.
    *   *teach Sheáin Uí Mhurchú* - Seán Murphy's house
    *   *teach Thomáis Mhic Mhathúna* - Thomas MacMahon's house

#### Forms for Unmarried Women

An unmarried woman's surname (her birth name) uses `Ní` or `Nic`.

*   `Ní` is used for `Ó` names (from `Iníon Uí`, "daughter of the descendant of").
*   `Nic` is used for `Mac` names (from `Iníon Mhic`, "daughter of the son of").
*   Both `Ní` and `Nic` cause lenition.
    *   `Máire Ní Cheallaigh` (Mary O'Kelly)
    *   `Áine Nic Mhathúna` (Ann MacMahon)

In some cases, particularly with certain `Mac` names, `Ní` is used instead of `Nic`, representing `Iníon` ("daughter") alone.

*   `Mairghréad Ní tSuibhne` (Margaret MacSweeny)

#### Forms for Married Women

A woman who takes her husband's surname prefixes `Bean` ("woman/wife"). The surname that follows is in the genitive.

*   For `Ó` names, this becomes `(Bean) Uí`.
*   For `Mac` names, this becomes `(Bean) Mhic`.
*   The word `Bean` is often omitted. Both `Uí` and `Mhic` cause lenition.
    *   `Máire Uí Cheallaigh` (Mrs. O'Kelly)
    *   `Áine Mhic Mhathúna` (Mrs. MacMahon)

In Ulster, it is common for married women to continue using the `Ní`/`Nic` forms.

#### The `-(e)ach` Suffix

This form is created from the ancestor's name in the nominative plus the suffix `-(e)ach`. It is used with the definite article but **without a first name**.

*   `Ó Néill` (from `Niall`) → `an Niallach` (O'Neill)
*   `Mac Cárthaigh` (from `Cárthach`) → `an Cárthach` (MacCarthy)

This form is used:
*   To refer to a man without his first name: *Tháinig an Niallach isteach.* (O'Neill came in.)
*   In the vocative to address a man: `a Cheallaigh!` (Mr. O'Kelly!)
*   For a married woman, with `Bean`: `Bean an Cheallaigh` (Mrs. O'Kelly).
*   In the plural for a family: `na Ceallaigh` (the O'Kellys).

> **Note on Usage**
> Using the bare surname (e.g., `Ó Néill`) without a first name or article was historically a title reserved for a clan chief. To refer to an ordinary person, one must use the `-(e)ach` form (`an Niallach`) or include their first name (`Pádaí Ó Néill`).

#### Anglicised Forms

In English, `Ó` becomes `O’` and `Mac` is often shortened to `Mc`. These prefixes are attached without a space, and the following name part is capitalised (e.g., O'Sullivan, MacCarthy). These forms are used for both men and women.

### Surnames Ending in `-(e)ach`

A small number of common surnames are substantivised adjectives ending in `-(e)ach` and do not have an `Ó` or `Mac` form. They often indicate origin.

*   `Breathnach` (Welsh) - Walsh
*   `Caomhánach` (of St. Caomhán) - Kavanagh
*   `Cinnseallach` - Kinsella

For women, the surname is lenited after the first name: `Áine Bhreathnach`.

### Surnames with `de`

These names are of Anglo-Norman origin, where `de` corresponds to French *de* ("from").

*   `de Búrca` - Burke
*   `de Paor` - Power
*   `de hÍde` - Hyde

The form is the same for men and women (`Seán de Búrca`, `Máire de Búrca`) and does not change in the genitive.

## Forms of Address

When addressing someone directly, Irish uses the vocative case, preceded by the particle `a`, which causes lenition.

*   **First Names:** Male names in the 1st declension change to their genitive form.
    *   `Seán` → `a Sheáin!`
    *   `Séamas` → `a Shéamais!`
*   **Surnames:**
    *   `Ó` becomes `Uí`: `a Sheáin Uí Cheallaigh!`
    *   `Mac` becomes `Mhic`: `a Phóil Mhic Mhathúna!`
    *   Names ending in `-(e)ach` change to `-(a)igh`: `a Phóil Bhreathnaigh!`

### General Forms of Address

*   **Sir:** `a dhuine uasail!`
*   **Madam:** `a bhean uasal!`
*   **Ladies and Gentlemen:** `a dhaoine uaisle!`

## Place Names (`na logainmneacha`)

Most Irish places have both an Irish and an English name. The Irish name is usually the original, with the English name often being a phonetic corruption.

*   **Grammar:** Place names often preserve archaic grammatical features, such as eclipsis after a formerly neuter noun: `Loch nEathach` (Lough Neagh), `An Muileann gCearr` (Mullingar).
*   **Gender and Article:** Place names have a grammatical gender. Many river and country names use the definite article: `an Life` (the Liffey), `an Ghearmáin` (Germany). Others do not: `Éire` (Ireland), `Sasana` (England).

### Provinces and Regions

The names for Ulster (`Ulaidh`), Leinster (`Laighin`), Connacht (`Connachta`), and England (`Sasana`) were originally plural nouns referring to the peoples of those regions. Today, they are mostly used in the genitive with `Cúige` (province), e.g., `Cúige Uladh`. Munster (`an Mhumhain`) has always been a singular noun.

Many traditional landscape names refer to the clans who lived there, such as `Tír Chonaill` ("Conall's Land") for Donegal or `Tír Eoghain` ("Eoghan's Land") for Tyrone.
