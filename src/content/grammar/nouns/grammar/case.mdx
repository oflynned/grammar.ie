Irish has five grammatical cases (`tuisil`): nominative, genitive, dative, accusative, and vocative.

In the official standard, the nominative, accusative, and dative forms of a noun are often identical. For this reason, some grammars group them into a single "common case". However, distinct dative forms survive in dialects and older literature, and the rules for initial mutations differ between them. This guide treats all five cases separately to provide a complete reference.

## Nominative Case (`An Tuiseal Ainmneach`)

The nominative is the base form of a noun, as you would find it in a dictionary.

### Usage of the Nominative

The nominative case is used in several contexts:

-   **As the subject of a sentence:**
    -   *Itheann an fear.* (The man eats.)
-   **As the subject and predicate noun in a copula sentence:**
    -   *Is é an dochtúir an fear.* (The man is the doctor.)
-   **After certain prepositions:**
    -   The prepositions `seachas` (besides/except), `go dtí` (to/until), and `murach` (if not for) are followed by the nominative.
-   **As a qualitative nominative (`an t-ainmneach cáilíochtúil`):**
    -   This structure uses a noun in the nominative to describe a quality, often with a comparative adjective.
    -   *an fear is mó cáil* (the man of greatest fame; literally "the man who is greater fame")
    -   *an té is ard glór* (the one with a loud voice; literally "the one who is high voice")
-   **As an absolute nominative (`an t-ainmneach leithliseach`):**
    -   This is used to emphasise a noun by placing it at the beginning or end of a sentence.
    -   *An fear a chonaic mé inné, bhí sé ag dul abhaile.* (The man I saw yesterday, he was going home.)

## Genitive Case (`An Tuiseal Ginideach`)

The genitive case primarily shows possession or relationship. A noun in the genitive case almost always follows the noun it modifies.

### Usage of the Genitive

-   **As an attribute of another noun**, indicating:
    -   **Possession:** *teach an fhir* (the man's house)
    -   **Origin:** *mac Sheáin* (Seán's son)
    -   **Material:** *teach adhmaid* (a wooden house; literally "a house of wood")
    -   **Content:** *bosca chíste* (a box of biscuits)
    -   **Purpose:** *bróg cailín* (a girl's shoe)
    -   **Measurement:** *leanbh bliana* (a one-year-old child)
    -   **Apposition:** *baintreach fir* (a widower; literally "a widow of a man")
-   **As the object of a verbal noun:**
    -   *Tá sé ag déanamh na hoibre.* (He is doing the work.)
    -   *Tá sé ag cur báistí.* (It is raining; literally "It is at putting of rain.")
-   **After certain prepositions** that are themselves derived from nouns, such as `chun` (to), `trasna` (across), `timpeall` (around), and `tar éis` (after).
    -   *trasna an ghoirt* (across the field)
-   **After quantifiers** like `mórán` (much), `beagán` (a little), `neart` (plenty), `roinnt` (some), and `cuid` (part/share).
    -   *roinnt bainne* (some milk)

### The Functional Genitive

When a noun phrase that already contains a definite genitive attribute is itself used as a genitive, a "double genitive" is usually avoided. Instead, only the final noun takes the genitive form. The preceding noun in the chain remains in its nominative form but is lenited. This is known as the functional genitive.

-   *teach **mhac** Sheáin* (the house of Seán's son)
    -   Here, `mac` is functionally genitive but appears in its nominative form `mac` (lenited to `mhac`), while `Seán` takes the genitive form `Sheáin`.
-   *stair **Phoblacht** na hÉireann* (the history of the Republic of Ireland)
-   *fuinneog **theach** **mhac** Sheáin* (the window of the house of Seán's son)

### Double Genitive

A true double genitive, where two consecutive nouns both take genitive forms, occurs mainly when at least one of the genitives is indefinite, forming a semantic unit like a compound noun.

-   *[[scoil phobail] chathrach]* (a city community school)
-   *[[fear ionaid] an rí]* (the king's representative)

In Ulster Irish, a double genitive is common after `cuid` (share/portion) where other dialects would use a single genitive.

-   Ulster: *cuid Gaeilge an gharda* (the policeman's Irish)
-   Standard: *Gaeilge an gharda*

### Partitive Genitive and Dative

To express a part of a whole, Irish uses either the partitive genitive or the partitive dative.

-   **Partitive Genitive:** Used with **indefinite** nouns.
    -   *roinnt airgid* (some money)
    -   *ceathrú úill* (a quarter of an apple)
-   **Partitive Dative:** Used with **definite** nouns, employing the preposition `de`.
    -   *cuid den airgead* (a part of the money)
    -   *roinnt de na fir* (some of the men)

### Definite and Indefinite Genitive Attributes

-   A **definite genitive attribute** (e.g., `an fhir`, `Sheáin`) makes the entire noun phrase definite. You cannot add another article.
    -   *teach an dochtúra* means "the doctor's house", not "a doctor's house".
    -   To say "a doctor's house", you must use a prepositional phrase: *teach don dochtúir*.
-   An **indefinite genitive attribute** often functions like the first part of a compound word. The article, if used, precedes the main noun.
    -   *scian phóca* (a pocketknife)
    -   *an scian phóca* (the pocketknife)

## Dative Case (`An Tuiseal Tabharthach`)

The dative case is used after most simple prepositions. In modern standard Irish, its form is almost always identical to the nominative.

### Usage of the Dative

-   **After most simple prepositions**, including `ag`, `ar`, `as`, `de`, `do`, `faoi`, `i`, `le`, `ó`, `roimh`, and `trí`.
-   **For the indirect object**, where a preposition like `do` (to/for) is required.
    -   *Tugaim an leabhar don bhean.* (I give the book to the woman.)
-   **To show disadvantage**, often with `ar` (on).
    -   *Bhris mé an claí ar an bhfear.* (I broke the man's fence; implies a disadvantage to him.)
-   **As a possessive dative** with `le` or `do` for indefinite relationships.
    -   *cara dom* (a friend of mine) vs. *mo chara* (my friend)
-   **As a partitive dative** with `de` (see above).
-   **As a dative of agent** to indicate the logical subject in certain passive or subjectless constructions.
    -   *Is as Dóire dom.* (I am from Derry.)
    -   *Tá an litir le scríobh agam.* (The letter is to be written by me.)

### Dative Forms

While the dative form is usually the same as the nominative, a few nouns retain a distinct dative singular form in the standard language. These are mostly feminine nouns of the second declension, where the dative is formed by palatalisation (making the final consonant slender).

| Nominative | Dative   | Meaning   |
| :--------- | :------- | :-------- |
| `cos`      | `cois`   | foot      |
| `lámh`     | `láimh`  | hand      |
| `bróg`     | `bróig`  | shoe      |
| `cluas`    | `cluais` | ear       |
| `Éire`     | `Éirinn` | Ireland   |

In some dialects, particularly in Connacht and Ulster, these dative forms (e.g., `Éirinn`, `bróig`) are often used as the default nominative form.

### Initial Mutations in the Dative

Mutations in the dative depend on the preposition and whether the noun is definite (i.e., preceded by an article).

-   **Without an article:** Some prepositions cause lenition (`do`, `de`, `ó`), one causes eclipsis (`i`), and others cause no mutation (`ag`, `as`, `le`).
-   **With the singular article:** The rules vary by dialect. The standard allows for either lenition or eclipsis after many prepositions (e.g., *ar an mbád* or *ar an bhád*). However, after `den`, `don`, and `sa(n)`, lenition is standard.

## Accusative Case (`An Tuiseal Cuspóireach`)

The accusative case is used for the direct object of a verb. Its form is always identical to the nominative in both singular and plural.

### Usage of the Accusative

-   **As the direct object of a verb:**
    -   *Chonaic mé an fear.* (I saw the man.)
-   **In adverbial expressions of time and distance:**
    -   *Bhí mé ann an oíche sin.* (I was there that night.)
    -   *Chuaigh mé míle.* (I went a mile.)
-   **After the prepositions** `ach` (but), `gan` (without), `idir` (between), `mar` (as/like), and `ná` (than). Note that `idir` takes the dative with plural nouns.

## Vocative Case (`An Tuiseal Gairmeach`)

The vocative case is used for direct address.

### Usage and Formation

The vocative is marked by the particle `a`, which is placed before the noun and causes lenition.

-   *Dia dhuit, a Sheáin!* (Hello, Seán!)

The form of the noun and any following adjectives depends on the noun's declension and gender.

-   **Nouns:**
    -   **1st Declension:** The vocative singular form is the same as the genitive singular.
        -   *fear* (man) → *a fhir!*
    -   **2nd–5th Declension:** The vocative singular form is the same as the nominative singular.
        -   *cailín* (girl) → *a chailín!*
-   **Adjectives:**
    -   An adjective following a vocative noun is **always lenited**.
    -   After a **masculine** noun, the adjective takes its genitive singular form.
        -   *a fhir mhóir!* (big man!)
    -   After a **feminine** noun, the adjective takes its nominative singular form.
        -   *a bhean mhór!* (big woman!)

### Special Cases

-   In surnames, `Mac` becomes `Mhic` and `Ó` becomes `Uí`.
    -   *a Sheáin Mhic Mhathúna!* (Seán MacMahon!)
    -   *a Phóil Uí Shé!* (Paul O'Shay!)
-   The vocative form is generally not used for terms of endearment, collective nouns, or multi-word titles, although the particle `a` and lenition are still required.
    -   *a stór!* (darling!)
    -   *a phobal!* (people!)
    -   *a Cheann Comhairle!* (Chairman!)
