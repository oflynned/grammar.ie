Irish has three grammatical numbers (`uimhreacha`): the singular (`an t-uatha`), the plural (`an t-iolra`), and remnants of the dual (`an déach`).

## The Singular (`an t-uatha`)

The nominative singular is the basic dictionary form of a noun. It is used for:

-   A single instance of a countable noun.
-   Uncountable nouns.
-   Countable nouns after the numbers 1, 3–10, 13–19, and multiples of ten (20, 30, etc.).
-   After `cúpla` (a couple/some) and `cá mhéad` (how many).

## The Plural (`an t-iolra`)

Plural formation in Irish can be highly irregular, with many variations across dialects. It is not uncommon for a single noun to have several accepted plural forms. Two main types of plural formation are distinguished: the weak plural and the strong plural.

### Weak Plural (`an lagiolra`)

A plural is considered "weak" because the noun stem is only slightly modified, often by changing the quality of the final consonant (palatalisation/depalatalisation) or by adding a vowel. A consonantal ending that adds a syllable is absent.

-   **1st Declension**: The nominative plural is often identical to the genitive singular.
    -   *leabhar* (book) → *leabhair* (books)
    -   Sometimes it takes an `-a` ending: *cleas* (trick) → *cleasa* (tricks)
-   **2nd Declension**: The plural ending is typically `-a`.
    -   *bróg* (shoe) → *bróga* (shoes)
-   **3rd Declension**: A weak plural is rare, but uses the ending `-a`.
    -   *dreas* (bramble) → *dreasa* (brambles)

In weak plurals, the genitive plural form is identical to the nominative singular. In some dialects, strong plural endings are often preferred over weak ones (e.g., `nascanna` instead of `naisc` for the plural of `nasc`).

### Strong Plural (`an tréaniolra`)

A plural is "strong" because the stem is augmented with a consonantal ending, adding a syllable. Common endings include `-í`, `-acha`, `-anna`, and `-tha`.

-   *bás* (death) → *básanna* (deaths)
-   *cailín* (girl) → *cailíní* (girls)

Historically, these endings were modified for case (e.g., nominative `-anna`, genitive `-ann`, dative `-annaibh`), but in Modern Irish, the strong plural ending remains the same in the nominative, genitive, dative, and vocative cases.

While these endings were once restricted to certain declensions, they have spread widely in the modern language due to their clarity.

**Dialectal Forms**

In some dialects, particularly in Connacht, plural endings are often combined:

-   `-aí` (instead of `-a`)
-   `-thaí` (from `-tha` + `-í`)
-   `-annaí` (from `-anna` + `-í`)
-   `-achaí` (from `-acha` + `-í`)

The `-í` element likely derives from the old dative plural ending `-ibh`, which is pronounced /iː/ in Connacht. In parts of Co. Mayo (Erris), this additional `-í` is sometimes dropped if the noun is followed by an adjective or a genitive attribute.

-   *an mhéar* (the finger) → *na méaraí* (the fingers)
-   BUT: *na méara beaga* (the small fingers)

## Counting Plural (`an t-iolra comhairimh`)

While the singular form is typically used with numbers from 3–10, a special "counting plural" is used for certain nouns, mostly those denoting units of measurement (time, quantity, length, money). This form is always a weak plural.

For some nouns, the counting plural is a distinct form that is only used with numbers, while a different (often strong) plural is used in other contexts.

| Singular | Meaning | Counting Plural | Ordinary Plural | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `aird` | direction | `airde` | `airde` | *as ceithre hairde* (from four directions) |
| `bliain` | year | **`bliana`** | `blianta` | *trí bliana* (three years) |
| `uair` | hour, time | **`uaire`** | `uaireanta` | *ceithre huaire* (four hours, four times) |
| `seachtain` | week | **`seachtaine`** | `seachtainí` | *cúig seachtaine* (five weeks) |
| `ceann` | piece | `cinn` | `cinn`, `ceanna` | *sé cinn* (six pieces) |
| `cloigeann` | head | `cloigne` | `cloigne` | *seacht gcloigne déag fear* (17 men) |
| `orlach` | inch | `orlaí` | `orlaí` | *ocht n-orlaí* (eight inches) |
| `troigh` | foot | `troithe` | `troithe` | *naoi dtroithe* (nine feet) |
| `slat` | yard | `slata` | `slata` | *cúig slata* (five yards) |
| `pingin` | penny | **`pingine`** | `pinginí` | *deich bpingine* (ten pence) |
| `scilling` | shilling | **`scillinge`** | `scillingí` | *trí scillinge* (three shillings) |
| `ubh` | egg | **`uibhe`** | `uibheacha` | *cúig uibhe* (five eggs) |
| `trian` | third | **`treana`** | `trianta` | *trí treana* (three thirds) |
| `fiche` | twenty | **`fichid`** | `fichidí` | *trí fichid fear* (sixty men) |

**Usage Notes:**

-   When nouns for units of measurement are used as ordinary nouns, they take the singular form after a number: *trí slata* (three yards) but *trí slat* (three sticks).
-   For `uair`, the singular is used in the phrase `uair an chloig`: *trí huaire* (three hours) but *trí uair an chloig* (three o'clock / three hours).

## The Dual (`an déach`)

The dual is a remnant of an older grammatical number used for pairs of things. In Modern Irish, it only appears with the number two (`dhá`).

The **form of the dual** is identical to the dative singular. This is most noticeable with nouns for paired body parts.

-   *cos* (one foot, nominative singular)
-   *ar an gcois* (on the foot, dative singular)
-   *dhá chois* (two feet, nominative dual)
-   *ar dhá chois* (on two feet, dative dual)

If a special dual form does not exist or is not common, the singular form is used after `dhá`.

-   *dhá bhád* (two boats)

### Remnants of the Dual

1.  **The Dual Article**: The article used with `dhá` is the singular article `an`, not the plural `na`.
    -   *an dá bhád* (the two boats)
    -   Compare: *na trí bhád* (the three boats)

2.  **The Dual Noun Form**: The noun takes its dative singular form.
    -   *dhá bhróig* (two shoes)
    -   *dhá láimh* (two hands)

3.  **Adjectives in the Dual**: An adjective following a dual noun takes its nominative plural form.
    -   *an dá bhád mhóra* (the two big boats)

4.  **Adjectives after `beirt`**: Adjectives also take the nominative plural form after `beirt` (two people).
    -   *an bheirt bhan óga* (the two young women)

Verbs and pronouns referring to a dual noun are in the plural.

-   *Thángadar an dá chapall anall.* (The two horses came over.)
-   *an dá chapall* → *siad* (they)

## Plural Forms by Case

### Genitive Plural (`an ginideach iolra`)

The form of the genitive plural depends on whether the noun has a weak or strong plural.

-   **For weak plurals**, the genitive plural is identical to the **nominative singular**.
    -   *an fear* (the man) → *na bhfear* (of the men)
    -   *an bhróg* (the shoe) → *na mbróg* (of the shoes)
    -   *an tsúil* (the eye) → *na súl* (of the eyes)
-   **For strong plurals**, the genitive plural is identical to the **nominative plural**.
    -   *na cailíní* (the girls) → *na gcailíní* (of the girls)
-   **For some 5th declension nouns**, the genitive plural is identical to the **genitive singular**.
    -   *an cara* (the friend) → *an charad* (of the friend) → *na gcarad* (of the friends)
-   **Irregular forms** exist.
    -   *bean* (woman) → *ban* (of women)

### Dative Plural (`an tabharthach iolra`)

Historically, the dative plural was formed by adding the ending `-ibh` to the nominative plural stem. This ending is cognate with the Latin `-ibus`.

| Nominative Plural | Historical Dative Plural | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| palatal (`-ir`) | depalatalised + `-aibh` | *fir* → *le fearaibh* (with men) |
| `-a` | `-aibh` | *bróga* → *i mbrógaibh* (in shoes) |
| `-í` | `-íbh` | *cailíní* → *le cailíníbh* (with girls) |
| `-anna` | `-annaibh` | *busanna* → *i mbusannaibh* (in buses) |
| `-acha` | `-achaibh` | *neadacha* → *i neadachaibh* (in nests) |

Today, this form is mostly archaic. It survives in Munster dialects, especially with indefinite nouns (*le fearaibh*). In the standard language and other dialects, the **nominative plural form is used for the dative plural**.

-   Standard: *leis na fir* (with the men)
-   Standard: *i mbusanna* (in buses)

An exception is the province name `Ulaidh` (Ulster), which uses the dative plural form: *in Ultaibh* (in Ulster).

### Vocative Plural (`an gairmeach iolra`)

The vocative plural is used when addressing a group.

-   **For weak plurals**, the vocative is formed from the **nominative singular + `a`**.
    -   *a fheara!* (Men!)
    -   *a mhaca!* (Sons!)
-   **For strong plurals**, the vocative is identical to the **nominative plural**.
    -   *a bhuachaillí!* (Boys!)

Adjectives and other attributes following a vocative plural noun are **not lenited** and take their nominative plural form.

-   *a fheara Gearmánacha!* (German men!)

An exception is `aingeal` (angel), plural `aingil`, which has the vocative plural `a aingle`.
