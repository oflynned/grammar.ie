In Irish, as in English, a noun (`ainmfhocal`) is a word that represents a person, place, thing, or idea. However, Irish nouns have several grammatical properties that do not exist in English, such as gender and case. Understanding these concepts is essential for constructing grammatically correct sentences.

This page provides a high-level overview of the main features of Irish nouns.

## Gender

Every noun in Irish has a grammatical gender. There are two genders:

-   **Masculine** (`firinscneach`)
-   **Feminine** (`baininscneach`)

The gender of a noun is not always related to its physical properties and often must be memorised. Gender affects other words in a sentence, such as articles, adjectives, and pronouns.

For example, the adjective `mór` (big) changes to `mhór` after a feminine noun:
-   *bád mór* (a big boat) - `bád` is masculine.
-   *bróg mhór* (a big shoe) - `bróg` is feminine.

## Number

Irish nouns have three grammatical numbers:

-   **Singular** (`uatha`): Refers to one of something. This is the basic form of the noun found in dictionaries.
-   **Plural** (`iolra`): Refers to more than one of something. Irish has many different ways to form the plural.
-   **Dual** (`déach`): A special form used only with the number two (`dhá`). For most nouns, the dual form is the same as the singular, but it triggers lenition.

## Case

Case (`tuiseal`) refers to the form a noun takes depending on its grammatical function in a sentence. Modern Irish uses four cases:

-   **Nominative** (`ainmneach`): The basic form of the noun. It is used for the subject of a verb and often for the direct object.
-   **Genitive** (`ginideach`): Primarily used to show possession, equivalent to "'s" or "of" in English. It is also used after certain prepositions and verbal nouns.
-   **Vocative** (`gairmeach`): Used to directly address a person or thing. It is always preceded by the particle `a`, which causes lenition.
-   **Dative** (`tabharthach`): Used after most simple prepositions. In modern Irish, the dative singular form is usually identical to the nominative singular, but a distinct form still exists for some feminine nouns and in the plural.

## Declension

Declension (`díochlaonadh`) is the system of changing a noun's form to reflect its case and number. Nouns are grouped into different declensions—traditionally five—based on the patterns they follow. Each declension has its own set of rules for forming the genitive singular and the nominative plural.
