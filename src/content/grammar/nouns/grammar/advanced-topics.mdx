This page covers several advanced topics in Irish noun grammar, including how nouns relate to each other in apposition, the rules governing definiteness, and the grammatical behaviour of abbreviations.

## Apposition (An Chomhaisnéis)

Apposition is when two nouns or noun phrases are placed side-by-side, with one element serving to identify, explain, or modify the other. The case of the nouns involved depends on the type of apposition.

### First Name and Surname

When a first name is followed by a surname, both names are declined according to the grammatical case.

-   **Nominative**: *Peadar Ó hAnluain*
-   **Genitive**: *teach Pheadair Uí Anluain* (Peadar Ó hAnluain's house)
-   **Vocative**: *A Pheadair Uí Anluain!* (Hello, Peadar Ó hAnluain!)

### Title and Surname

When a title is followed by a surname, both the title and the surname are declined.

-   **Nominative**: *an tAthair Ó hAnluain* (Father Ó hAnluain)
-   **Genitive**: *teach an Athar Uí Anluain* (Father Ó hAnluain's house)
-   **Vocative**: *A Athair Uí Anluain!* (Hello, Father Ó hAnluain!)

### Title and First Name

When a title is followed by a first name (and optionally a surname), only the title is declined. The name remains in the nominative.

-   **Nominative**: *an Bráthair Seán Ó Cléirigh* (Brother Seán O’Cleary)
-   **Genitive**: *teach an Bhráthar Seán Ó Cléirigh* (Brother Seán O’Cleary's house)
-   **Vocative**: *A Athair Peadar!* (Hello, Father Peter!)

An exception is made for the titles `Naomh` and `San` (Saint), where neither the title nor the name is declined.

-   **Genitive**: *Scoil Naomh Pól* (St. Paul's School)
-   **Vocative**: *A Naomh Peadar!* (St. Peter!)

### First Name and a Modifying Noun

When a first name is followed by a noun that describes it (like a profession), only the first name is declined. The modifying noun remains in the nominative.

-   *leabhar Sheáin gabha* (the book of Seán the smith)
-   *teach Phóil, mac an dochtúra* (the house of Paul, the doctor's son)

### The Appositive Genitive

The appositive genitive is a construction where a noun in the genitive case modifies another noun, often creating a more specific compound idea.

#### With Place Names

This is very common with place names, where the specific name is in the genitive.

-   *cathair Chorcaí* (the city of Cork)
-   *Contae na Gaillimhe* (County Galway)
-   *Poblacht na hÉireann* (the Republic of Ireland)

#### With Common Nouns

It also occurs with common nouns to create specific compound terms.

-   *baintreach fir* (a widower, literally "a widow of a man")
-   *múinteoir mná* (a female teacher, literally "a teacher of a woman")
-   *rógaire linbh* (a mischievous child, literally "a rogue of a child")

In modern Irish, outside of established phrases, the second noun often remains in the nominative.

#### With Two Titles

When two titles are used together, the second is in the genitive. The article precedes the first title, which is the only one that can be declined.

-   *an Tiarna Easpaig* (the Lord Bishop, literally "the lord of a bishop")

### Other Forms of Apposition

-   **With a copula relative clause**: A clause starting with `is` can act as an apposition.
    -   *an t-amadán is mac duit* (your son, the fool; literally "the fool who is a son to you")
-   **With the preposition `de`**: Used to mean "of a".
    -   *amadán de mhac* (a fool of a son)
    -   *gliogar de chathaoir* (a rickety chair)
    -   *Bhí scian agam, de scian bheag ghéar.* (I had a knife, a small sharp knife.)
-   **With the preposition `mar`**: Used to mean "as" or "that", often referring back to something just mentioned.
    -   *Is mór an scrios é, mar thobac.* (It is a great ruin, that tobacco.)
    -   *Bhí sé glic, mar shionnach.* (He was cunning, that fox.)

## Definiteness (Cinnteacht)

A noun in Irish is considered **definite** (referring to a specific, known entity) in several contexts, not just when it uses the definite article. Understanding definiteness is key to correct sentence structure.

### When is a Noun Definite?

A noun is definite if:

-   It is preceded by the **definite article** (`an`, `na`).
    -   *an fear* (the man)
-   It is preceded by a **possessive pronoun** (`mo`, `do`, `a`, etc.).
    -   *mo leabhar* (my book)
-   It is preceded by `gach` (every).
    -   *gach fear* (every man)
-   It is **numbered** or assigned a letter.
    -   *fear a haon* (man No. 1)
    -   *ceist a dó* (question No. 2)
    -   *Aguisín B* (Appendix B)
-   It is a **proper noun**.
    -   *Seán*, *Éire*
    -   Note: A name is treated as indefinite when it is being identified as a name: *Is Albain ainm na tíre seo.* (Scotland is the name of this country.)
-   It refers to a **unique concept**, often used without an article.
    -   `neamh` (heaven), `ifreann` (hell), `Dia` (God)
-   It is modified by another **definite noun in the genitive case**.
    -   *teach an dochtúra* (the doctor's house)
    -   *teach Sheáin* (Seán's house)
-   It is in the **vocative case**.
    -   *a fhir!* (O man!)

### Consequences of Definiteness

#### In Copula Sentences

Definite nouns require a pronoun (`é`, `í`, `iad`) in certain copula sentences.

-   **Identification**: A definite predicate requires a pronoun.
    -   *Is é an bus seo an ceann is fearr.* (This bus is the best one.)
    -   *Is é ár mbus an ceann is fearr.* (Our bus is the best one.)
-   **Classification**: A definite subject requires a pronoun.
    -   *Is dochtúir é Seán.* (Seán is a doctor.)
    -   *Is dochtúir é an fear seo.* (This man is a doctor.)

#### With Genitive Attributes

A noun modified by a definite genitive attribute is itself definite and **cannot** take another definite article.

-   *teach an dochtúra* (the doctor's house)
-   *bás m’athar* (my father's death)
-   *tús chaibidil a trí* (the beginning of chapter 3)

You cannot say `*an teach an dochtúra`.

#### Avoiding Double Definiteness

Irish avoids "doubling up" on definiteness. If a noun is already definite (e.g., from a possessive pronoun), you cannot add another definite marker (like the article needed for a superlative). A different construction is required, often using a prepositional phrase.

-   *an mac is sine agam* (my eldest son; literally "the eldest son at me")
-   *an chéad charr aige* (his first car; literally "the first car at him")

Similarly, to avoid a definite genitive modifying an already definite noun, the partitive dative is used.

-   *mo chuid den airgead* (my share of the money) not `*mo chuid an airgid`

### Expressing Indefiniteness

To express indefiniteness (e.g., "a friend of mine," "a poem by Goethe"), you must avoid constructions that create definiteness. A definite genitive like `dán Goethe` means "Goethe's poem" (a specific one). To make it indefinite, a prepositional phrase is used.

-   *dán le Goethe* or *dán de chuid Goethe* (a poem by Goethe)
-   *teach don dochtúir* (a doctor's house) vs. *teach an dochtúra* (the doctor's house)

The same principle applies to possessives. Instead of a possessive pronoun, a prepositional pronoun is used.

-   *cara dom* (a friend of mine)
-   *cos léi* (a leg of hers)
-   *cara dá chuid* (a friend of his / one of his friends)

### Definiteness of Clauses and Infinitives

When a verbal noun phrase or a subordinate clause functions as a noun in a sentence, it is considered definite. It is referred to with the pronoun `é`.

-   *Is deas an rud é bualadh le do chairde.* (Meeting your friends is a nice thing.)

This does not apply in copula modal constructions, which do not take a secondary subject.

-   *Is maith liom bualadh le mo chairde.* (I like meeting my friends.)

## Abbreviations and Acronyms (Giorrúcháin agus Acrainmneacha)

Abbreviations are formed by combining the initial letters of words.

-   `SAM` for *Stáit Aontaithe Mheiriceá* (USA)
-   `RTÉ` for *Raidió Teilifís Éireann*
-   `BÁC` for *Baile Átha Cliath* (Dublin)

They follow special grammatical rules, though usage can vary.

### Initial Mutations

In standard Irish, abbreviations and acronyms are not lenited or eclipsed and do not take prefixes like `t-`, `h-`, or `n-`.

-   The preposition `i` becomes `in`: *in RTÉ* (on RTÉ)
-   The prepositions `de` and `do` are not shortened to `d'`: *de OÉ* (from the University of Ireland)

However, common usage sometimes deviates, especially with familiar acronyms: *i mBÁC* (in Dublin) is more common than *in BÁC*.

### Use of the Article

-   **Inherently Definite**: Acronyms for unique entities are definite by nature and usually do not require an article. This is mandatory if the full form does not start with an article (`SAM`) or if the article is internal (`OÉ` for *Ollscoil na hÉireann*).
    -   *OÉ*, never `*an OÉ`
    -   *SAM*, never `*na SAM`
-   **Optional Article**: If the full form begins with an article, the acronym can be used with or without it.
    -   *(an) AE* for *an tAontas Eorpach* (the EU)
    -   *(na) NA* for *na Naisiúin Aontaithe* (the UN)
-   **Indefinite**: For abbreviations of common nouns, the article is needed to make them definite.
    -   `DD` (*dlúthdhiosca*) = a CD
    -   `an DD` = the CD

### Number and Gender

-   **Gender**: Abbreviations are generally treated as **masculine singular**, regardless of the gender or number of the original words. They are referred to with the pronoun `é`.
    -   *Is é SAM ...* (The USA is...)
-   **Plural**: Indefinite abbreviations can be made plural by adding the suffix `-anna` (or `-nna` after a vowel). They are then referred to with `iad`.
    -   `DDanna` (CDs)
    -   *na DDanna* (the CDs)
