While most Irish nouns follow one of the five standard declensions, a small group of very common nouns have unique, irregular forms that must be learned individually. A noun is typically classified by its genitive singular form, but these nouns resist easy classification. This page covers the most frequent irregular nouns you will encounter.

## Core Irregular Nouns

### Bean (woman)

This is one of the most common and most irregular nouns in Irish.

| Case | Singular | Plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Nominative** | `an bhean` | `na mná` |
| **Genitive** | `na mná` | `na mban` |
| **Dative** | `an mhnaoi` | `na mná` |

- *Tá bean sa ghairdín.* - There is a woman in the garden.
- *Hata na mná.* - The woman's hat.
- *Thug sé an leabhar don mhnaoi.* - He gave the book to the woman.

<Details summary="Historical note">
The old dative plural form was `mnáibh`, which is now archaic.
</Details>

### Deirfiúr (sister)

The word for a female sibling is highly irregular.

| Case | Singular | Plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Nominative** | `an deirfiúr` | `na deirfiúracha` |
| **Genitive** | `na deirféar` | `na ndeirfiúracha` |
| **Dative** | `an deirfír` | `na deirfiúracha` |

- *Sin í mo dheirfiúr, Síle.* - That is my sister, Síle.
- *Ainm mo dheirféar ná Aoife.* - My sister's name is Aoife.

<Dialect region="Connacht">
In Cois Fharraige, the pronunciation is often `[dr′aur]`, and the genitive form can be `drifíre`.
</Dialect>

<Details summary="Etymology">
The word `deirfiúr` was formed with the prefix `deirbh-` ("true" or "of the same blood") to distinguish a biological sister from a `siúr` (a religious sister or nun). The `f` comes from the lenition of `s` in the Indo-European root *swesor*, which is also the root of the English word "sister".
</Details>

### Siúr (sister, nun)

This word refers to a religious sister or nun.

| Case | Singular | Plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Nominative** | `an tsiúr` | `na siúracha` |
| **Genitive** | `na siúrach` | `na siúracha` |

- *Is siúr í i gclochar i mBaile Átha Cliath.* - She is a nun in a convent in Dublin.

<Note>
This noun largely follows the 5th declension pattern, but the broad `-r` in the nominative singular is unusual for that class.
</Note>

### Dia (God)

The word for God has a distinct genitive form.

| Case | Singular | Plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Nominative** | `Dia` / `an dia` | `na déithe` |
| **Genitive** | `Dé` / `an dé` | `na ndéithe` |

- *Grásta Dé.* - The grace of God.
- *Scéalta faoi na déithe.* - Stories about the gods.

### Lá (day)

This common noun has a distinct dative form, `ló`, which is often seen in set phrases.

| Case | Singular | Plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Nominative** | `an lá` | `na laethanta` |
| **Genitive** | `an lae` | `na laethanta` |
| **Dative** | `an ló` | `na laethanta` |

- *Lá breá gréine a bhí ann.* - It was a fine sunny day.
- *Ag deireadh an lae.* - At the end of the day.
- *Oibríonn sé i rith an lae agus codlaíonn sé i rith na hoíche.* - He works during the day and sleeps during the night.

<Note>
The dative form `ló` is less common in modern speech than `lá`, but it survives in important phrases like *de ló is d'oíche* (by day and by night).
</Note>

### Leaba (bed)

| Case | Singular | Plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Nominative** | `an leaba` | `na leapacha` |
| **Genitive** | `na leapa` | `na leapacha` |

- *Tá an leabhar ar an leaba.* - The book is on the bed.
- *Cos na leapa.* - The leg of the bed.

<Details summary="Historical forms">
The genitive `leapa` likely arose from an older form `leabtha`. Other historical variants for the genitive include `leapan`, `leaban`, and `leabadh`.
</Details>

### Mí (month)

| Case | Singular | Plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Nominative** | `an mhí` | `na míonna` |
| **Genitive** | `na míosa` | `na míonna` |

- *Tá dhá mhí dhéag sa bhliain.* - There are twelve months in the year.
- *Ag tús na míosa seo chugainn.* - At the start of next month.

### Olann (wool)

| Case | Singular | Plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Nominative** | `an olann` | `na holanna` |
| **Genitive** | `na holla` | `na n-olann` |

- *Tá an geansaí seo déanta as olann.* - This jumper is made of wool.
- *Praghas na holla.* - The price of wool.

<Details summary="Historical note">
Although it appears irregular, `olann` can be seen as a regular 3rd declension noun. The genitive `olla` comes from syncope (loss of a vowel) in the older form `olna`, where the `ln` sound combination evolved into `ll`.
</Details>

### Talamh (land, ground)

| Case | Singular | Plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Nominative** | `an talamh` | `na tailte` |
| **Genitive** | `na talún` | `na dtailte` |

- *Cheannaigh siad píosa talún.* - They bought a piece of land.
- *Tá na tailte sin an-torthúil.* - Those lands are very fertile.

<Details summary="Historical forms">
The genitive `talún` is consistent with the 5th declension; the older spelling was `talmhan`. There is also a regular 1st declension masculine variant: `talamh` (nom. sg.), `talaimh` (gen. sg.).
</Details>

### Ó / Ua (grandson, descendant in names)

This element, common in surnames, has its own declension.

| Case | Form | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Nominative** | `Ó` / `Ua` | *Pádraig Ó Súilleabháin* |
| **Genitive** | `Uí` | *Bean Uí Shúilleabháin* (Mrs. O'Sullivan) |
| **Vocative** | `Uí` | *A Uí Shúilleabháin!* (Mr. O'Sullivan!) |

<Note>
These forms are used for the surname particle. The standalone noun `ó` meaning "grandson" is a regular 4th declension noun. The dative plural `Uibh` survives in some place names, such as `Uíbh Ráthach` (Iveragh).
</Note>

### Micheál (Michael)

Like many proper names, `Micheál` has an irregular genitive.

| Case | Form |
| --- | --- |
| **Nominative** | `Micheál` |
| **Genitive** | `Michíl` |

- *Sin é carr Mhichíl.* - That is Michael's car.

## Nouns Sometimes Classed as Irregular

Some nouns have highly distinct forms but can technically be assigned to a standard declension, usually the 2nd.

### Teach (house)

| Case | Singular | Plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Nominative** | `an teach` | `na tithe` |
| **Genitive** | `an tí` | `na dtithe` |
| **Dative** | `an tigh` | `na tithe` |

- *Tá an teach mór.* - The house is big.
- *Doras an tí.* - The door of the house.
- *Tá sé sa tigh.* - He is in the house.

### Deoch (drink)

| Case | Singular | Plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Nominative** | `an deoch` | `na deochanna` |
| **Genitive** | `na dí` | `na ndeochanna` |
| **Dative** | `an digh` | `na deochanna` |

- *Ba mhaith liom deoch uisce.* - I would like a drink of water.
- *Blas na dí.* - The taste of the drink.

## Nouns with Irregular or Variant Forms

### Bia (food)

In the standard language, `bia` is a regular 4th declension noun, with `bia` for both nominative and genitive singular. However, older forms are still common in some dialects.

- *An bhfuil an bia réidh?* - Is the food ready?

<Dialect region="Munster">
In Munster, the older forms `biadh` (nominative) and `bídh` (genitive) are still frequently used. The plural can be `na biadha` or `na bianna`.
</Dialect>

### Bó (cow)

This noun is now mostly regular (4th declension), with the main exception being its very short plural form.

| Case | Singular | Plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Nominative** | `an bhó` | `na ba` |
| **Genitive** | `na bó` | `na mbó` |

- *Tá an bhó sa pháirc.* - The cow is in the field.
- *Tá na ba ag ithe féir.* - The cows are eating grass.

<Details summary="Historical forms">
An older dative singular form, `boin`, placed this noun closer to the 5th declension. The old dative plural was `buaibh`.
</Details>
