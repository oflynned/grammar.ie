The third declension includes both masculine and feminine nouns that end in a consonant, which can be either broad or slender. This declension is characterised by a genitive singular ending in `-a` and a variety of strong plural endings.

## Genitive Singular

The genitive singular is typically formed by adding `-a`. The process differs slightly depending on the final consonant of the noun.

### Nouns ending in a broad consonant

For nouns ending in a broad consonant, simply add the ending `-a`.

- `beannacht` (a blessing) → `beannachta`
- `áth` (a ford) → `átha`

### Nouns ending in a slender consonant

For nouns ending in a slender consonant, the final consonant is first made broad (a process called depalatalisation), and then `-a` is added.

- `feoil` (meat) → `feola`
- `cuid` (a part, a share) → `coda`
- `fuil` (blood) → `fola`
- `mil` (honey) → `meala`

### Nouns ending in -int

For nouns with the ending `-int`, the final `-t` is dropped before adding the genitive ending `-a`.

- `canúint` (a dialect) → `canúna`

### Nouns ending in -irt

For nouns ending in `-irt`, the `-t` changes to `-th-` before the `-a` is added.

- `imirt` (playing, a game) → `imeartha`

## Plural Forms

While older forms exist, modern Irish almost exclusively uses strong plural endings for the third declension.

<Details summary="Historical note on weak plurals">
  Older grammar books often describe a weak plural ending in `-a` as the main form for this declension. This is now rare. In these cases, the genitive plural is formed by dropping the `-a` from the nominative plural.
  - *béas* (a habit) → plural *béasa*, genitive plural *béas*
  - *dreas* (a turn, a spell) → plural *dreasa*, genitive plural *dreas*
  Nouns with a vowel change in the genitive singular, like `fuil` (blood) becoming `fola`, also sometimes use this `-a` plural.
</Details>

In modern Irish, the following strong plural patterns are common.

### Nouns for people and agents

Nouns ending in `-aeir`, `-éir`, `-eoir`, `-óir`, or `-úir` (often denoting people or agents) form their plural by adding `-í`.

- `Gaeilgeoir` (an Irish speaker) → `Gaeilgeoirí`
- `dochtúir` (a doctor) → `dochtúirí`

### Abstract nouns

Nouns ending in `-acht` or `-íocht` are typically feminine and form their plural by adding `-aí`.

- `Gaeltacht` (an Irish-speaking area) → `Gaeltachtaí`
- `mallacht` (a curse) → `mallachtaí`

<Note>
  An exception is `comhlacht` (a company), which is masculine but follows this plural pattern: `comhlachtaí`.
</Note>

### Monosyllabic masculine nouns

Many masculine, single-syllable nouns take the ending `-anna` or `-aí`.

- `loch` (a lake) → `lochanna`
- `rud` (a thing) → `rudaí`

<Note>
  An exception is `mionn` (an oath), which has a weak plural `mionna` and a genitive plural `mionn`.
</Note>

### Nouns ending in -l, -n, or -r

Nouns ending in `-l`, `-n`, or `-r` have several possible plural endings.

- The ending `-acha` is common, often after making the final consonant broad: `barúil` (an opinion) → `barúlacha`.
- The ending `-ta` is also used, especially for nouns ending in `-l` or `-n`: `bliain` (a year) → `blianta`.
- The ending `-te` is a less common alternative: `móin` (peat, turf) → `móinte`.
