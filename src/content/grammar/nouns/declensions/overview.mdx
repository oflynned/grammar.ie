In Irish, nouns change their form depending on their grammatical function in a sentence. This system of changing endings is called declension. Nouns are grouped into five main declensions based on how they form their genitive singular, which is the case used to show possession, similar to "of the..." in English.

The primary feature that defines a noun's declension is the form of its genitive singular. The dative singular, though less common in modern speech, also often follows a predictable pattern for each declension. Plural forms, however, are much more varied and are not used to classify nouns.

A small number of common nouns have highly irregular forms and are grouped separately. Additionally, verbal nouns ending in `-adh` or `-ú` follow a regular pattern where their genitive form is the same as the verbal adjective.

<Dialect>
In some dialects, certain nouns may shift from one declension to another. For example:
- `guala` ("shoulder"): Usually 5th declension (genitive `gualann`) but sometimes treated as 2nd declension (genitive `gualainne`).
- `athair` ("father"): Usually 5th declension (genitive `athar`) but sometimes treated as 3rd declension (genitive `athara`).
- `teanga` ("tongue"): Usually 4th declension (genitive `teanga`) but sometimes treated as 5th declension (genitive `teangan`).
</Dialect>

## Overview of Declensions

The following sections summarise the main patterns for each declension.

### Main Declensions

The first four declensions have the most regular genitive singular patterns.

| Declension | Typical Gender | Genitive Singular Pattern | Dative Singular Pattern | Example (Nom. - Gen. - Dat.) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1st | Masculine | The final consonant becomes slender (palatalisation). | Same as the nominative. | `fear` - `fir` - `fear` |
| 2nd | Feminine | The final consonant becomes slender and adds `-e`. | The final consonant becomes slender. | `long` - `loinge` - `loing` |
| 3rd | m. or f. | Adds `-(e)a`. The final consonant may become broad. | Same as the nominative. | `leann` - `leanna` - `leann` |
| 4th | Masculine | No change from the nominative. | Same as the nominative. | `cailín` - `cailín` - `cailín` |

### Fifth Declension Groups

The fifth declension contains several smaller patterns, many of them historical stem types.

| Group | Typical Gender | Genitive Singular Pattern | Dative Singular Pattern | Example (Nom. - Gen. - Dat.) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Group 1 | m. or f. | The final consonant becomes broad (depalatalisation). | Same as the nominative. | `athair` - `athar` - `athair` |
| Group 2 | Feminine | Adds `-(e)ach`. | Adds `-(a)igh`. | `beoir` - `beorach` - `beoraigh` |
| Group 3 | Feminine | Adds a broad `-n` or `-nn`. | Adds a slender `-n` or `-nn`. | `Éire` - `Éireann` - `Éirinn` |
| Group 4 | Masculine | Adds a broad `-d`. | Adds a slender `-d`. | `cara` - `carad` - `caraid` |

### Special Patterns

Some nouns do not fit neatly into the five main declension patterns.

| Pattern | Typical Gender | Genitive Singular Pattern | Dative Singular Pattern | Example (Nom. - Gen. - Dat.) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Irregular nouns | m. or f. | Follows no standard pattern. | Follows no standard pattern. | `bean` - `mná` - `mnaoi` |
| Verbal nouns ending in `-adh` or `-ú` | Masculine | Uses the form of the verbal adjective. | Same as the nominative. | `bualadh` - `buailte` - `bualadh` |

<Details summary="Historical note on Latin parallels">
The classification of Irish declensions shows some parallels with Latin.
- **1st Declension:** Compare Latin *vir* (gen. *viri*) with Irish `fear` (gen. `fir`). The Latin genitive ending *-i* of the o-declension corresponds to the palatalisation of the final consonant in Irish.
- **2nd Declension:** Compare Latin *longa* (gen. *longae*) with Irish `long` (gen. `loinge`). The Latin genitive ending *-ae* of the a-declension corresponds to the Irish ending `-e` along with palatalisation.
</Details>
