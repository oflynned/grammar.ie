The 5th declension is a group of mostly feminine nouns in Irish. It is considered an "irregular" declension because its nouns follow several different patterns, often involving a change from a slender to a broad consonant in the genitive case. This group includes common words like kinship terms and place names.

Nouns in this declension typically have one of the following characteristics:
- They are mostly **feminine**. Masculine nouns in this group often have a `-d` in the genitive singular.
- They end in a **slender consonant**, such as `-il`, `-in`, or `-ir`.
- They end in a **vowel** or in `-inn` or `-in`.
- They are **terms of kinship** ending in `-ir`, like `athair` (father), `máthair` (mother), and `bráthair` (brother, friar).

## Genitive Singular

The genitive singular of 5th declension nouns always ends in a **broad consonant**. There are several ways this is formed, based on the noun's historical stem.

### Depalatalisation

The most straightforward pattern is the depalatalisation of the final consonant, where a slender consonant becomes broad.

- *athair* -> *athar* (father -> of a father)
- *Albain* -> *Alban* (Scotland -> of Scotland)
- *abhainn* -> *abhann* (river -> of a river)
- *Nollaig* -> *Nollag* (Christmas -> of Christmas)

### Adding -ach or -each

Many nouns ending in `-il`, `-in`, or `-ir` form the genitive by depalatalising the final consonant and adding the ending `-ach`.

- *beoir* -> *beorach* (beer -> of beer)
- *cathair* -> *cathrach* (city -> of a city)

Polysyllabic nouns with a slender consonant in the second syllable often add `-each` instead, sometimes without depalatalisation. This frequently causes syncope (the loss of a vowel in the middle of the word).

- *litir* -> *litreach* (letter -> of a letter)
- *uimhir* -> *uimhreach* (number -> of a number)

### Adding -n, -nn, or -d

Nouns that end in a vowel typically add `-n`, `-nn`, or `-d` in the genitive.

- *cara* -> *carad* (friend -> of a friend)
- *pearsa* -> *pearsan* (person -> of a person)
- *Éire* -> *Éireann* (Ireland -> of Ireland)
- *caoga* -> *caogad* (fifty -> of fifty)

<Details summary="Historical note on n-stems">
These patterns derive from older "consonantal stems" in Old Irish. The `-n`, `-nn`, and `-d` endings come from historical n-stems and nt-stems, which did not show these consonants in the nominative case, similar to Latin (e.g., `natio` (nom.) vs. `nationis` (gen.)). The double `-nn` ending typically appears after `l`, `n`, or `r` (e.g., `Éireann`, `Manann`), while the single `-n` appears after other consonants (e.g., `Alban`, `comharsan`), though exceptions exist.
</Details>

<Dialect region="Ulster">
In Ulster Irish, some nouns that end in a vowel in the standard language retain older forms. They may have the dative ending `-idh` in the nominative and the ending `-adh` in the genitive. For example, standard `léine` (shirt) might be `léinidh` (nom.) and `léineadh` (gen.). In other dialects, these nouns have merged with the 4th declension (e.g., `léine` - `léine`).
</Dialect>

## Dative Singular

Historically, all dative forms in this declension ended in a **slender consonant**. In modern Irish, the dative singular is often replaced by the nominative singular form, but some dative forms remain in common use, especially after prepositions.

### Historical Dative Forms

Some common 5th declension nouns are actually historical dative forms that have replaced the original nominative. For these words, the nominative and dative are identical.

- `Éirinn` (from `Éire`) is common in Connacht and Ulster.
- `Albain` (from `Alba`, which is still used in Scottish Gaelic).
- `abhainn` (from `abha`).

### Palatalising the Genitive

Nouns with a genitive in `-n`, `-nn`, or `-d` form the dative by palatalising this ending (making it slender).

- Genitive `fichead` (of twenty) -> Dative `fichid`
- Genitive `carad` (of a friend) -> Dative `caraid`
- Genitive `Éireann` (of Ireland) -> Dative `Éirinn`

The dative form `Éirinn` is required after prepositions.
- *in Éirinn* - in Ireland (Correct)
- *in Éire* - in Ireland (Incorrect)

### Palatalising -(e)ach

Nouns with a genitive in `-(e)ach` historically formed the dative by changing the ending to `-(a)igh`. This form is less common today and is often replaced by the nominative.

- Genitive `caorach` (of a sheep) -> Dative `caoirigh`
- Genitive `beorach` (of beer) -> Dative `beoraigh`

## Nominative Plural

### Genitive Form + -a

A common way to form the nominative plural is to take the genitive singular form and add `-a`. The plural endings `-(e)acha` and `-(e)anna`, now common in other declensions, originate from this pattern.

- *cathair* (city) -> *cathrach* (gen. sg.) -> *cathracha* (nom. pl.)
- *comharsan* (neighbour) -> *comharsana* (nom. pl.)

### Exceptions

Some nouns have irregular plural forms.

- *athair* (father) -> *aithreacha*
- *abhainn* (river) -> *aibhneacha*

## Genitive Plural

The genitive plural can be formed in two main ways:
- It is most often identical to the **nominative plural**.
- For some nouns, especially those with a genitive singular in `-n` or `-d`, it is identical to the **genitive singular**.

## Declension Patterns

This table summarises the main patterns. The dative is omitted as it is mostly replaced by the nominative in modern usage.

| Nom. Sing. | Gender | Gen. Sing. | Nom. Plur. | Gen. Plur. |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **slender** consonant | f | **broad** consonant | various | various |
| **slender** consonant (-l, n, r) | f | **broad** consonant + `-ach` | **broad** consonant + `-acha` | **broad** consonant + `-acha` |
| **slender** consonant (-l, n, r) | f | **slender** consonant + `-each` | **slender** consonant + `-eacha` | **slender** consonant + `-eacha` |
| **Vowel** | f | **Vowel** + `-n`, `-nn` | **Vowel** + `-na`, `-nna` | **Vowel** + `-n(a)`, `-nna` |
| **Vowel** | m | **Vowel** + `-d` | various | various |
