Lenition, known in Irish as *An Séimhiú*, is a fundamental sound change that softens the initial consonant of a word. It is a core feature of Irish grammar that affects nouns, verbs, and adjectives in specific contexts. In writing, lenition is shown by adding an 'h' after the initial consonant.

For example, the word for "boat" is `bád`. When lenited, it becomes `bhád`.

*   *bád* - a boat
*   *mo bhád* - my boat

You will encounter lenition constantly, so understanding its rules is essential for speaking and reading Irish correctly.

<Details summary="Historical background">
  The underlying principle of lenition is spirantisation, where a plosive (a stop consonant like `p`, `t`, `k`) is replaced by a fricative (a sound made with friction, like `f`, `th`, `ch`) formed at the same place in the mouth.

  Historically, the lenition of `d` and `t` produced dental fricatives (the "th" sounds in English *that* and *thing*). Over time, these sounds evolved into the sounds we hear today for `dh` and `th`.
</Details>

## Spelling and Pronunciation

Lenition is indicated by writing an `h` after the affected consonant. In older texts, you may see a dot (a *ponc séimhithe* or *buailte*) written over the consonant instead.

The following table shows which consonants can be lenited and how their pronunciation changes.

| Consonant | Lenited | Broad Pronunciation | Slender Pronunciation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `b` | `bh` | [w], [v] | [v′] |
| `c` | `ch` | [x] | [x′] |
| `d` | `dh` | [ɣ] | [j] |
| `f` | `fh` | (silent) | (silent) |
| `g` | `gh` | [ɣ] | [j] |
| `m` | `mh` | [w], [v] | [v′] |
| `p` | `ph` | [f] | [f′] |
| `s` | `sh` | [h] | [h], [x′] |
| `t` | `th` | [h] | [h], [x′] |

The consonants `l`, `n`, `r`, `h`, `j`, `v`, `w`, and `z` are not lenited in standard written Irish.

### Pronunciation Notes

*   **`gh` / `dh`**: Broad `gh` and `dh` are pronounced as a voiced velar fricative [ɣ], a sound not found in English but similar to a soft 'g' in Spanish *agua*. Slender `gh` and `dh` are pronounced like the 'y' in "yes" [j].
*   **`bh` / `mh`**: These are generally pronounced as `v`. Broad `bh` and `mh` at the beginning of a word are often pronounced as `w`, while the `v` sound is more common in Munster. `mh` can also cause the surrounding vowels to become nasalised.
*   **`ch`**: Broad `ch` is like the 'ch' in Scottish *loch* [x]. Slender `ch` is like the 'h' in "hue" or the 'ch' in German *ich* [x′].
*   **`sh` / `th`**: Both are usually pronounced as `h`. However, when a slender `sh` or `th` comes before a long `a`, `o`, or `u`, it is often pronounced [x′] (like slender `ch`).
    *   *a Sheáin* - [ə x′aːnʲ] - O Seán!
    *   *mo sheol* - [mə x′oːl] - my sail
*   **`fh`**: The combination `fh` is completely silent. A word beginning with `fh` is treated as if it begins with a vowel.

<Details summary="Historical pronunciation of 'dh' and 'th'">
  Originally, `dh` was pronounced like the voiced 'th' [ð] in *that*, and `th` was pronounced like the voiceless 'th' [θ] in *thing*. These sounds are no longer used in modern Irish, having merged with the sounds of `gh` and `h` respectively.
</Details>

## When Lenition is Blocked

In certain situations, lenition is blocked even when the grammatical rule calls for it. This often happens when a word ends in a consonant that is formed in the same part of the mouth as the initial consonant of the following word. This phenomenon is sometimes known by the older term *an cadad*.

The rule is applied most strictly for the coronal consonants, often called the "dentals rule".

### Coronal Consonants: d, n, t, l, s

The consonants `d`, `t`, and `s` are not lenited if they follow a word ending in `d`, `n`, `t`, `l`, or `s`. This rule is very common because of the article `an`.

*   *don doras* - to the door (not `*don dhoras`)
*   *an-te* - very hot (not `*an-the`)

<Note>
In standard grammar, the dentals rule does not apply to attributive adjectives or to definite nouns in the genitive case.
- *bean dheas* - a nice woman
</Note>

<Dialect region="Connacht">
In Connacht and in the Official Standard, the dentals rule also prevents eclipsis after a preposition and article. Words beginning with `d` or `t` are not eclipsed after the `-n` of the article.
</Dialect>

### Labial Consonants: b, m, p

Lenition of `b`, `m`, and `p` is often blocked after words ending in `-m`.

*   *um bosca* - around a box

<Dialect region="Munster">
In Munster, `b`, `m`, and `p` are not lenited after possessive forms like `im` (in my), `dom` (to my), and `lem` (with my).
- *im póca* - in my pocket (Standard: *i mo phóca*)

In these cases, a lenited `m` is often pronounced as `b`.
- *dom muintir* - [dəm bin′t′ər′] - to my people (Standard: *do mo mhuintir*)
- *agam mac* - [əgum bak] - with my son (Standard: *ag mo mhac*)
</Dialect>

### Velar Consonants: c, g

Lenition of `c` and `g` is blocked in surnames after `Mhic` and `Nic`, which would normally cause lenition.

*   *Bríd Nic Cárthaigh* - Bridget MacCarthy
*   *teach Sheáin Mhic Gabhann* - Seán MacGowan's house

## Special Cases and Exceptions

*   **`s-` to `ts-`**: After the article `an`, `s-` is not lenited. Instead, it changes to `ts-` if followed by a vowel, `l`, `n`, or `r`. This is a separate mutation known as the t-prefix.
*   **Consonant Clusters**: Initial clusters like `sc-`, `sp-`, `st-`, `sm-`, `sf-`, `spl-`, `spr-`, `scl-`, `scr-`, and `str-` are never lenited.
*   **Silent `fh-`**: Because `fh` is silent, a word beginning with `fh-` followed by a vowel is treated as a vowel-initial word.
    *   *m’fhear céile* - my husband (like *m'athair*)
    *   *D’fhoghlaim mé Gaeilge.* - I learned Irish. (The past tense particle `do` becomes `d'`)
    *   *D’fhreagair sé an cheist.* - He answered the question.
*   **`gan` + `f`**: The preposition `gan` (without) never lenites a following `f`.
    *   *gan fear* - without a man
    *   *gan fadhb* - without a problem

<Warning>
Lenition is sometimes omitted if it would make a word unrecognisable or cause it to be confused with another word. For example, `fuisce` (whiskey) is never lenited because `fhuisce` would be silent and sound identical to `uisce` (water).
- *droch-fuisce* - bad whiskey
- *droch-uisce* - bad water
</Warning>

<Details summary="Phonetic lenition of l, n, and r">
Although `l`, `n`, and `r` are not lenited in writing, their pronunciation can change in spoken Irish depending on the dialect. The unlenited sounds are "tense" or "strong" ([ʟ], [ɴ], [r]), while the lenited versions are "lax" or "weak" ([l], [n], [ʀ]). This distinction is audible but not reflected in standard spelling.
- *an leabhar* (the book) vs. *mo leabhar* (my book)
</Details>

## When to Use Lenition

Lenition is triggered by a wide range of grammatical particles, words, and contexts.

### Nouns

*   After the vocative particle `a`.
    *   *a Cháit!* - Kate!
    *   *a Sheáin!* - John!
*   After the article `an` with feminine singular nouns in the nominative case (except `d`, `t`, `s`).
    *   *an bhean* - the woman
    *   *an chathaoir* - the chair
*   After the article `an` with masculine singular nouns in the genitive case (except `d`, `t`, `s`).
    *   *hata an fhir* - the man's hat
*   After the possessive pronouns `mo` (my), `do` (your, sg.), and `a` (his).
    *   *mo theach* - my house
    *   *do chara* - your friend
    *   *a mháthair* - his mother
*   After many prepositions when not followed by the article, including `ar`, `de`, `do`, `faoi`, `ó`, `mar`, `roimh`, `trí`, `thar`.
    *   *ar bhord* - on a table
    *   *ó Chorcaigh* - from Cork
*   After the combined preposition-article forms `don` (to the) and `den` (from the).
    *   *don fhear* - to the man
*   After the number `aon` (one) and the personal number `beirt` (two people).
    *   *aon bhád amháin* - one boat
    *   *beirt pháistí* - two children
*   In the second part of many compound words.
    *   *seanbhean* - old woman (`sean` + `bean`)
    *   *scian phóca* - pocket knife (`scian` + `póca`)

### Verbs

*   In the past tense (preterite), imperfect tense, and conditional mood. This is historically due to a particle `do`, which is now often omitted.
    *   *Bhí sé anseo.* - He was here.
    *   *Cheannaigh mé é.* - I bought it.
    *   *D’ól sí an tae.* - She drank the tea.
*   After the negative particle `ní` (is not, does not).
    *   *Ní thuigim.* - I don't understand.
*   After past tense particles like `ar`, `gur`, `nár`, `níor`.
    *   *Ar chuala tú?* - Did you hear?
    *   *Dúirt sé nár tháinig sé.* - He said that he didn't come.
*   After the direct relative particle `a`.
    *   *an fear a cheannaigh an teach* - the man who bought the house
*   After `má` (if).
    *   *má thagann tú* - if you come
*   Verbal nouns after the preposition `a` (to).
    *   *ag iarraidh tú a phósadh* - trying to marry you

### Adjectives

*   When modifying a feminine singular noun.
    *   *bean mhaith* - a good woman
*   When modifying a masculine singular noun in the genitive case.
    *   *praghas an chóta mhóir* - the price of the big coat
*   When modifying a plural noun that ends in a slender consonant.
    *   *fir mhóra* - big men

<Dialect region="Ulster">
In Ulster, an adjective is lenited after a noun in the dative case (following a preposition + article), for both masculine and feminine nouns.
- *leis an fhear mhór* - with the big man
- *leis an bhean mhór* - with the big woman
</Dialect>

<Dialect region="Munster Connacht">
In Munster and Connacht, where the noun is often eclipsed in the dative, the adjective is only lenited after feminine nouns.
- *leis an bhfear mór* - with the big man
- *leis an mbean mhór* - with the big woman
</Dialect>

### Other Cases

*   After prefixes like `an-` (very), `fíor-` (true/very), and `sean-` (old).
    *   *an-mhaith* - very good
    *   *fíordheacair* - very difficult
*   After the past/conditional form of the copula, `ba`.
    *   *Ba mhaith liom tae.* - I would like tea.
*   In certain fixed words.
    *   `dhá` (two), `bhur` (your, pl.), `cheana` (already), `choíche` (never), `chomh` (as), `chun` (to).

<Details summary="A note on the term 'aspiration'">
  Lenition is sometimes incorrectly called "aspiration". Aspiration is a much weaker phonetic change where a puff of air follows a consonant, as with the 'p' in the English word "pin". In Irish lenition, the consonant itself fundamentally changes (e.g., `p` becomes `f`). Therefore, "lenition" (from Latin for "to weaken" or "to soften") is the more accurate term.
</Details>
