Initial mutations are changes to the beginning of a word that serve a grammatical purpose. While this might seem unusual if you are new to Celtic languages, it is a core feature of Irish and its relatives. The unchanged, default form of a word, with no mutation, is known as the radical form, or `an lomadh` ("the bareness").

This page explains the historical linguistic processes that gave rise to the modern system of mutations.

## The Principle of Sandhi

In spoken Irish, words within a phrase are typically pronounced together without pauses, creating a continuous flow of sound. This process, where the sounds at the end of one word influence the sounds at the beginning of the next, is known in linguistics as **sandhi**.

A similar process occurs in French with "liaison", where a normally silent consonant at the end of a word is pronounced when the next word begins with a vowel (e.g., *les amis* is pronounced `[lezami]`).

In Old Irish, many words had endings that have since been lost in the modern language. These historical endings, often vowels or nasal consonants, caused predictable sound changes to the start of the following word to make pronunciation smoother. Over time, even after the original endings disappeared, the initial sound changes they caused were retained and became a fundamental part of the grammar.

## Lenition

Lenition originally occurred to make speech flow more smoothly by softening "stop" consonants (like `p`, `t`, `c`, `b`, `d`, `g`), which momentarily interrupt the flow of air.

<Details summary="Historical origin of lenition">
The primary cause of lenition in Old Irish was a consonant being positioned between two vowels, both within a word and across word boundaries. If a word ended in a vowel and the next word began with a consonant, that consonant was lenited.

Many of the vowel endings that once caused lenition have disappeared from modern Irish, but the mutation remains as a grammatical rule.

-   **The feminine article:** The singular feminine article was originally *`sinda`*. It ended in a vowel, which lenited the following noun. Although the article was later shortened to `an`, it still causes lenition on feminine nouns.
-   **The masculine genitive:** The masculine genitive singular article was *`sindi`*. Its final vowel also caused lenition, a pattern that continues today.
-   **The possessive adjective `a` (his):** This comes from an older form, *`esyo`*, which was a genitive form. Like the masculine genitive article, it ended in a way that caused lenition.
-   **Adjectives after feminine nouns:** In Old Irish, most feminine nouns ended in `-a` (similar to Latin). This final vowel caused lenition on any following adjective or genitive noun. This rule was later generalised to apply after all feminine nouns, regardless of their original ending.
</Details>

## Eclipsis and the N-Prefix

Eclipsis and the n-prefix (used before vowels) are the result of a preceding word that once ended in a nasal sound, typically `-n` or `-m`.

<Details summary="Historical origin of eclipsis">
When a word ended in a nasal sound like `-n`, it was difficult to pronounce a following voiceless consonant (like `p` or `t`) without voicing it. This led to changes like `n + p` becoming `n-b` and `n + t` becoming `n-d`. Many of these final nasal sounds have since disappeared, but the eclipsis they caused remains as a grammatical marker.

-   **Numerals:** The numbers `seacht` (seven), `naoi` (nine), and `deich` (ten) originally ended in a nasal consonant, similar to their Latin counterparts *septem*, *novem*, and *decem*. This historical nasal is preserved as the eclipsis they trigger today, e.g., *seacht mbád* (seven boats). The number `ocht` (eight) now also causes eclipsis by analogy, even though it never had a nasal ending.
-   **The preposition `i` (in):** This preposition was originally `in`, ending with a nasal that caused eclipsis. The eclipsis made the `-n` redundant, so the preposition was shortened to `i`.
-   **The conjunction `go` (that):** This word also had a historical nasal ending (compare with its Latin cognate *cum*), which is the source of the eclipsis it causes.
-   **Preposition + Article:** In Connacht and Munster, combinations like `ar an` cause eclipsis (e.g., *ar an mbord*). This comes from the old accusative form of the article, which was *`sindon`* (masculine) or *`sindan`* (feminine), ending in `-n`. In Ulster, the same combination causes lenition (*ar an bhord*). This derives from the old dative form of the article, *`sindu`* or *`sinda`*, which ended in a vowel. The distinction between accusative (movement towards) and dative (location) after prepositions is no longer made, but the two different historical forms survive as a dialectal difference.
-   **Genitive Plural:** The article in the genitive plural was *`sindan`*, whose final `-n` is the source of modern eclipsis in this case (e.g., *hata na bhfear*, the hat of the men).
-   **Plural Possessive Adjectives:** The plural possessives `ár` (our), `bhur` (your), and `a` (their) all derive from old genitive plural forms that ended in a nasal (*`saron`*, *`svaron`*, *`esyon`*), hence they cause eclipsis.
</Details>

## The T-Prefix

The t-prefix, which appears before vowels on masculine singular nouns after the article `an`, is a remnant of the old article itself.

<Details summary="Historical origin of the t-prefix">
The Old Irish masculine article was *`sindos`*. (The similarity to the modern demonstrative pronoun `sin` is not a coincidence; in many languages, articles evolve from demonstrative pronouns).

Over time, *`sindos`* was shortened to `an`. However, before a word starting with a vowel, the `-d-` of *`sindos`* was preserved and eventually became the voiceless `t-` of the modern `an t-`.

A similar process explains the `t` in `an tsúil` (the eye). The feminine article *`sinda`* caused lenition on a following `s`, turning it into `sh-` (pronounced /h/). This `/h/` sound caused the preceding `-d` of the article to become a voiceless `t`, resulting in the `ts-` combination.
</Details>

## The H-Prefix

The h-prefix is generally used to separate two vowel sounds, but historically it is often the remnant of a lenited final `-s` from the preceding word.

<Details summary="Historical origin of the h-prefix">
In Old Irish, as in many other Indo-European languages, many words ended in `-s`. When such a word was followed by a word beginning with a vowel, the final `-s` was lenited to an `/h/` sound.

-   **The possessive adjective `a` (her):** This comes from an older form, *`esjas`*. The final `-s` was lenited, creating the h-prefix that now follows `a` (her) before a vowel.
-   **The feminine genitive article:** The genitive singular form of the feminine article was *`sindas`*. Its final `-s` is the source of the h-prefix in this context.
-   **The plural article `na`:** The nominative plural article was also *`sindas`*, which again explains the h-prefix it triggers before vowels.

In contrast, the masculine article *`sindos`* had a different vowel in its ending. This led to a different sound change where the lenited `-s` devoiced the preceding `-d-` to `-t-`, resulting in the t-prefix rather than the h-prefix.
</Details>
