In addition to lenition and eclipsis, Irish uses three prefixes—`t-`, `h-`, and `n-`—to change the initial sound of a word. These changes, known as *réamhlitreacha* (prefix letters), are often triggered by preceding particles, especially the definite article.

## The t-Prefix

The t-prefix is added to words beginning with a vowel or the letter `s`. Its application depends on two different principles, but in the standard language, it almost always appears after the singular article `an`.

**Spelling and Pronunciation**

*   Before a vowel, it is written with a hyphen: `t-a`, `t-e`. In capital letters, the hyphen is omitted: `tA`, `tE`.
    *   *an t-Éireannach* - the Irishman
*   Before `s`, it is written as `ts`. In capital letters, only the `s` is capitalised: `tS`.
    *   *an tsráid* - the street

<Note>
When `ts` appears as a result of the t-prefix, the `s` sound is dropped in pronunciation. For example, `an tsráid` is pronounced roughly as [an tráid].
</Note>

### Usage with Vowels

The t-prefix is used before masculine singular nouns that begin with a vowel, but only in the nominative and accusative cases after the article `an`.

*   *an t-úll* - the apple

It is not used in the genitive or dative cases.

*   *craiceann an úill* - the skin of the apple (genitive)
*   *ar an úll* - on the apple (dative)

### Usage with s-

The t-prefix is used before nouns beginning with `s-` (followed by a vowel, `l`, `n`, or `r`) in specific situations after the article `an`. This usage effectively replaces lenition, which cannot apply to `s`.

**Nominative and Accusative (Feminine Nouns)**
Feminine nouns in the nominative/accusative singular take a t-prefix.
*   *an tsráid* - the street

**Genitive (Masculine Nouns)**
Masculine nouns in the genitive singular take a t-prefix.
*   *an tsagairt* - of the priest

**Dative (After Prepositions)**
Usage in the dative case (after a preposition + `an`) varies by dialect.

<Dialect region="Standard">
In Standard Irish and Connacht, only feminine nouns take the t-prefix in the dative, even where eclipsis would normally be expected. Masculine nouns are unaffected.
*   *ar an tsráid* - on the street (feminine)
*   *ag an sagart* - at the priest's (masculine)
</Dialect>

<Dialect region="Ulster">
In Ulster, both masculine and feminine nouns take the t-prefix in the dative.
*   *ag an tsagart* - at the priest's
</Dialect>

<Dialect region="Munster">
In Munster, both masculine and feminine nouns take the t-prefix, but only after the combined prepositions `den` (`de + an`), `don` (`do + an`), and `sa` (`i + an`).
*   *don tsagart* - to the priest
</Dialect>

### Usage with Numbers

The t-prefix appears before numbers beginning with a vowel after the article `an` in the nominative/accusative.

*   *an t-aon bhád amháin* - the one boat
*   *an t-ochtú* - the eighth
*   *an t-ochtar* - the eight people

However, it is not used in the dative: *ar an ochtar* (on the eight people).

<Note>
In the genitive case, numbers beginning with `s-` do **not** take a t-prefix in the standard language.
*   *i dtoiseach an seachtú haois déag* - at the beginning of the 17th century

An exception is the personal numbers `seisear` (six people) and `seachtar` (seven people), which do take a t-prefix in the genitive.
*   *i measc an tseachtair* - among the seven people
</Note>

### Dialectal and Historical Usage

In some dialects, a t-prefix appears before `s-` in other contexts, particularly after words ending in `-n` or `-l`.

*   *an-tsean* - very old (Standard: `an-sean`)
*   *aon tsúil* - one eye (Standard: `aon súil`)

This is also seen in some proper names and place names.

*   *Clann tSuibhne* - the MacSweeney family
*   *Cionn tSáile* - Kinsale

<Details summary="Historical note">
This dialectal usage is generally considered etymologically incorrect, as the `t-` historically derives from the definite article. However, it is widespread and may be explained as a phonologically conditioned insertion. In some proper names like `Clann tSuibhne`, the `t-` may reflect an older form of the preceding word, such as `*cland Shuibhne`.
</Details>

<Warning>
Do not confuse the t-prefix with `t'`, which is a common contraction of the possessive pronoun `do` (your). The apostrophe is the key difference.
*   *t'uncail* - your uncle
</Warning>

## The h-Prefix

The h-prefix is only used before words that begin with a vowel. It serves to separate two adjacent vowel sounds where neither lenition nor eclipsis is required.

**Spelling**

The `h` is placed directly before the vowel: `ha`, `he`. In capital letters, only the original vowel is capitalised: `hA`, `hE`.

*   *Poblacht na hÉireann* - Republic of Ireland

<Note>
Older orthography sometimes used a hyphen (`h-Eireann`), but this is no longer standard.
</Note>

### Usage with Nouns

*   **After the article `na`**:
    *   In the plural (nominative, accusative, dative): *na héin* - the birds
    *   In the genitive singular of feminine nouns: *na hoifige* - of the office
*   **After `a` (her)**:
    *   *a huncail* - her uncle
    *   *a dhá húll* - her two apples
*   **After certain numbers**:
    *   After `trí`, `ceithre`, `cúig`, `sé` with plural nouns like `uaire` (hours) or `orlaí` (inches): *trí huaire* - three hours; *cúig horlaí* - five inches.
    *   This does not apply to singular nouns: *trí úll* - three apples.
*   **After ordinal numbers** (except `an chéad`):
    *   *an tríú húll* - the third apple
*   **After non-leniting prepositions**:
    *   *le hÚna* - with Úna
    *   *go hÉirinn* - to Ireland
*   **After `cá` (which)**:
    *   *cá háit?* - where? (literally "which place?")
    *   *cá haois?* - how old? (literally "which age?")
*   **In names with `Ó`**:
    *   *Ó hEidhin*, *Ó hAnluain*. The `h` disappears after leniting particles like `Ní` or `Uí` (*Ní Eidhin*).
*   **After `ní` (negative copula)** in specific cases:
    *   With nouns like `áibhéil` (exaggeration), `ionadh` (wonder), `ualach` (burden): *Ní hiontas ar bith é sin.* - That is no wonder at all.
    *   In set copular phrases: *Ní heol dó a athrach.* - He knows nothing else.
    *   In some proverbs: *Ní heolas go haontíos.* - You don't know someone until you live with them.

### Usage with Pronouns

The h-prefix is used before the pronouns `é`, `í`, `iad`, `ea`, etc., in these contexts:

*   After `ní` (negative copula): *ní hé* - it is not he; *ní hea* - it is not so.
*   After `cé` (who): *cé hé?* - who is he?
*   After `pé` (whoever): *pé hé tú féin* - whoever you are.

### Usage with Adjectives and Adverbs

*   After the adverbial particle `go`: *go holc* - badly.
*   After `chomh` (so, as): *chomh holc* - so bad.
*   After `ní` (negative copula) with specific adjectives like `ionann` (same) or `ansa` (difficult): *ní hionann iad* - they are not the same.
*   In set copular phrases: *Ní heagal duit an madra.* - You don't need to be afraid of the dog.

### Usage with Verbs

*   After the negative imperative particle `ná`: *Ná habair é!* - Don't say it!
*   Before the autonomous form (*Saorbhriathar*) in certain non-standard or dialectal contexts, often reflecting the historical particle `do`:
    *   *Hóladh é.* - It was drunk.
    *   *Níor hóladh é.* - It was not drunk.

<Dialect region="Munster">
The negative interrogative particle `ná` adds an h-prefix.
*   *Ná hólair é?* - Don't you drink it?
</Dialect>

### Other Cases

*   Before the numbers `aon` (1) and `ocht` (8) after the counting particle `a`: *a haon*, *a hocht*.
*   In the names of weekdays with `Dé`: *Dé hAoine* - Friday.

## The n-Prefix

The n-prefix is the form that eclipsis takes before a vowel.

**Spelling**

The `n-` is attached with a hyphen. In capital letters, the hyphen is omitted: `n-a`, `nA`.

*   *Tír na nÓg* - The Land of Youth (literally "land of the youths")

### Usage

The n-prefix appears wherever a word or particle would cause eclipsis on a following word that begins with a vowel.

*   *ár n-uncail* - our uncle
*   *i gcaitheamh na n-aoiseanna* - throughout the ages

<Note>
The n-prefix is not written after words that already end in `-n`, such as the article `an` or the preposition `in`. The eclipsis is still present in the grammar, but it is not reflected in the spelling.
*   *ar an úll* - on the apple (not `*ar an n-úll`)
*   *in Éirinn* - in Ireland
</Note>

<Dialect region="Connacht, Munster">
Verbal nouns after the preposition `le` meaning "(in order) to" take an n-prefix, even though `le` does not normally cause eclipsis. This is sometimes spelled `len'`.
*   *Cad a bheidh agat le n-ól?* - What will you have to drink?
*   *le n-é a dhéanamh* - to do it
</Dialect>

## Other Prefixes (Contractions)

The following forms look like prefixes but are actually contractions of common words. They are always written with an apostrophe to show that letters have been omitted.

### d' (Past Particle)

This is a shortening of the past tense particle `do`. In most dialects, it only appears before verbs starting with a vowel or `fh-`.

*   *d'ól mé* - I drank

### d' / t' (Possessive Pronoun)

These are contractions of the possessive pronoun `do` (your). `d'` is standard and common in Ulster, while `t'` is more common in other dialects.

*   *d'athair* / *t'athair* - your father

### M' (Possessive Pronoun)

This is a contraction of the possessive pronoun `mo` (my).

*   *m'athair* - my father

### D' (Preposition)

This can be a contraction of the prepositions `do` (to) or `de` (from, of).

*   *d'athair* - to a father / from a father

### B' / mb' (Copula)

This is a contraction of `ba`, the past/conditional form of the copula. It becomes `mb'` after a particle that causes eclipsis.

*   *b'fhéidir* - perhaps (literally "it would be possible")
*   *gach rud dá mb'fhéidir* - everything that would be possible
