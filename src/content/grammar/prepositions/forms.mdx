Prepositions are small words like "in", "on", "with", and "for" that link nouns to other parts of a sentence. In Irish, they are called `réamhfhocail`. They are fundamental to building sentences but have a few features that are different from English.

The most important things to learn about Irish prepositions are:
1.  They can cause initial mutations (lenition or eclipsis) on the noun that follows them.
2.  They combine with pronouns to form single words, often called "conjugated prepositions" or "prepositional pronouns".

## Simple and Compound Prepositions

Irish has two main types of prepositions.

**Simple prepositions** are single words. The most common ones are:
`ag`, `ar`, `as`, `chuig`, `de`, `do`, `faoi`, `gan`, `i`, `idir`, `le`, `ó`, `roimh`, `thar`, `trí`, `um`.

**Compound prepositions** are phrases made of two or more words, usually ending with a simple preposition.
- *in aice le* - next to
- *os comhair* - in front of
- *i lár* - in the middle of

## Mutations after Prepositions

Many simple prepositions cause an initial mutation on the noun that follows them. This is a key part of correct Irish grammar.

### Lenition
Some prepositions cause lenition (adding an `h`). These include `de`, `do`, `faoi`, `ó`, `roimh`, and `trí`.

- *Thit sé de **chrann*** - He fell from a tree. (from `crann`)
- *Labhair mé le **Mháire*** - I spoke with Máire. (from `Máire`)

### Eclipsis
Some prepositions cause eclipsis (prefixing a new consonant sound). The most common is `i`.

- *Tá mé i **nGaillimh*** - I am in Galway. (from `Gaillimh`)
- *Chuir sé snaidhm sa **gcrann*** - He put a knot in the tree. (from `crann`)

### No Mutation
Some prepositions, like `ag`, `as`, and `gan`, do not cause a mutation on their own.

- *Tá mé ag an siopa* - I am at the shop.
- *Tháinig sé as Baile Átha Cliath* - He came from Dublin.

<Tip>
The rules for mutations can change when a preposition is followed by the definite article (`an`, `na`). For example, `ar` on its own doesn't cause a mutation, but `ar an` causes eclipsis: *ar an **mbord*** (on the table). Each preposition's page will detail these rules.
</Tip>

## Prepositional Pronouns

A major feature of Irish is that prepositions merge with pronouns. Instead of saying "with me" or "on you", Irish uses a single, combined word. These are often called "conjugated prepositions".

Every preposition has its own set of these forms. For example, here are the forms for `ag` (at):

| Person | Form | Meaning | Example Usage |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1st Sg. | `agam` | at me | *Tá peann agam* (I have a pen) |
| 2nd Sg. | `agat` | at you | *An bhfuil airgead agat?* (Do you have money?) |
| 3rd Sg. (m) | `aige` | at him | *Tá carr aige* (He has a car) |
| 3rd Sg. (f) | `aici` | at her | *Tá post aici* (She has a job) |
| 1st Pl. | `againn` | at us | *Tá teach mór againn* (We have a big house) |
| 2nd Pl. | `agaibh` | at you (pl) | *Tá fáilte romhaibh agaibh* (You are welcome) |
| 3rd Pl. | `acu` | at them | *Tá madra acu* (They have a dog) |

Mastering these forms is essential for fluency, as they are used constantly in everyday speech, especially for expressing possession with `ag`.

## Combining with the Definite Article

Some prepositions merge with the singular definite article `an` to form a single word.

- `i` + `an` → `sa` (in the)
  - *Tá an t-airgead **sa** sparán* - The money is in the wallet.
- `de` + `an` → `den` (from the / of the)
  - *Thit an leabhar **den** bhord* - The book fell from the table.
- `do` + `an` → `don` (to the / for the)
  - *Thug mé an t-úll **don** mhúinteoir* - I gave the apple to the teacher.
- `ó` + `an` → `ón` (from the)
  - *Fuair mé litir **ón** dochtúir* - I got a letter from the doctor.

Note that `le` + `an` becomes `leis an`, which remains two words.
- *Labhair mé **leis an** ngarda* - I spoke with the guard.
