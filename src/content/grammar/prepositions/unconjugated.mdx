While many common Irish prepositions change their form when followed by a pronoun (e.g., `agam`, `agat`), this group of prepositions does not. They are known as "unconjugated prepositions" and are always followed by a standard noun or pronoun, making them simpler to learn.

## go / go dtí (to, until, towards)

The preposition `go` and its longer form `go dtí` are used to express movement towards a place or duration until a certain time. `go` is typically used with place names that do not take an article, while `go dtí` is used with nouns that do have an article.

<Details summary="Etymology and variants">
The form `go dtí` literally means "until I come", where `tí` is an old subjunctive form of the verb `tar` (to come). In speech, the `go` is sometimes omitted, leaving a pronunciation like [di:].

Other historical or dialectal variants include:
- `go dtige` (using another subjunctive form of `tar`, heard in Connacht)
- `go nuige` (from Old Irish `conicci`, "until you meet")
- `go ruige` (from Old Irish `co-ricci`, "until you reach")
</Details>

### Initial Mutations

The mutation caused by `go` / `go dtí` depends on whether an article is present.

- **Without an article** (usually with `go`): No lenition is caused. An h-prefix is added to a following vowel.
  - *`go hÉirinn`* - to Ireland
- **With an article** (always with `go dtí`): The standard mutation rules for the nominative case after the article apply.
  - *`go dtí an teach`* - to the house
  - *`go dtí an t-oileán`* - to the island

<Dialect region="Munster">
In the Dingle peninsula, `go dtí` causes eclipsis on the following noun, regardless of the standard rules.
</Dialect>

### Pronominal Forms

In Standard Irish, `go dtí` does not have special pronominal forms. You simply use the preposition followed by the regular pronoun.

- *`go dtí mé`* - to me
- *`go dtí tú`* - to you
- *`go dtí é`* - to him

<Details summary="Historical note">
Etymologically, the pronominal forms of `chun`/`chuig` (e.g., `chugam`, `chugat`) originally belonged to `go`.
</Details>

<Dialect region="Munster">
Dialectal forms using possessive pronouns can sometimes be heard, such as `go dtína` for "until his/her".
</Dialect>

### Usage

#### Destination of spatial movement

Use `go dtí` for destinations with an article and `go` for place names without an article.

- *`go dtí an teach`* - to the house
- *`go Meireacá`* - to America

#### Temporal extent

Use `go dtí` to mean "until" a specific time or event, and `go` in some set phrases.

- *`go dtí an lá sin`* - until that day
- *`go maidin`* - until morning

#### Comparison or exception

In some expressions, `go dtí` can mean "compared to" or "except for".

- *`Níor fheabhas go dtí é`* - There was nothing better than it.
- *`Níor mhadra go dtí é.`* - There was no dog like him.
- *`Tá mo chuidse díobh ite go dtí dhá cheann.`* - I have eaten my share except for two.

## go (with)

This is a separate preposition from the one above. This `go` means "with", though its use is now mostly restricted to set phrases. It is distinguished by the fact that it causes eclipsis.

<Warning>
Do not confuse `go` (with), which causes eclipsis, with `go` (to), which does not cause any mutation. The context and mutation will tell you which one is being used.
</Warning>

### Initial Mutations

When `go` means "with", it causes **eclipsis** on the following noun.

- *`go n-onóir`* - with honour

### Pronominal Forms

There are no standard pronominal forms. However, special forms exist when `go` is followed by a possessive pronoun.

| Context | Form |
| :--- | :--- |
| with my | `go mo` |
| with your | `go do` |
| with his | `gona` |
| with her | `gona` |
| with our | `gonár` |
| with your (pl.) | `go bhur` |
| with their | `gona` |

### Usage

#### Basic meaning "with"

This usage is now rare or archaic.

- *`gona laigí daonna uile`* - with all his human weaknesses
- *`gona chuid gealseol`* - with his white sails

#### Measurements

The most common use of this preposition is in the phrase `go leith` ("with half"), used to mean "...and a half".

- *`trí chéad go leith punt`* - 350 pounds

#### Idioms

It appears in several common idioms.

- *`go bhfios dom`* - as far as I know (literally: "with knowledge to me")
- *`go gcuimhin liom`* - as far as I remember
- *`go gclos dom`* - from what I hear

<Details summary="Historical note">
This preposition is etymologically related to the Latin `cum` ("with"). There is also evidence that the conjunction `go` ("that") derives from it.
</Details>

## gan (without)

The preposition `gan` means "without" and is the opposite of `le` (with). It typically causes lenition.

### Initial Mutation

The standard rule is that `gan` causes **lenition**.

- *`gan phósadh`* - unmarried
- *`gan chiall`* - without sense, senseless
- *`gan phingin`* - without a penny

However, there are several important exceptions where **no lenition** occurs:

- Before the consonants `d`, `s`, and `t`.
  - *`gan fear`* - without a man
- In infinitive constructions and non-finite clauses.
  - *`gan pósadh`* - without marrying
- When a noun is used elliptically.
  - *`gan clú (a bheith agat)`* - without (having) fame
- When the noun is followed by another attribute or phrase.
  - *`gan pingin ina phóca`* - without a penny in his pocket
- Before proper nouns.
  - *`gan Pól`* - without Paul

### Pronominal Forms

`gan` has no special pronominal forms. You simply use it with the standard pronouns.

- *`gan mise`* - without me
- *`gan tusa`* - without you
- *`gan é`* - without him

### Usage

#### Basic meaning "without"

- *`gan obair`* - without work

#### gan ... gan (neither ... nor)

Used in pairs, it means "neither ... nor". The second `gan` can also be replaced with `ná`.

- *`gan obair gan arán`* - neither work nor bread
- *`gan obair ná arán`* - neither work nor bread

#### Semi-negative expressions

In phrases with `ach`, it can mean "with only".

- *`gan ach aon alt amháin`* - with only one article
- *`gan níos mó ná trí scillinge ina phóca aige`* - with only 3 shillings in his pocket

#### Negation in non-finite clauses

`gan` is used to negate a verbal noun, meaning "not to..." or "without ...-ing".

- *`Aontaím gan labhairt leis.`* - I agree not to speak to him.
- *`D'imigh mé gan labhairt leis.`* - I left without speaking to him.

## Mar (like, as)

`mar` is a common preposition used for comparisons or to state a role or function, meaning "like" or "as".

### Initial Mutation

- **Without an article**: `mar` causes **lenition**.
  - *`mar fhear`* - like a man
- **With an article**: Usually no mutation occurs.
  - *`mar an fear`* - like the man
- **With an article (rarely)**: In some fixed phrases, `mar` can cause **eclipsis**.
  - *`mar an ngrian`* - like the sun
  - *`mar an gcéanna`* - likewise, the same

### Pronominal Forms

`mar` has no special pronominal forms.

- *`mar mise`* - like me

### Usage

#### Comparison

- *`mar bhean`* - as a woman, like a woman

#### Manner

- *`gach éinne mar a dhúchas`* - everyone according to their nature

#### Apposition or clarification

- *`Tá sé fairsing is fionnuar, mar sheomra.`* - It is spacious and cool, as a room.
- *`mar atá`* - as is, namely

#### Classification or role

- *`Bhí sí mar Leas-Uachtarán ar an ngrúpa.`* - She was Vice-President of the group.

#### Compound prepositions

`mar` forms part of two common compound prepositions:

- **`mar gheall ar`** (because of)
  - *`mar gheall ar an mbád`* - because of the boat
- **`mar le`** (as for, in view of)
  - *`Is breá an aimsir í, mar le geimhreadh.`* - It is fine weather for a winter.

## Amhail (like)

`amhail` is a more formal or literary alternative to `mar`, also meaning "like". It does not cause any mutation.

### Initial Mutation

`amhail` causes no mutation.

- *`amhail fear`* - like a man
- *`amhail an fear`* - like the man

### Pronominal Forms

`amhail` has no special pronominal forms.

- *`amhail mise`* - like me

<Note>
The adverb `amhlaidh` ("so", "thus") is an exception, derived from `amhail` + `ea`.
</Note>

### Usage

`amhail` is used in the same way as `mar` but is less common in everyday speech.

- *`amhail carraig`* - like a rock

## Os (over)

`os` is an old preposition meaning "over" or "above". Today, it survives only in a few fixed expressions and does not cause any mutation.

<Details summary="Historical note">
`os` is related to the adverbs of place `suas` (up), `thuas` (above), and `anuas` (down from above). It formerly had pronominal forms like `uasam` (over me), but these are now obsolete.
</Details>

### Usage

- **`os cionn`** (over, above)
  - *`os mo chionn`* - above me
- **`os comhair` / `os coinne`** (opposite, in front of)
- **`os ard`** (aloud, openly)
- **`os íseal`** (in a low voice, secretly)
- **`os meán`** (above the average/measure)

## Seachas (except, other than, besides)

`seachas` is used to draw a distinction, meaning "other than", "besides", or "except".

<Details summary="Etymology">
`seachas` is a compound of the older preposition `seach` and the copula `is`. It is sometimes written `seach is` or just `seach`.
</Details>

### Usage

#### Other than / different from

- *`an teach seachas an ceann seo`* - a house other than this one

#### Besides / except for

- *`daoine seachas ise`* - people besides her
- *`seachas thú féin`* - except yourself

#### Compared to how

- *`seachas mar a`* + direct relative clause

#### Rather than

- *`seachas a chéile`* - rather than each other

#### Correction (and not)

It can be used to correct a statement, meaning simply "and not".

- *`Rinne an fear sin, seachas an bhean.`* - That man did it, not the woman.
- *`Bearna seachas Barna`* - Bearna and not Barna (correcting an anglicised place name)

<Warning>
`seachas` implies a relative exception (others besides me). To express an absolute exception (no one but me), use `ach amháin` or `cé is moite de`.

- *`seachas mise`* - besides me (i.e., others in addition to me)
- *`ach amháin mise`* - except for me (i.e., nobody but me)
</Warning>

## Murach (if it were not for)

The preposition `murach` means "if it were not for" or "without" and is used in conditional sentences.

- *`Murach an dochtúir, bheadh sé marbh.`* - If it weren't for the doctor, he would be dead.

## is (ago)

This specialised preposition is used in temporal phrases to mean "ago". It is almost always found contracted with the article `an` as `isan`.

<Details summary="Etymology">
`isan` comes from Old Irish `i san`, meaning "in that".
</Details>

### Usage

- *`mí is an lá inniu`* - a month ago today (literally: "a month until the day today")
- *`bliain is an t-am seo`* - a year ago at this time

## a (to)

The particle `a`, often classed as a preposition, is primarily used before a verbal noun to form the infinitive, like "to" in "to do". It is a reduced form of the preposition `do`.

It is pronounced as a neutral schwa sound [ə]. Before a vowel, it becomes silent.

- *`beoir a ól`* - to drink beer (pronounced [b′oːr′ oːl])

<Dialect region="Munster, Ulster">
In Dingle and Ulster, a `dh'-` prefix is often inserted after `a` when the verbal noun begins with a vowel or `fh-`.
- *`beoir a dh'ól`* - to drink beer (pronounced [b′oːr′ ə ɣoːl])
</Dialect>

<Details summary="Historical note">
In older texts and still in some Munster dialects, the full form `do` is used instead of `a`. For example, `chun na Gaeilge d'fhoghlaim` is an older way of writing `chun na Gaeilge a fhoghlaim` (to learn Irish).
</Details>

### Initial Mutations

The particle `a` causes **lenition**.

- *`rud a dhéanamh`* - to do something

### Usage

#### Infinitive constructions

It connects an object (or sometimes a subject) to its verbal noun.

- *`Bhí mé sásta míle a shiúl.`* - I was content to walk a mile.

#### In progressive relative clauses

It replaces `ag` in the progressive aspect (`tá ... ag déanamh`) when the object of the verb is the antecedent of the relative clause.

- *`an litir atá an fear a scríobh`* - the letter that the man is writing
- *`Céard atá tú a dhéanamh?`* - What are you doing?

#### In other progressive constructions

It also replaces `ag` when the object does not immediately follow the verbal noun for other reasons, such as emphasis or negation.

- *`Níl an fear a scríobh ach an litir.`* - The man is only writing the letter.
- *`Pingin ní raibh mé a shaothrú.`* - I didn't earn a penny.

#### Infinitive of purpose (Ulster)

In Ulster Irish, `a` often replaces `ag` after verbs of motion or verbs of starting/stopping to indicate purpose.

- *`Tháinig sé a chónaí sa tsráidbhaile.`* - He came to live in the village.

#### Other uses

A rare use outside of verbal nouns is in telling time.

- *`Tá sé a trí a chlog.`* - It is 3 o'clock.

## Dar (by)

`dar` is a preposition used in oaths, affirmations, and exclamations, meaning "by". The preposition `ar` is sometimes used in its place.

### Usage

- *`dar Dia!`* - by God!
- *`dar m'anam!`* - by my soul!
- *`dar m'fhocal!`* - by my word!
