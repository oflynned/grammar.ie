The simple preposition `de` primarily means "from" or "of". It is one of the most versatile prepositions in Irish, used to express separation, origin, material, and the partitive (a part of a whole).

In most dialects, `de` and the preposition `do` (to, for) have merged in pronunciation. They are typically pronounced [gə] in Connacht and [də] in Ulster and Munster. Only the conjugated forms help to distinguish them in speech.

## Core Grammar

### Case

`de` is followed by the dative case. In modern Irish, this is only noticeable in a few fixed phrases and with feminine singular nouns after the article, which are lenited.

### Initial Mutations

The mutations following `de` depend on whether the article is present.

-   **Without the article:** `de` causes **lenition**.
    -   *de thír* (from a country)
-   **With the singular article (`den`):** `de` causes **lenition**.
    -   *den Ghearmáin* (from Germany)
    -   In the Dingle peninsula (Corca Dhuibhne), eclipsis is sometimes found instead: *den nGearmáin*.
-   **With the singular article before `s`:** A **t-prefix** is added.
    -   *den tsagart* (from the priest)

### Form Before Vowels

Before a vowel or `fh-`, `de` contracts to `d’`. This form is identical to `do` before a vowel.

-   *d’Úna* (from Úna)

## Forms and Combinations

### With the Article

-   `de` + `an` (singular) becomes `den`.
-   `de` + `na` (plural) remains `de na`. The forms `des na` or `desna` are also seen.

### With Pronouns (Conjugated Forms)

Like all simple prepositions, `de` combines with pronouns to form a single word, often called a prepositional pronoun.

| Person | Standard Form | Emphatic Form | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1st sg (I) | `díom` | `díomsa` | from me |
| 2nd sg (you) | `díot` | `díotsa` | from you |
| 3rd sg (he/it) | `de` | `desean` | from him/it |
| 3rd sg (she/it) | `di` | `dise` | from her/it |
| 1st pl (we) | `dínn` | `dínne` | from us |
| 2nd pl (you) | `díbh` | `díbhse` | from you |
| 3rd pl (they) | `díobh` | `díobhsan` | from them |

**Dialect Notes:**

-   These forms are often lenited, especially after a vowel: `dhíom`, `dhíot`, etc.
-   In Connemara, short forms are common: `’íom`, `’íot`, `’e`, `’í`, `’ínn`, `’íbh`, `’íobh`.
-   The conjugated forms of `do` are often used instead, e.g., `dom`/`dhom` for `díom`/`dhíom`.

### With Possessive Pronouns

| Pronoun | Combination | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `mo` (my) | `de mo` | from my |
| `do` (your) | `de do` | from your |
| `a` (his/her/its) | `dá` | from his/her/its |
| `ár` (our) | `dár` | from our |
| `bhur` (your pl.) | `de bhur` | from your |
| `a` (their) | `dá` | from their |

**Dialect Notes:**

-   In Munster, `dem` and `ded` are sometimes used for `de mo` and `de do`.
-   In Ulster, `dena` may be used for `dá` (from his/her/their).

### With Relative Clauses

`de` combines with the relative particle `a` and the copula.

**Indirect Relative Pronoun and Copula**

| Tense | Relative Pronoun | Copula Form | Copula (before vowel) | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Present | `dá` | `dar` | `darb` | from which/whom |
| Past | `dár` | `dar` | `darbh` | from which/whom |

**General Relative Pronoun**

| Tense | Form | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Present | `dá` | from all that |
| Past | `dár` | from all that |

Note on spelling: Some sources distinguish between `dár` (relative pronoun) and `dar` (copula relative form), while others write both as `dar`.

### Question Words

-   *Cé de ...?* (From whom ...?)
-   *Cad de / Cé de / Cá de ...?* (From what ...?)

## Meanings and Usage

### Separation and Removal ('from', 'off')

This is a core meaning, similar to `ó`, but `de` often implies a closer connection or being part of something.

-   *Thit sé den chathaoir.* (He fell from the chair.)
-   *Bain díot an cóta.* (Take off your coat.)
-   *Stad sé den ól.* (He stopped drinking.)
-   *Tóg den bhord é.* (Take it off the table.)
-   *Scaoil díom!* (Let me go!)

### Attachment and Continuation ('to', 'with')

Conversely, `de` can indicate attachment or continuation.

-   *Greamaíonn sé den bhalla é.* (He attaches it to the wall.)
-   *Leanaim den obair.* (I continue with the work.)
-   *Lean de sin!* (Stick with that!)

### Relative Position ('of')

Used with adverbs of place.

-   *laistiar den teach* (behind the house)
-   *taobh abhus den abhainn* (this side of the river)
-   *faoi mhíle den chathair* (within a mile of the city)

### Origin and Descent ('from', 'of')

-   *bean de Ghearaltach* (a Fitzgerald woman)
-   *Cér díobh thú?* (To which family do you belong?)

### Cause ('of', 'by')

-   *Fuair sé bás den ocras.* (He died of hunger.)
-   *Tá mé tuirseach den chaint.* (I am tired of talking.)
-   *Tá mé buíoch díot.* (I am grateful to you.)
-   *Briseadh de thaisme é.* (It broke by accident.)

### Material and Substance ('of', 'made of')

-   *Rinneadh d’adhmad é.* (It was made of wood.)
-   *lámh déanta de mhaidí* (a hand made of sticks)
-   *íorna de shnáth* (a ball of yarn)

With the verb `déan`, it can mean "to become".
-   *Rinne fíon den uisce.* (Wine was made from the water / The water became wine.)

### Content and Filling ('with')

-   *Líonaim d’uisce é.* (I fill it with water.)
-   *Tá an buicéad lán de ghaineamh.* (The bucket is full of sand.)

### Depiction ('of')

-   *pictiúr de Sheán* (a picture of Seán)
-   *Tóg grianghraf díom!* (Take a photo of me!)

### Instrument and Means ('with', 'by')

-   *buille de bhata* (a blow with a stick)
-   *de ghlór ard* (with a loud voice)
-   *de léim* (with a jump)
-   *de bhóthar* (by road)
-   *de thraein* (by train)

### Apposition ('of', 'as')

`de` is used to link a noun to another noun that describes or classifies it.

-   *a leithéid de lá* (such a day)
-   *amadán de mhac* (a fool of a son)
-   *Is fearr d’fhear é ná Seán.* (He is a better man than Seán.)
-   *Tá sagart de mhac aige.* (He has a priest as a son.)

It is also used to show the extent of a difference in comparisons.
-   *Is sine de chúig bliana é.* (He is five years older.)

### Partitive Use ('of')

This is a very common function, used where English uses "of" to denote a part of a larger group or quantity. It is used instead of the partitive genitive.

-   *cuid de na daoine* (some of the people)
-   *píosa den cháca* (a piece of the cake)
-   *triúr de na mná* (three of the women)
-   *leabhar de chuid Joyce* (a book by Joyce)

It can also indicate belonging or being a characteristic of something.
-   *Is den mhúineadh é.* (It is part of good manners.)
-   *Tá sé de cheart agam é a dhéanamh.* (I have the right to do it.)

In "only" sentences (`ní ... ach ...`), `de` introduces the whole from which a part is taken.
-   *Ní bhfuair mé d’fhreagra ach sin.* (I only got that as an answer.)
-   *Níl de chairde aige ach a mhadra.* (He has no friends except his dog.)

### Time and Duration ('at', 'by')

-   *ag obair de ló is d’oíche* (working day and night)
-   *den chéad uair* (for the first time)

### Abstract Relations ('according to', 'concerning')

-   *de réir* (according to)
-   *de mo dhóighse* (in my opinion)
-   *de mo dheoin* (with my consent / willingly)
-   *de m’ainneoin* (against my will)

## In Surnames

In many surnames of Anglo-Norman origin, `de` is a borrowing from French "de" (of) or "le" (the). This `de` is not the native Irish preposition. It does not cause lenition and is pronounced differently, usually as [də] or [ə].

-   `de Búrca` (Burke, from "de Bourg")
-   `de Buitléir` (Butler, from "le Bouteillier")
