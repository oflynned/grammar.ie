The preposition `ag` primarily means "at" or "by". It is one of the most common and versatile prepositions in Irish, essential for expressing possession, the progressive aspect of verbs, and many other idiomatic concepts.

Although written `ag`, it is often pronounced /egʲ/. When used to form the progressive aspect, its pronunciation is reduced to /ə/ or /əg/ before a vowel.

In Munster Irish, `aige` is often used instead of `ag`, except in the progressive aspect. This also leads to dialectal combinations with the article, such as `aigen` for `ag an` and `aigesna` for `ag na`.

## Grammar Rules

### Case

`ag` is followed by the dative case. In modern Irish, this is only noticeable in a few nouns that have distinct dative forms.

### Initial Mutations

The mutation caused by `ag` depends on whether it is followed by the definite article `an`.

- **Without the article**: `ag` causes no mutation.
  - *ag Seán* (at Seán)
- **With the singular article `an`**: The mutation varies by dialect.
  - **Standard/Connacht**: Causes eclipsis, except on `d` and `t`.
    - *ag an bhfear* (at the man)
  - **Munster**: Causes eclipsis on all consonants, including `d` and `t`.
    - *ag an ndoras* (at the door)
  - **Connacht**: Adds a t-prefix to feminine singular nouns beginning with `s-`.
    - *ag an tsúil* (at the eye)
  - **Ulster**: Causes lenition.
    - *ag an fhear* (at the man)

## Conjugated Forms (Prepositional Pronouns)

When `ag` is followed by a pronoun, they combine to form a single word known as a prepositional pronoun.

| Person | Standard Form | Emphatic Form | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1st sg (I) | `agam` | `agamsa` | at me |
| 2nd sg (you) | `agat` | `agatsa` | at you |
| 3rd sg (he) | `aige` | `aigesean` | at him |
| 3rd sg (she) | `aici` | `aicise` | at her |
| 1st pl (we) | `againn` | `againne` | at us |
| 2nd pl (you) | `agaibh` | `agaibhse` | at you |
| 3rd pl (they) | `acu` | `acusan` | at them |

**Dialect Notes:**
- In Connemara, shortened forms are sometimes heard: `'am` for `agam`, `'ad` for `agat`, and `'ainn` for `againn`.
- In Munster, where `aige` is the base form, combinations with possessive pronouns can occur, such as `aigena` (at his) or `aigenár` (at our).

## Asking Questions

- `cé aige...?` (+ indirect relative clause) - at whom...?
- `cad aige / cé aige / cá haige...?` (+ indirect relative clause) - at what...?
- `cé acu...?` (+ direct relative clause) - which of them...?
- `cé againn/agaibh/acu...?` (+ direct relative clause) - who among us/you/them...?

## Usage

### Spatial and Temporal Location

`ag` indicates a location at or by a nearby reference point.

- *ag an bhfuinneoig* - at the window
- *ag a trí a chlog* - at 3 o'clock

### Progressive Aspect with Verbal Nouns

`ag` is used before a verbal noun to form the progressive aspect (the "-ing" form in English).

- *Tá mé ag ithe.* - I am eating. (Literally: "I am at eating.")

### Possession and Related States

The structure `tá` + noun + `ag` is the primary way to express possession in Irish. This pattern extends to many other concepts.

- **To have (possess)**: *Tá teach aige.* - He has a house.
- **To have a skill**: *Tá Gaeilge agam.* - I speak Irish. *Tá snámh aige.* - He can swim.
- **To have permission**: *Tá cead agam imeacht.* - I am allowed to leave.
- **To have to do something**: *Tá rud le déanamh agam.* - I have something to do.
- **To only have to**: *Níl agat ach fiafraí.* - You only have to ask.
- **To love**: `tá grá ag` (subject) `ar` (object). *Tá grá agam uirthi.* - I love her.
- **To desire**: `tá dúil ag` (subject) `i` (object). *Tá dúil aige sa cheol.* - He desires music.
- **To be interested**: `tá súim ag...` *Tá súim agam sa stair.* - I have an interest in history.
- **Perfect aspect with verbal adjectives**: *Tá an bord briste agam.* - I have broken the table. (Literally: "The table is broken by me.")

### With Adjectives

`ag` can indicate the person for whom an adjective's quality is relevant.

- *deas agat* - nice of you
- *éasca agam* - easy for me
- *maith againn* - good for us
- *trom aici* - heavy for her
- *loch róchúng ag luch* - a hole too narrow for a mouse

### Partitive Use (Subsets)

`ag` can be used instead of `de` to mean "of" when referring to a subset of a group, especially with plural pronouns.

- *go leor acu* - a lot of them
- *roinnt acu* - some of them
- *neart acu* - plenty of them

This is less common with nouns, but can occur: *neart ag tithe* (a lot of houses).

### Replacing Possessive Pronouns

`ag` is used to show possession after a noun modified by a demonstrative (`seo`, `sin`), a superlative, or an ordinal number.

- *an teach sin agamsa* - that house of mine
- *an mac is sine atá agam* - my eldest son
- *an chéad leabhar agam* - my first book

### Agent and Cause

`ag` is frequently used to mark the agent (the doer of an action) or the cause of a state.

- **General cause**: *Chuala mé agat féin é.* - I heard it from you yourself. *Tá tinneas cinn orm aige.* - I have a headache because of him.
- **Cause with adjectives**: *Táim tinn ag an ngrian.* - I am sick from the sunshine. It can also imply an action: *Táim bodhar agat.* - You have made me deaf.
- **Agent in passive/perfect constructions**: *Tá an bord briste ag Máirtín.* - The table was broken by Martin. / Martin broke the table. *Tá sé á dhéanamh agam.* - It is being done by me.
- **After adjectives + verbal nouns**: *Tá mé tinn tuirseach ag breathnú ort!* - I am sick and tired of watching you!

### Expressing Opinion

- *is beag agam é* - I think little of him/it. (Literally: "is little at-me it")
- *tá sé ina laoch acu* - they see him as a hero. (Literally: "he is in his hero with them")

### With Demonstratives

The phrases `ag seo` and `ag sin` can introduce something, similar to "here is" or "there is".

- *Ag seo mar a tharla sé...* - Here is how it happened...

This construction originates from an old imperative meaning "look!", and today the `ag` is often omitted.

### Common Verbs

`ag` is used with certain verbs to indicate source or responsibility.

- `clois ag` - to hear from
- `faigh ag` - to get from
- `fág ag` - to leave with

### Dialectal Use for 'chuig'

In Connacht and Ulster, `ag` is sometimes used as a substitute for the preposition `chuig` (to, towards), due to the similar pronunciation of `chuig` as /higʲ/ or /aigʲ/.
