The preposition `ag` is one of the most common in Irish, generally meaning "at" or "by". You will encounter it constantly, most notably in phrases expressing possession (like "I have") and in forming the progressive aspect of verbs (like "I am eating").

<Note>
Although spelled `ag`, it is usually pronounced as if it were spelled `aig` [eg′]. However, when used to form the progressive aspect, its pronunciation weakens to a schwa sound [ə], or [əg] before a vowel. After a vowel, it can become silent.
</Note>

<Dialect region="Munster">
In Munster, `aige` is often used instead of `ag` before a noun. This can also lead to combinations with the article, such as `aigen` for `ag an`, and `aigesna` for `ag na`.
</Dialect>

## Case

The preposition `ag` is followed by the dative case. In modern Irish, this is only distinct from the nominative for certain feminine nouns.

## Initial Mutations

The mutation caused by `ag` depends on whether it is followed by the definite article `an`.

### Without the Article

Without the article, `ag` causes no mutation.

*   *ag Seán* - at Seán's / with Seán

### With the Singular Article

With the singular article `an`, the mutation varies by dialect.

*   **Standard and Connacht:** `ag an` causes **eclipsis** on all consonants except `d` and `t`.
    *   *ag an bhfear* - at the man
*   **Connacht:** A **t-prefix** is added to feminine nouns beginning with `s-`.
    *   *ag an tsúil* - at the eye
*   **Munster:** **Eclipsis** applies to all consonants, including `d` and `t`.
    *   *ag an ndoras* - at the door
*   **Ulster:** `ag an` causes **lenition**.
    *   *ag an fhear* - at the man

## Question Words

`Ag` combines with question words to form several common questions.

*   `cé aige...?` (+ indirect relative clause) - at whom...?
*   `cad aige / cé aige / cá haige...?` (+ indirect relative clause) - at what...?
*   `cé acu...?` (+ direct relative clause) - Which of them...? / Which of the...?
*   `cé againn/agaibh/acu...?` (+ direct relative clause) - Who among us/you/them...?

## Conjugated Forms

Like all Irish prepositions, `ag` combines with pronouns to form a single word, often called a prepositional pronoun.

| Person | Standard Form | Emphatic Form | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1st Sg. (I) | `agam` | `agamsa` | at me |
| 2nd Sg. (you) | `agat` | `agatsa` | at you |
| 3rd Sg. (he) | `aige` | `aigesean` | at him |
| 3rd Sg. (she) | `aici` | `aicise` | at her |
| 1st Pl. (we) | `againn` | `againne` | at us |
| 2nd Pl. (you) | `agaibh` | `agaibhse` | at you |
| 3rd Pl. (they) | `acu` | `acusan` | at them |

<Dialect region="Connacht">
In Connemara, you may hear shortened forms like `’am` for `agam`, `’ad` for `agat`, and `’ainn` for `againn`.
</Dialect>

<Dialect region="Munster">
Because the base form `aige` is used, combinations with possessive pronouns can occur, such as `aigena` (at his) or `aigenár` (at our).
</Dialect>

## Usage

`Ag` has a wide range of uses beyond its core meaning of "at".

### Spatial and Temporal Location

`Ag` indicates a location at or by a nearby reference point.

*   *ag an bhfuinneoig* - at the window
*   *ag a trí a chlog* - at 3 o'clock

### Progressive Aspect

Used with a verbal noun, `ag` forms the progressive aspect (the "-ing" form in English).

*   *Tá mé ag ithe.* - I am eating. (Literally: "I am at eating")

### Possession and Related Idioms

The most common way to express "to have" is with the construction `tá` + noun + `ag`. This pattern extends to many related concepts.

*   **Possession:** *Tá teach aige.* - He has a house.
*   **Skills and Abilities:** *Tá Gaeilge agam.* - I speak Irish. / *Tá snámh aige.* - He can swim.
*   **Permission:** *Tá cead agam imeacht.* - I have permission to leave. / I am allowed to leave.
*   **Obligation:** *Tá rud le déanamh agam.* - I have something to do. / *Níl agat ach fanacht.* - You only have to wait.
*   **Love:** The structure is `tá grá ag` (subject) `ar` (object).
    *   *Tá grá agam uirthi.* - I love her.
*   **Desire:** The structure is `tá dúil ag` (subject) `i` (object).
    *   *Tá dúil aige sa cheol.* - He desires music. / He is fond of music.
*   **Interest:** *Tá suim agam sa stair.* - I have an interest in history.
*   **Perfect Aspect:** With a verbal adjective, this structure forms a perfect tense, indicating a completed action.
    *   *Tá an bord briste agam.* - I have broken the table. (Literally: "The table is broken by me.")

### With Adjectives

`Ag` can specify the person for whom an adjective's quality is relevant.

*   *Tá sé deas agat.* - That is nice of you.
*   *Tá an cheist éasca agam.* - The question is easy for me.
*   *Bhí an mála trom aici.* - The bag was heavy for her.

### Partitive Usage

`Ag` can be used instead of `de` ("of") to indicate a subset of a group, especially with plural pronouns.

*   *go leor acu* - a lot of them
*   *roinnt acu* - some of them
*   *neart acu* - plenty of them

With nouns, this is less common but possible: *neart ag tithe* - plenty of houses.

### Replacing Possessive Pronouns

`Ag` is used to show possession when a demonstrative pronoun (`seo`, `sin`, `siúd`) or a superlative/ordinal number prevents the use of a standard possessive pronoun.

*   *an teach sin agamsa* - that house of mine
*   *an mac is sine atá agam* - my eldest son
*   *an chéad leabhar agam* - my first book

### Agent or Cause

`Ag` can mark the agent (the doer of an action) or the cause of a state. This is closely related to its use in the perfect aspect.

*   *Chuala mé agat féin é.* - I heard it from you yourself.
*   *Tá tinneas cinn orm aige.* - I have a headache because of him.
*   *Táim tinn ag an ngrian.* - I am sick from the sunshine.
*   *Táim bodhar agat.* - You have made me deaf.
*   *Tá an féasta ullamh agam.* - I have prepared the feast.
*   *Tá an bord briste ag Máirtín.* - The table was broken by Martin. / Martin broke the table.
*   *Tá sé á dhéanamh agam.* - It is being done by me.

It can also mean "of" when an adjective is followed by a verbal noun.

*   *Tá mé tinn tuirseach ag breathnú ort!* - I am sick and tired of watching you!

### Expressing Opinion

`Ag` is used in idioms to express one's opinion or estimation of something.

*   *Is beag agam é.* - I think little of it. (Literally: "It is little at me.")
*   *Tá sé ina laoch acu.* - They see him as a hero. (Literally: "He is in his hero with them.")

### With Demonstrative Pronouns

The phrases `ag seo` and `ag sin` can be used to mean "here is" or "there is".

*   *Ag seo mar a mhínigh sé é.* - Here is how he explained it.

<Details summary="Historical note">
This use of `ag` is thought to originate from an old imperative form of a verb meaning "to see" or "to behold", rather than the preposition `ag`. In modern Irish, the `ag` is often dropped, resulting in sentences that begin directly with `seo` or `sin`.
</Details>

### With Specific Verbs

`Ag` forms set phrases with certain verbs.

*   `clois ag` - to hear from
*   `faigh ag` - to get from
*   `fág ag` - to leave with

### Dialectal Use for 'chuig'

<Dialect region="Connacht,Ulster">
In Connacht and Ulster, `ag` is sometimes used as a substitute for the preposition `chuig` (to, towards), due to the similarity in their pronunciation in those dialects (`chuig` > `aig`).
</Dialect>
