The preposition `do` generally means "to" or "for". It is one of the most common prepositions in Irish and appears in a wide variety of grammatical structures, from indicating the indirect object of a verb to forming expressions of necessity and possession.

<Dialect region="all">
In most dialects, the simple prepositions `do` and `de` ("from, of") are pronounced identically. Both are pronounced [gə] in Connacht and [də] in Ulster and Munster. The Connacht pronunciation [gə] is distinguished from the preposition `go` ("to, until") because `do` causes lenition, whereas `go` does not.
</Dialect>

## Initial Mutations

The preposition `do` causes lenition on the noun that follows it.

<MutationCard
  title="Without an article"
  rule="do + noun causes lenition."
  before="do Séan"
  after="do Sheán"
/>

*do Sheán* - to Seán

<MutationCard
  title="With the singular article"
  rule="don (do + an) + noun causes lenition."
  before="don máthair"
  after="dón mháthair"
/>

*dón mháthair* - to the mother

<Dialect region="Munster">
In the Dingle peninsula, eclipsis is sometimes heard instead of lenition after `dón`, for example, `dón mbord` instead of `dón bhord`.
</Dialect>

<MutationCard
  title="With the singular article before 's'"
  rule="don (do + an) + noun beginning with 's' adds a t-prefix."
  before="don sagart"
  after="dón tsagart"
/>

*dón tsagart* - to the priest

## Forms of 'do'

### Before Vowels and 'fh'

Before a word beginning with a vowel or `fh-`, `do` contracts to `d’`.

*d’Úna* - to Úna
*d’fhear* - to a man

<Note>
This contracted form `d'` is identical in spelling and pronunciation to the contracted form of the preposition `de` ("from, of"). Context is required to tell them apart.
</Note>

### With the Article

When `do` comes before the definite article, the two words combine.

| Combination | Result |
| :--- | :--- |
| `do` + `an` | `don` |
| `do` + `na` | `do na` |

<Dialect region="all">
The form `do na` is sometimes written or pronounced as `dos na` or `dosna`.
</Dialect>

### With Question Words

`Do` is used to form questions about the recipient or purpose of an action. These questions require an indirect relative clause.

- `cé dó?` - to whom?
- `cad dó / cé dó / cá dó?` - for what purpose? / why?

## Conjugated Forms

Like all simple prepositions, `do` combines with pronouns to form a single word. These are known as conjugated prepositions.

| Person | Standard Form | Emphatic Form | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1st sg. (I) | `dom` | `domhsa` | to me |
| 2nd sg. (you) | `duit` | `duitse` | to you |
| 3rd sg. (he) | `dó` | `dósan` | to him |
| 3rd sg. (she) | `di` | `dise` | to her |
| 1st pl. (we) | `dúinn` | `dúinne` | to us |
| 2nd pl. (you) | `daoibh` | `daoibhse` | to you |
| 3rd pl. (they) | `dóibh` | `dóibhsean` | to them |

<Note>
These forms are often lenited in speech, especially after vowels and slender consonants, giving `dhom` instead of `dom`, `dhuit` instead of `duit`, and so on.
</Note>

<Dialect region="Connacht">
In Connemara, shortened forms are common in speech: `'om` (dom), `'uit` (duit), `'ó` (dó), `'i` (di), `'úinne` (dúinn), `'aoibh` (daoibh), `'óibh` (dóibh).
</Dialect>

<Dialect region="Ulster">
In Ulster, `domh` or `damh` is used instead of `dom`.
</Dialect>

## Forms with Possessive Pronouns

`Do` also combines with possessive pronouns. These forms are important for constructing the progressive aspect with a pronominal object.

| Combination | Standard Form | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `do mo` | `dom` | to my |
| `do do` | `dod` | to your |
| `do a` | `dá` | to his / to her / to their |
| `do ár` | `dár` | to our |
| `do bhur` | `do bhur` | to your (pl.) |

<Dialect region="Munster">
In Munster, the forms `dom` (for `do mo`) and `dod` (for `do do`) are standard.
</Dialect>

<Dialect region="Ulster">
In Ulster, `dona` is often used instead of `dá` for "to his/her/their".
</Dialect>

<Note>
When used as the object of a verbal noun, `á` is used instead of `dá`. For example, *ag iarraidh é a dhéanamh* (trying to do it) but *á iarraidh* (trying it).
</Note>

## With Relative Clauses

`Do` combines with the indirect relative particle `a` (or `ar` in the past tense) and with the relative form of the copula.

| Tense | Relative Particle (`do` + `a`/`ar`) | Copula Form (`do` + `ar`) | Copula (before vowel) | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Present | `dá` | `dar` | `darb` | to which/who |
| Past | `dár` | `dar` | `darbh` | to which/who |

These forms are also used with the generalising relative pronoun, meaning "to everything that" or "to all those who".

## Common Uses

### Indirect Object (Dative)

`Do` marks the indirect object of a verb, indicating the person or thing for whom or to whom an action is done.

*Scríobh mé litir duit.* - I wrote you a letter.
*Thug sé an leabhar dom.* - He gave the book to me.
*Inis an scéal dóibh.* - Tell them the story.

This is different from indicating a direct spatial destination, which often uses `chuig`.

*Chuir mé litir chugat.* - I sent a letter to you.

### Proximity and Relationship

`Do` can indicate closeness, relationship, or exclusivity.

*Tá an siopa cóngarach don scoil.* - The shop is close to the school.
*Is deartháir dom é.* - He is a brother of mine.
*Teastaíonn seomra dom féin.* - I need a room for myself.

### In Wishes

`Do` is used in common greetings and wishes.

*Oíche mhaith duit!* - Good night to you!
*Nollaig faoi mhaise daoibh.* - A happy Christmas to you (all)!

### In Copula Sentences

`Do` is frequently used in sentences with the copula (`is`) to indicate the person affected by a quality or state.

*Is breá liom an aimsir, ach is fuar di.* - I love the weather, but it's cold for her.
*Ní gá duit imeacht.* - It is not necessary for you to leave. (You don't have to leave.)
*Is fíor duit.* - You are right. (lit. It is true for you.)

It is also used to express obligation or necessity (must/should).

*B’éigean dom rith.* - I had to run.
*Ba chóir duit staidéar a dhéanamh.* - You should study.

### Identifying the Subject

In certain common questions and statements with the copula, `do` identifies the subject.

*Cad is ainm duit?* - What is your name?
*Seán is ainm dom.* - My name is Seán.
*Cá haois duit?* - How old are you?
*Carb as duit?* - Where are you from?

### With Verbal Nouns

`Do` is used to mark the agent (subject) in certain verbal noun constructions, particularly those indicating time.

*tar éis é a dhéanamh dom* - after I had done it
*le linn é a dhéanamh duit* - while you were doing it
*roimh é a dhéanamh dó* - before he had done it

### In the Progressive Aspect

The forms of `do` with possessive pronouns are used to express the object in the progressive aspect (the "be...-ing" tenses).

*Tá mé do do bhualadh.* - I am hitting you.
*Tá mé do mo bhualadh aige.* - I am being hit by him.

### With the Verbal Adjective

`Do` can be used with a verbal adjective to express necessity or suitability.

*Ní gearánta dom.* - I shouldn't complain / I have no reason to complain.
*Is scéal inchreidte dom é.* - It is a believable story for me.

### Telling Time

`Do` can mean "to" when telling time, though `chun` is also common.

*Tá sé ceathrú don a trí.* - It is a quarter to three.

### Spatial Destination

Less commonly, `do` can indicate a spatial destination, where `chuig` or `go` would be more typical today.

*Téigh don siopa.* - Go to the shop.
*Don diabhal leat!* - Go to hell!

<Dialect region="Munster">
In Munster, `do` (contracted to `d'`) is sometimes still used as the infinitive marker before a verbal noun, where the standard form today is `a`.

*chun Gaeilge d’fhoghlaim* - to learn Irish
</Dialect>
