The preposition `i` is one of the most fundamental prepositions in Irish, primarily meaning "in". It is used to describe location, time, states, and membership. It triggers specific initial mutations and combines with the definite article and pronouns to form new words.

<Note>
Despite its spelling, `i` is pronounced as a short schwa sound, [ə], similar to the 'a' in "about". The spelling is historical, deriving from an older form `in`.
</Note>

## Case and Meaning

The preposition `i` is followed by the dative case. In modern Irish, the dative is usually identical to the nominative, so this is rarely a concern for learners except with a few irregular nouns.

Unlike some other languages, Irish does not distinguish between location ("in") and movement ("into") with this preposition. The same form is used for both.

- *sa phota* - in the pot / into the pot

## Initial Mutations

The mutation that `i` causes depends on whether it is followed by the definite article.

### Without the Article

When `i` is used alone before a noun, it causes **eclipsis**.

- *i gCeanada* - in Canada
- *i mBaile Átha Cliath* - in Dublin

### With the Article

When `i` combines with the singular definite article to form `sa` or `san`, the mutation rule varies by dialect. The Standard Irish rule is the most common one taught to learners.

In Standard Irish, `sa`/`san` causes **lenition**. A **t-prefix** is added to feminine nouns beginning with `s`.

- *sa chistin* - in the kitchen
- *san oifig* - in the office
- *sa tsráid* - in the street (feminine)

<Dialect region="Ulster">
In Ulster, `sa`/`san` causes **lenition**. A **t-prefix** is added to both masculine and feminine nouns beginning with `s`.

- *sa Ghearmáin* - in Germany
- *sa tseomra* - in the room (masculine)
</Dialect>

<Dialect region="Munster">
In Munster, `sa`/`san` generally causes **lenition**. However, words beginning with `f` are **eclipsed** instead. A **t-prefix** is added to both masculine and feminine nouns beginning with `s`.

- *sa bhfarraige* - in the sea
</Dialect>

<Dialect region="Connacht">
In Connacht, `sa`/`san` causes **eclipsis** (except on `d` and `t`). A **t-prefix** is added only to feminine nouns beginning with `s`.

- *sa nGearmáin* - in Germany
- *sa tsúil* - in the eye (feminine)
</Dialect>

## The Form 'in'

Under certain circumstances, the preposition takes the form `in` instead of `i`. This form does not cause any initial mutation.

This occurs:
- Before vowels: *in Éirinn* (in Ireland), *in Albain* (in Scotland).
- Before the number `dhá` (two): *in dhá bhróig* (in two shoes).
- Before the possessive pronoun `bhur` (your, plural): *in bhur dteach* (in your house).
- Before titles of books, films, etc.: *in "Cré na Cille"* (in "Graveyard Clay").

<Note>
In older texts, you may see eclipsis after `i` before a vowel, such as *i nÉirinn*. The modern standard is `in Éirinn` with no mutation.
</Note>

## The Forms 'ins' and 'is'

In some dialects, `i` becomes `ins` or `is` before `gach` (every) and its variants like `chuile` or `chaon`. In these cases, `gach` is not eclipsed.

- *ins gach bliain* - in every year
- *sa chuile áit* - everywhere

The standard form remains `i ngach`, with eclipsis.

## Combining with the Definite Article

The preposition `i` merges with the definite article `an`, `an`, and `na`.

- `i` + `an` (before a consonant) = `sa`
- `i` + `an` (before a vowel) = `san`
- `i` + `na` (plural) = `sna`

In some dialects, you may also encounter the unmerged forms `ins an` and `ins na`.

## Conjugated and Possessive Forms

Like all Irish prepositions, `i` combines with pronouns to form a single word, known as a conjugated or prepositional pronoun. It also combines with possessive pronouns.

### Conjugated Forms (in me, in you)

These forms mean "in" + a personal pronoun.

| Person | Standard Form | Emphatic Form | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1st sg. | `ionam` | `ionamsa` | in me |
| 2nd sg. | `ionat` | `ionatsa` | in you |
| 3rd sg. (m) | `ann` | `annsan` | in him, in it |
| 3rd sg. (f) | `inti` | `intise` | in her, in it |
| 1st pl. | `ionainn` | `ionainne` | in us |
| 2nd pl. | `ionaibh` | `ionaibhse` | in you |
| 3rd pl. | `iontu` | `iontusan` | in them |

### Possessive Forms (in my, in your)

These forms mean "in" + a possessive pronoun.

| Person | Possessive Form | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 1st sg. | `i mo` (or `im`) | in my |
| 2nd sg. | `i do` (or `id`) | in your |
| 3rd sg. (m) | `ina` | in his |
| 3rd sg. (f) | `ina` | in her |
| 1st pl. | `inár` | in our |
| 2nd pl. | `in bhur` | in your |
| 3rd pl. | `ina` | in their |

<Note>
The forms `ina` and `inár` are often contracted in speech and writing to `'na` and `'nár`, especially after a vowel. In Munster, `im` and `id` are the common forms for "in my" and "in your", while other dialects may use the full `i mo` and `i do`.
</Note>

## Adverbial Forms with 'Seo', 'sin', and 'Siúd'

The 3rd person singular masculine form `ann` combines with the demonstratives `seo`, `sin`, and `siúd` to create adverbs of place.

- `ann` + `seo` = `anseo` (here)
- `ann` + `sin` = `ansin` (there; then)
- `ann` + `siúd` = `ansiúd` (over there, yonder)

When used in the literal sense of "in this" or "in that", the words are usually kept separate: `ann seo`, `ann sin`.

<Warning>
Learners often confuse three similar-sounding forms. Pay close attention to the spelling and context.

| Form | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| `annsan` | in him (emphatic) | *Tá an mhaith annsan.* (The good is in him.) |
| `ann sin` | in that | *Tá ciall ann sin.* (There is sense in that.) |
| `ansin` | there / then | *Bhí mé ansin.* (I was there.) |
</Warning>

## Relative Clause Forms

The preposition `i` combines with the indirect relative pronoun `a` and the relative form of the copula `ar`.

| Tense | Relative Pronoun (`i` + `a`/`ar`) | Copula Form (`i` + `ar`) | Copula Form (before vowel) | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Present | `ina` | `inar` | `inarb` | in which |
| Past | `inar` | `inar` | `inarbh` | in which |

## Common Uses

### Spatial Location

The most common use is to indicate a location *in* or *inside* something.

- *Tá an bainne sa chuisneoir.* - The milk is in the fridge.

### Relative Location

It can also describe a relative position, similar to "at".

- *in íochtar na tíre* - in the lower part of the country
- *i mbun an chnoic* - at the bottom of the hill
- *in imeall na foraoise* - at the edge of the forest

### Temporal Usage

It is used to specify a time *in* which something happens.

- *san oíche* - in the night
- *i mí Eanáir* - in January

### Describing Qualities or Parts

`i` can be used to specify a part of something or to describe an inherent quality.

- *briste sa lár* - broken in the middle
- *bán sa ghruaig* - fair-haired (literally "white in the hair")
- *Tá éirim an cheoil sa ghasúr.* - The boy has a musical aptitude.

### Describing a State

It is used to describe a person's physical or emotional state.

- *i bpian* - in pain
- *in imní* - in anxiety

### Expressing 'in a state of being'

A very common structure uses `bí` + `i` + possessive pronoun + noun to describe a state, role, or condition.

- **Progressive with verbs of state**: This structure forms the progressive aspect for verbs like "sleep", "sit", "stand", and "live".
  - *Tá mé i mo chodladh.* - I am sleeping. (literally "I am in my sleeping")
  - *Tá sí ina cónaí i nGaillimh.* - She lives in Galway. (literally "She is in her living...")

- **Temporary roles or professions**: It is used instead of the copula to describe a temporary or newly acquired role.
  - *Tá mé i mo dhochtúir.* - I am a doctor.
  - *Tá tú i do fhear anois.* - You are a man now.

- **Existence**: The form `ann` is used with `tá` to express existence, meaning "there is" or "there are".
  - *Tá teach ann.* - There is a house.

- **Becoming**: The verb `éirigh` is used with this structure to mean "to become".
  - *Éireoidh mé i mo dhochtúir.* - I will become a doctor.

### Membership

It is used to indicate membership in a group or organisation.

- *sna Gardaí* - in the police (Gardaí)
- *i mbanna ceoil* - in a band

### Expressing Ability ('in Ann')

The phrase `in ann` followed by a verbal noun means "to be able to" or "capable of".

- *An bhfuil tú in ann snámh?* - Are you able to swim?

### Rates and Ratios ('per')

With the article, it can mean "per".

- *deich bpunt sa lá* - ten pounds per day

### Distributive Usage ('for')

It can be used in a "one for one" sense.

- *fear san fhear* - man for man

### With Specific Verbs

Some verbs are idiomatically followed by `i`.

- *glaoigh in ainm* - to call by name
