The preposition `thar` means "past", "over", or "beyond". It is used to describe movement, to talk about time, and to make comparisons. Like other prepositions, it causes initial mutations on the following noun and combines with pronouns to form a unique set of words.

## Case and Mutations

The preposition `thar` is followed by the dative case. In modern Irish, the dative form of a noun is almost always the same as its nominative (dictionary) form. However, `thar` causes different initial mutations depending on whether it is followed by the definite article `an`.

### Without the Article

When `thar` is followed by a noun without the article, it causes **lenition**.

*   *thar bhosca* - over a box
*   *thar chat* - past a cat

<Note>
With general, abstract, or uncountable nouns, lenition is often omitted.
- *thar muir* - overseas (lit. "past sea")
</Note>

### With the Article

When `thar` is followed by the singular definite article `an`, the standard mutation is **eclipsis**.

*   *thar an mbosca* - over the box
*   *thar an gcathaoir* - past the chair

This rule has several exceptions and dialectal variations.

*   Nouns beginning with `d` or `t` are not eclipsed in the standard language.
*   Nouns beginning with `l`, `n`, `r`, `m`, or a vowel do not change.

<Dialect region="Munster">
In Munster, nouns beginning with `d` and `t` are also eclipsed.
- *thar an dteach* - past the house (Standard: *thar an teach*)
</Dialect>

<Dialect region="Connacht">
In Connacht, a t-prefix is added to feminine nouns beginning with `s-`.
- *thar an tsúil* - past the eye
</Dialect>

<Dialect region="Ulster">
In Ulster, **lenition** is used after `thar an` instead of eclipsis.
- *thar an bhosca* - over the box (Standard: *thar an mbosca*)
</Dialect>

## Conjugated Forms

When `thar` is followed by a pronoun, they combine to form a single word.

| Person | Standard Form | Emphatic Form | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1st sg. | `tharam` | `tharamsa` | past me |
| 2nd sg. | `tharat` | `tharatsa` | past you |
| 3rd sg. (m) | `thairis` | `thairis-sean` | past him |
| 3rd sg. (f) | `thairti` | `thairtise` | past her |
| 1st pl. | `tharainn` | `tharainne` | past us |
| 2nd pl. | `tharaibh` | `tharaibhse` | past you |
| 3rd pl. | `thartu` | `thartusan` | past them |

<Note>
The form `thart` means "past it" and is often used as an adverb meaning "past" or "over".
- *Chuaigh an bus thart.* - The bus went past.
</Note>

## Usage

### Spatial Movement

`thar` can describe horizontal movement ("past") or vertical movement ("over").

*   *Shiúil sé thar an teach.* - He walked past the house.
*   *Léim an madra thar an mbosca.* - The dog jumped over the box.

### Time

It can be used to mean "over" or "past" a certain time.

*   *Bhí sé thar am agat teacht.* - It was high time for you to come. (lit. "it was over the time at you")

### Figurative Meaning

Figuratively, `thar` can mean "beyond" or "despite".

*   *Rinne sé é thar chomhairle a athar.* - He did it despite his father's advice. (lit. "over his father's advice")

### Quantity and Comparison

`thar` is frequently used to mean "more than" or "beyond" a certain quantity.

*   *Tá sé anseo thar bhliain.* - He has been here for over a year.
*   *D’ól sé braon thar an gceart.* - He drank a drop too much. (lit. "a drop over the right")

It also appears in special comparative phrases. The forms `thar is` and `tharas` are also used in this context.

*   *Tá an scannán thar a bheith go maith.* - The film is more than good / exceptionally good.
*   *Bhí sé níos deacra thar mar a shíl mé.* - It was more difficult than I thought.
*   *Is fearr é tharas aon duine eile.* - He is better than anyone else.

## Asking Questions

To ask "past whom?" or "past what?", you use the form `thart` with a question word and an indirect relative clause.

*   *Cé thart a chuaigh tú?* - Whom did you go past?
*   *Cad thart a léim an cat?* - What did the cat jump over?
