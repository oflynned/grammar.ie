The preposition `ar` is one of the most versatile in Irish, with a core meaning of "on". Beyond simple location, it is used to express possession of states and emotions, names, obligations, and much more. It also triggers specific initial mutations on the word that follows it.

<Note>
  Although written `ar`, this preposition is usually pronounced as if it were spelled `oir` or `eir` ([er′]).
</Note>

## Initial Mutations

The mutation caused by `ar` depends on whether it is followed by the definite article `an`.

### Without the Article

When `ar` is followed directly by a noun, the mutation depends on the noun's meaning.

#### Lenition with Specific Nouns

`ar` causes lenition on specific, concrete nouns.

- *ar mhuir* - on a sea
- *ar bhord* - on a table
- *ar chíos mór* - for a high rent

#### No Mutation with General or Abstract Nouns

`ar` causes no mutation on abstract or general nouns, especially when describing a state or a general location.

- *ar muir* - at sea
- *ar bord* - on board
- *ar cíos* - for rent

<Warning>
The difference between *ar mhuir* (on a specific sea) and *ar muir* (at sea, in general) is subtle but important. Pay close attention to the context to determine whether the noun is being used in a specific or a general sense.
</Warning>

### With the Singular Article

When `ar` is followed by the singular article `an`, the standard rule is to cause **eclipsis** on the following noun.

- *ar an mbord* - on the table

This rule has significant dialectal variations.

<Dialect region="Ulster">
In Ulster Irish, lenition is used instead of eclipsis: *ar an bhord*.
</Dialect>

<Dialect region="Connacht">
In Connacht, a t-prefix is added to feminine singular nouns beginning with `s-`: *ar an tsúil* (on the eye).
</Dialect>

<Dialect region="Munster">
In Munster, the consonants `d` and `t` are also eclipsed, which is not the case in the standard language.
</Dialect>

## Forms with Pronouns

Like all Irish prepositions, `ar` combines with pronouns to create a single word. These are known as conjugated prepositions or prepositional pronouns.

| Person | Standard Form | Emphatic Form | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1st sg. (I) | `orm` | `ormsa` | on me |
| 2nd sg. (you) | `ort` | `ortsa` | on you |
| 3rd sg. (he) | `air` | `airsean` | on him |
| 3rd sg. (she) | `uirthi` | `uirthise` | on her |
| 1st pl. (we) | `orainn` | `orainne` | on us |
| 2nd pl. (you) | `oraibh` | `oraibhse` | on you |
| 3rd pl. (they) | `orthu` | `orthusan` | on them |

## Forms with Possessive Adjectives

When `ar` is followed by a possessive adjective (`mo`, `do`, `a`, etc.), they remain separate words.

| Preposition + Adjective | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `ar mo` | on my |
| `ar do` | on your |
| `ar a` | on his / on her / on their |
| `ar ár` | on our |
| `ar bhur` | on your (plural) |

### The Special Form arna

The forms `arna` (for `ar a`) and `arnár` (for `ar ár`) are special contractions used primarily with the verbal noun to mean "after" doing something. This usage is a modern replacement for the older preposition `iar`.

- *arna críochnú dom* - after I had finished it

<Note>
You may also see `arna` in the set phrase *an lá arna mhárach* (on the following day). While it can also appear with relative pronouns (`arna` meaning "on which"), this is less common in modern speech, where `ar a` is preferred.
</Note>

## Common Uses of ar

`ar` has a very wide range of meanings and uses beyond simple location.

### Location

- **On top of something**: *ar an mbord* - on the table
- **In a town or city**: *ar an mbaile* - in the town

### Possession of States and Attributes

A very common pattern with `ar` is to express states, conditions, or attributes that someone or something "has on" them.

- **Wearing clothes**: *Tá cóta nua air* - He is wearing a new coat.
- **Appearance and features**: *Tá dath dearg ar an teach* - The house is red (lit. "A red colour is on the house").
- **Parts of objects**: *Tá doirse ar an teach* - The house has doors.
- **Emotions and physical states**: *Tá áthas orm* - I am glad (lit. "Joy is on me"). *Tá tart ort* - You are thirsty. *Tá eagla orainn* - We are afraid.
- **Names**: *Máirtín atá air* - His name is Martin (lit. "Martin is what is on him").

### Causing a State

The verb `cuir` is often used with `ar` to mean "to cause" a state or emotion.

- *Chuir sé fearg orm* - He made me angry.

### Obligation

The structure `tá...ar` is used to express obligation or necessity.

- *Tá orm bainne a ól* - I have to drink milk.

### Responsibility and Authority

- **Responsibility for**: *Tá a báis ort* - You are responsible for her death.
- **Authority over**: *Rí ar Sasana* - King of England.

### Expressing Feelings and Knowledge

When used with `tá...ag`, `ar` indicates the recipient of a feeling or the object of knowledge.

- *Tá grá agam ort* - I love you (lit. "I have love on you").
- *Tá aithne agam ar Cháit* - I know Cáit.
- *Tá buíochas agam ar Cháit* - I am grateful to Cáit.
- *Tá airgead agam ort* - You owe me money (lit. "I have money on you").

### Advantage and Disadvantage

- **To someone's disadvantage**: *Bhris sé an fhuinneog uirthi* - He broke the window on her (i.e., to her detriment).
- **Advantage over**: *Tá bliain agam air* - I am one year older than him (lit. "I have a year on him"). *Bhí deich nóiméad aige orm* - He had a 10-minute lead over me.

### With Verbs

`ar` is the required preposition after many common verbs.

- *féach ar...* - to look at...
- *iarr ar...* - to ask someone...
- *tosaigh ar...* - to begin...
- *glaoigh ar...* - to call someone...

### Price

- *ar phunt* - for a pound
- *Tá punt ar an úll* - The apple costs a pound.

### In Numbers and Arithmetic

- **Counting above 20**: *duine ar fhichid* - 21 people (lit. "one person on twenty")
- **Division**: *a sé ar a trí, sin a dó* - six divided by three is two

### Describing Similarity

`ar` can be used to mean "-coloured" or "-shaped".

- *ar dhath an róis* - rose-coloured
- *ar dhéanamh báid* - boat-shaped
- *ar ghile an tsneachta* - as white as snow

### Probability

- *Tá báisteach air* - It looks like rain.
- *Tá cogadh air* - It looks like there will be a war.

### With Abstract Nouns to Describe a State

- *ar meisce* - drunk
- *ar buile* - mad, furious
- *ar fheabhas* - excellent

### Indicating Activity

With a verbal noun, `ar` can mean "at" or "engaged in".

- *Tá sé ar an obair* - He is at work / working.

### Temporal Expressions

- *ar maidin* - in the morning
- *ar a dó a chlog* - at two o'clock

### Reinforcing an Imperative

`ar` can be added to an imperative to add a sense of encouragement or urgency.

- *Scríobh ort!* - Write on! / Go on, write!

### Asking Questions

- *Cé air a bhfuil tú ag smaoineamh?* - Who are you thinking about? (lit. "On whom are you thinking?")
- *Cad air a bhfuil tú ag féachaint?* - What are you looking at?

<Details summary="Historical note">
The modern preposition `ar` is a coalescence of three distinct prepositions from Old Irish: `ar` ("before, for the sake of"), `for` ("on, over"), and `iar` ("after"). This history explains why `ar` has such a wide range of meanings today, including its use with the verbal noun to mean "after", which directly replaces the function of `iar`.
</Details>
