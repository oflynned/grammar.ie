The prepositions `chun` and `chuig` both mean "to" or "towards". While they have the same core meaning, they are not interchangeable. The main difference is that `chun` requires the genitive case, while `chuig` requires the dative case (which is usually the same as the nominative). Their usage also varies by dialect.

## Choosing Between 'chun' and 'chuig'

As a general rule, `chun` is more common in Munster and in the written standard, especially with locations. `chuig` is more common in Connacht and Ulster, especially when referring to people or events.

- **`chun`** requires the **genitive case**.
- **`chuig`** requires the **dative case**.

<Dialect region="Clare">
  In the dialect of County Clare, `chuig` was also historically followed by the genitive case.
</Dialect>

## Mutations after 'chuig'

The preposition `chuig` causes different initial mutations depending on whether it is followed by the definite article `an`.

- **Without the article**: No mutation.
  - *chuig cailín* - to a girl
- **With the singular article `an`**: The standard rule is **eclipsis** (except on `d` and `t`).
  - *chuig an gcailín* - to the girl
  - *chuig an bhfear* - to the man

<Dialect region="Munster">
  In Munster, `d` and `t` are also eclipsed after `chuig an`.
</Dialect>

<Dialect region="Connacht">
  In Connacht, a t-prefix is added to feminine nouns beginning with `s-`.
  - *chuig an tsúil* - to the eye
</Dialect>

<Dialect region="Ulster">
  In Ulster, **lenition** is used instead of eclipsis.
  - *chuig an chailín* - to the girl
</Dialect>

## Mutations after 'chun'

The preposition `chun` is always followed by the genitive case. The mutation is determined by the genitive form of the article.

- **Without the article**: No mutation, but the noun is in the genitive.
  - *chun buachalla* - to a boy (genitive of `buachaill`)
- **With the masculine singular article `an`**: Lenition.
  - *chun an bhuachalla* - to the boy
- **With the feminine singular article `na`**: No mutation.
  - *chun na mná* - to the woman
- **With the plural article `na`**: Eclipsis.
  - *chun na mban* - to the women

## Conjugated Forms

When used with pronouns, `chuig` and `chun` share a single set of conjugated forms.

| Person | Standard Form | Emphatic Form | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1st Sg. | `chugam` | `chugamsa` | to me |
| 2nd Sg. | `chugat` | `chugatsa` | to you |
| 3rd Sg. (m) | `chuige` | `chuigesean` | to him |
| 3rd Sg. (f) | `chuici` | `chuicise` | to her |
| 1st Pl. | `chugainn` | `chugainne` | to us |
| 2nd Pl. | `chugaibh` | `chugaibhse` | to you |
| 3rd Pl. | `chucu` | `chucusan` | to them |

<Dialect region="Connacht, Ulster">
  In these dialects, `chuig` is often pronounced [huig], which sounds very similar to `ag`. As a result, the preposition `ag` is often used instead of `chuig`. The conjugated forms may appear with an initial `h-` or without it: `hagam` or `agam` instead of `chugam`.
</Dialect>

<Dialect region="Munster">
  In Munster, the `g` or `c` in the conjugated forms is usually lenited (written `gh` or `ch`), except in the 3rd person singular masculine.
  - `chugham` (to me)
  - `chughat` (to you)
  - `chuige` (to him)
  - `chuichi` (to her)
  - `chughainn` (to us)
  - `chughaibh` (to you pl.)
  - `chuchu` (to them)
</Dialect>

## Common Uses and Meanings

### Movement Towards a Place or Person

This is the most common meaning, equivalent to "to".

- *Scríobh chugam.* - Write to me.
- *Tá mé ag dul chuig an dochtúir.* - I am going to the doctor.

It can also be used with place names that take an article.

- *chun na Fraince* - to France

### Figurative Movement or Change

`chun` is often used to describe a change of state.

- *cuir chun báis* - to put to death
- *dul chun olcais* - to turn to bad / to get worse
- *dul chun dearmaid* - to be forgotten (lit. "to go to forgetting")
- *chun bealaigh* - on the way

### Expressing Reason or Purpose

It can mean "for" or "for the sake of".

- *Rinne muid chuig glóire Dé é.* - We did it for the glory of God.

### Indicating Proximity

`chun` can mean "at", "near", or "close to".

- *chun na gaoithe* - close to the wind
- *chun mo láimhe* - near my hand

### Telling Time

`chun` is used to mean "to" or "before" the hour.

- *ceathrú chun a dó* - a quarter to two
- *Tá sé chun a dó.* - It is nearly 2 o'clock.

### Future or Upcoming Events

The phrase `seo chugainn` means "next" or "coming".

- *Dé Luain seo chugainn* - next Monday (lit. "Monday this to-us")

### With a Verbal Noun (Purpose)

Used with a verbal noun, `chun` or `chuig` means "in order to".

- *chun an fhírinne a rá* - in order to tell the truth
- *chun teanga a fhoghlaim* - in order to learn a language

<Note>
When `chun` is used with a verbal noun to express purpose, it is usually **not** followed by the genitive case. The verbal noun remains in its basic form.
</Note>

### With a Verbal Noun (Readiness)

It can also indicate that something is ready or suitable for an action.

- *Tá an féar chun bainte.* - The grass is ready to be cut.
- *fear chun cainte* - a man for talking (i.e., a talkative man)
- *lá chun taistil* - a day for travelling
- *an aimsir chuige* - suitable weather for it

### Idiomatic Phrases

- *chuige* - at all (used as an adverb)
- *Chugat leat!* - Away with you!

## Asking Questions

- **`cé chuige?`** - to whom?
- **`cad chuige?` / `cá chuige?`** - for what purpose? / why?

<Dialect region="Connacht, Ulster">
  In speech, `cad chuige` is often contracted to `tuige`.
</Dialect>

## Pronunciation and History

The pronunciation of `chun` varies significantly by dialect.

<Dialect region="Munster">
  In Munster, `chun` is pronounced [xʊn] or [xʊnʲ], and is sometimes spelled `chuin`.
</Dialect>

<Dialect region="Connacht, Ulster">
  In Connacht and Ulster, the initial `ch-` is often dropped, so it is pronounced [ʊn], [ən], or just [ə]. This is sometimes reflected in the spelling `’un`.
  - *’un an aonaigh* - to the fair

  In Ulster, `chun an` or `chun na` can merge into `’na` [nə].
  - *chun an chnoic* becomes *’na chnoic* - to the hill
  - *chun an bhaile* becomes *’na bhaile* - home / to the town
</Dialect>

<Details summary="Historical note">
  The preposition `chun` derives from Old Irish `dochum`, which came from `tochim`, a verbal noun meaning "to step towards it". This origin is why `chun` requires the genitive case. Before the spelling reform, it was written `chum`.

  The form `chuig` is a back-formation from the 3rd person singular conjugated form `chuige`. These conjugated forms originally belonged to the preposition `go` ("to"), which has a similar meaning, and developed by analogy with the forms of `ag`.
</Details>
