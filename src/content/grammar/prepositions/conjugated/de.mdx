The preposition `de` primarily means "from" or "of". It has a very wide range of uses, from indicating origin and material to forming partitive and appositional phrases. It requires the dative case for the noun that follows it.

<Warning>
In most dialects, the simple prepositions `de` ("from") and `do` ("to") have merged in pronunciation. Both are typically pronounced /də/ or /gə/. This can be confusing for learners, but the context and grammatical mutations they cause usually make the meaning clear. The conjugated forms (e.g., `díom` vs. `dom`) are more distinct.
</Warning>

<Dialect region="All">
The pronunciation of `de` and `do` varies:
- **Connacht (Galway, Mayo):** Both are pronounced /gə/.
- **Ulster & Munster (Donegal, Cork, Kerry):** Both are pronounced /də/.
- **Ring (East Munster):** A distinction is sometimes maintained, with `de` as /d'e/ and `do` as /də/.
</Dialect>

## Initial Mutations

The preposition `de` causes lenition on the noun that follows it.

### Without the Article

When `de` is used without an article, it causes lenition.

*   *de thír* - from a country

### With the Singular Article

When `de` combines with the singular article `an` to form `den`, it also causes lenition.

*   *den Ghearmáin* - from Germany

If the noun begins with 's', a t-prefix is added instead of lenition.

*   *den tsagart* - from the priest

<Dialect region="Munster">
In parts of Munster (e.g., Dingle), eclipsis is sometimes found after `den` instead of lenition.
</Dialect>

## Forms of 'de'

### Before Vowels and 'fh'

Before a word starting with a vowel or `fh-`, `de` becomes `d’`.

*   *d’Úna* - from Úna

<Note>
This form `d'` is identical to the form of the preposition `do` ("to") before a vowel.
</Note>

### With the Article

`de` combines with the singular article `an` but not the plural article `na`.

| Combination | Result |
| :--- | :--- |
| `de` + `an` | `den` |
| `de` + `na` | `de na` |

<Dialect region="All">
In some dialects, you may encounter `des na` or `desna` as an alternative to `de na`.
</Dialect>

### In Questions

To ask "from whom?" or "from what?", `de` is used with interrogative pronouns.

*   *Cé de?* - From whom?
*   *Cad de?* / *Cé de?* / *Cá de?* - From what?

These questions are typically followed by an indirect relative clause.

## With Pronouns (Conjugated Forms)

Like all Irish prepositions, `de` combines with pronouns to form a single word, often called a prepositional pronoun or a conjugated preposition.

| Person | Standard Form | Emphatic Form | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1st sg. (I) | `díom` | `díomsa` | from me |
| 2nd sg. (you) | `díot` | `díotsa` | from you |
| 3rd sg. (he/it) | `de` | `desean` | from him/it |
| 3rd sg. (she/it) | `di` | `dise` | from her/it |
| 1st pl. (we) | `dínn` | `dínne` | from us |
| 2nd pl. (you) | `díbh` | `díbhse` | from you |
| 3rd pl. (they) | `díobh` | `díobhsan` | from them |

*   *Tóg grianghraf díom!* - Take a photo of me!
*   *Tá mé buíoch díot.* - I am grateful to you.

<Note>
These conjugated forms are often lenited in speech, especially after a vowel or a slender consonant, becoming `dhíom`, `dhíot`, etc.
</Note>

<Dialect region="Connacht">
In Connemara, shortened forms are common in speech: `’íom`, `’íot`, `’e`, `’í`, `’ínn`, `’íbh`, `’íobh`.
</Dialect>

<Warning>
Because of the merger in pronunciation between `de` and `do`, you will sometimes hear the conjugated forms of `do` used in place of `de` (e.g., `dom` or `dhom` instead of `díom` or `dhíom`).
</Warning>

## With Possessive Pronouns

`de` can also be followed by a possessive pronoun.

| Person | Form | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1st sg. (my) | `de mo` | from my |
| 2nd sg. (your) | `de do` | from your |
| 3rd sg. (his/her/its) | `dá` | from his/her/its |
| 1st pl. (our) | `dár` | from our |
| 2nd pl. (your) | `de bhur` | from your |
| 3rd pl. (their) | `dá` | from their |

<Dialect region="Munster">
In Munster, `dem` and `ded` are often used instead of `de mo` and `de do`. These forms are sometimes written `dom` and `dod`.
</Dialect>

<Dialect region="Ulster">
In Ulster, `dena` is sometimes used instead of `dá` for "from his/her/their".
</Dialect>

## In Relative Clauses

`de` combines with the relative pronoun `a` and the relative particle `ar`.

| Tense | With Relative Pronoun | With Copula | With Copula (before vowel) | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Present | `dá` | `dar` | `darb` | from which/who |
| Past | `dár` | `dar` | `darbh` | from which/who |

When used with the general relative pronoun, the forms are `dá` (present) and `dár` (past), meaning "from all that".

<Note>
There is some disagreement on the spelling of these forms. The Christian Brothers' Grammar and Ó Dónaill's dictionary distinguish between `dár` (relative pronoun) and `dar` (copula). However, de Bhaldraithe's dictionary writes both as `dar`.
</Note>

## Usage

`de` has a wide variety of uses beyond its core meaning.

### Distance and Separation

It indicates removal, separation, or ceasing an activity. This is similar to `ó`, but `de` focuses on the separation itself, whereas `ó` focuses on the starting point.

*   *Thit sé den chathaoir.* - He fell from the chair.
*   *Stad sé den ól.* - He stopped drinking.
*   *Tóg den bhord é.* - Take it off the table!
*   *Bain dhíot an cóta.* - Take off your coat.
*   *Scaoil díom!* - Let me go!

### Attachment and Continuation

Conversely, `de` can mean attaching something *to* a surface or continuing *with* an action.

*   *Greamaíonn sé den bhalla é.* - He attaches it to the wall.
*   *Leanaim den obair.* - I continue with the work.
*   *Lean de sin!* - Stick with that!

### Relative Position

It is used with adverbs of place to mean "of".

*   *laistiar den teach* - behind the house
*   *taobh abhus den abhainn* - this side of the river

### Origin and Descent

`de` is used to indicate family origin or belonging to a group.

*   *bean de Ghearaltach* - a Fitzgerald woman (a woman of the Fitzgeralds)
*   *Cér díobh thú?* - To which family do you belong?

### Cause

It can indicate the cause or reason for something.

*   *Fuair sé bás den ocras.* - He died of hunger.
*   *de bhrí* - because of
*   *de bharr* - as a result of
*   *Tá mé tuirseach den chaint.* - I am tired of the talking.

### Material and Substance

`de` is used to say what something is made of.

*   *Rinneadh d’adhmad é.* - It was made of wood.
*   *déanta de phrás* - made of brass

### Content and Filling

It indicates what something is filled with.

*   *Líonaim d’uisce é.* - I fill it with water.
*   *lán de chlocha* - full of stones

### Depiction

It means "of" when describing a picture or photograph.

*   *pictiúr de Sheán* - a picture of Seán

### Instrument

`de` can indicate the instrument used for an action, especially a sudden one.

*   *buille de bhata* - a blow with a stick
*   *de léim* - with a jump
*   *de traein* - by train
*   *de bhóthar* - by road

### Apposition

`de` is used to link a noun to another noun that describes or classifies it.

*   *amadán de mhac* - a fool of a son
*   *a leithéid de lá* - such a day
*   *Is fearr d’fhear é ná Seán.* - He is a better man than Seán.
*   *Is sine de chúig bliana é.* - He is five years older.

### Partitive Usage (Subset)

It is used to mean "of" when referring to a part of a larger group, often replacing the partitive genitive.

*   *cuid de na daoine* - some of the people
*   *triúr de na mná* - three of the women
*   *leabhar de chuid Joyce* - a book by Joyce (one of Joyce's books)

<Note>
With plural pronouns, `ag` often replaces `de` in this partitive sense. For example, *go leor acu* ("many of them"), but *go leor de* ("much of it").
</Note>

It can also express belonging or characteristics.

*   *Is den mhúineadh é.* - It is part of good manners.
*   *Tá sé de cheart agam é a dhéanamh.* - I have the right to do it.

### Time and Duration

`de` can be used in time expressions.

*   *ag obair de ló is d’oíche* - working day and night
*   *den chéad uair* - for the first time

### In Relation To

It appears in many fixed phrases to mean "according to" or "concerning".

*   *de réir* - according to
*   *de mo dhóighse* - in my opinion
*   *de mo dheoin* - with my consent / willingly
*   *de m’ainneoin* - against my will

### In Names

`de` appears in many names of Anglo-Norman origin, corresponding to the French "de" or English "the".

*   *de Búrca* - Burke
*   *de Buitléir* - Butler

<Note>
This historical `de` in surnames is not treated as the native Irish preposition. It does not cause lenition and is often pronounced differently.
</Note>
