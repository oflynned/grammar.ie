The preposition `roimh` means "before" and is most commonly used to talk about time. It is usually pronounced /rɪvʲ/. Like many Irish prepositions, it conjugates for person and number when used with pronouns, and it causes initial mutations on the following noun.

## Conjugated Forms

When `roimh` is followed by a pronoun (like "me", "you", or "them"), it combines with the pronoun to create a single word.

| Person | Standard Form | Emphatic Form | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1st sg. | `romham` | `romhamsa` | before me |
| 2nd sg. | `romhat` | `romhatsa` | before you |
| 3rd sg. (m) | `roimhe` | `roimhesean` | before him |
| 3rd sg. (f) | `roimpi` | `roimpise` | before her |
| 1st pl. | `romhainn` | `romhainne` | before us |
| 2nd pl. | `romhaibh` | `romhaibhse` | before you |
| 3rd pl. | `rompu` | `rompusan` | before them |

<Dialect region="Munster">
In Munster, the form `roimis` is sometimes used instead of `roimhe` for "before him".
</Dialect>

## Mutations

The initial mutation caused by `roimh` depends on whether it is followed by the definite article `an`.

### Without the Article

When `roimh` is followed directly by a noun, it causes lenition.

<MutationCard
    title="roimh + noun"
    before="roimh + maidin"
    after="roimh mhaidin"
    rule="Lenites the following noun."
/>

- *Tá sé fuar roimh mhaidin.* - It is cold before morning.

### With the Singular Article

When `roimh` is followed by the singular definite article `an`, it causes eclipsis.

<MutationCard
    title="roimh + an + noun"
    before="roimh + an + bus"
    after="roimh an mbus"
    rule="Eclipses the following noun."
/>

- *Bhí mé ann roimh an mbus.* - I was there before the bus.

There are several dialectal exceptions to this rule:

<Dialect region="Munster">
In Munster, `d` and `t` are also eclipsed after `roimh an`.
- *roimis an ndalta* - before the pupil
</Dialect>

<Dialect region="Connacht">
In Connacht, a `t-`prefix is added to feminine nouns beginning with `s-`.
- *roimh an tsúil* - before the eye
</Dialect>

<Dialect region="Ulster">
In Ulster, lenition is used instead of eclipsis after `roimh an`.
- *roimh an bhus* - before the bus
</Dialect>

## Usage and Examples

### Temporal "before"

The most common use of `roimh` is to mean "before" in time, or "earlier than".

- *Tháinig sé roimh a seacht a chlog.* - He arrived before seven o'clock.
- *Tá mé romhat sa scuaine.* - I am before you in the queue.

### Spatial "before"

Using `roimh` to mean spatially "in front of" is rare in modern Irish. The phrase `os comhair` is much more common for this meaning.

- *Tá an crann os comhair an tí.* - The tree is in front of the house.

### Idiomatic Expressions

`Roimh` appears in several important idioms where its meaning is not "before".

* **`faitíos roimh`** - fear of
  * *Tá faitíos orm roimh an madra.* - I am afraid of the dog.

<Warning>
English speakers often mistakenly use `de` to mean "of" in the context of fear. In Irish, the correct preposition to use with `faitíos` (fear) is `roimh`.
</Warning>

* **`fáilte roimh`** - welcome to
  * *Tá fáilte romhat.* - You are welcome. (Literally: "Welcome is before you.")

* **`tar/téigh roimh`** - to intercept, stop, prevent
  * *Chuaigh sé rompu ag an droichead.* - He intercepted them at the bridge.

## Other Forms and Grammar

### Dialectal Variants

Besides the conjugated forms, the preposition itself has several variants:
- **`roimhe`**: Common in Ulster.
- **`roim`**: Common in Munster.
- **`roimis`**: Often used in Munster before the article, e.g., `roimis an lá`.

### Asking Questions

To ask "before whom?" or "before what?", you use the following structures, which require an indirect relative clause.

- **`cé roimhe...?`** - before whom...?
- **`cad roimhe / céard roimhe / cá roimhe...?`** - before what...?

### Grammatical Case

Historically, `roimh` governs the dative case. In modern standard Irish, the dative form of a noun is almost always identical to the nominative, so this rule has little practical effect for learners.

<Note>
In parts of Munster, an older usage survives where `roimh` is followed by the genitive case in certain phrases.
- *roimis na Nollag* - before Christmas (where `Nollag` is the genitive of `Nollaig`)
</Note>
