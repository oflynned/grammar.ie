## What are Initial Mutations?

Initial mutations are grammatically triggered changes to the first letter of a word. These changes are a fundamental feature of all living Celtic languages.

The two main types of mutation are **lenition** (séimhiú), which typically softens a consonant, and **eclipsis** (urú), which voices or nasalises a consonant. There are also two prefixes, the **h-prefix** and the **t-prefix**, which are added to vowel-initial words in certain contexts.

When a word is not affected by any mutation, it is said to be in its base form, also known as the radical form or, in Irish grammar, `an lomadh` (the stripping).

## Historical Causes of Initial Mutations

The initial mutations of modern Irish are the fossilised remnants of sound changes that happened centuries ago in Old Irish. Words in a spoken sentence were not pronounced in isolation but flowed together without pauses. This process, where the sounds of adjacent words influence each other, is known in linguistics as **sandhi**.

In Old Irish, many words had endings (often vowels or nasals) that have since been lost. However, the effect they had on the beginning of the *next* word was preserved. Over time, these sound changes became a core part of the grammar, used to express relationships between words even after the original sounds that caused them had disappeared.

> **Note on historical forms:** In the explanations below, historical or reconstructed forms are marked with an asterisk, e.g., `*sindos`.

### Lenition

Lenition originally occurred when a consonant was positioned between two vowels, either within a word or across a word boundary. This softened the consonant, making it easier to pronounce and allowing for a smoother flow of speech. Many of the words that now trigger lenition once ended in a vowel, even if they no longer do.

-   **Feminine nouns after the article `an`**: The Old Irish article for a feminine noun was `*sinda`. A noun following it, like `*cloch` (stone), would have its initial `c` between two vowels (`a` and `o`). This caused the `c` to soften. The article was later shortened to `an`, but the lenition it caused remains: `an chloch`.
-   **Masculine nouns in the genitive**: The genitive singular of the masculine article was `*sindi`. It also ended in a vowel and therefore caused lenition on the following noun.
-   **After the possessive adjective `a` (his)**: This word comes from the Old Irish `*esyo`. As a genitive form, it triggered lenition on the following noun.
-   **Adjectives modifying feminine nouns**: Feminine nouns in Old Irish often ended in `-a`. An adjective following such a noun would have its initial consonant between two vowels, causing it to be lenited. This rule was generalised and now applies even to feminine nouns that did not originally end in `-a`.

### Eclipsis and the n-prefix

Eclipsis and the n-prefix (which is a form of eclipsis for vowels) occurred when the preceding word originally ended in a nasal consonant (like `n` or `m`). This final nasal sound merged with the initial sound of the next word.

-   **After certain numbers**: The numbers `seacht` (seven), `naoi` (nine), and `deich` (ten) once ended in a nasal sound, similar to their Latin counterparts *septem*, *novem*, and *decem*. This historical nasal ending is preserved as eclipsis on the following noun, e.g., *seacht mbád* (seven boats). The number `ocht` (eight) now also causes eclipsis by analogy, even though it never had a nasal ending.
-   **After the preposition `i` (in)**: This preposition was originally `*in`. The final `-n` caused eclipsis on the following word. As the eclipsis made the `-n` redundant, the preposition was shortened to just `i`.
-   **After the conjunction `go` (that)**: This word is related to the Latin `cum` (with) and once had a nasal ending that has since been lost, leaving only the eclipsis it triggers.
-   **After the plural possessive adjectives**: `ár` (our), `bhur` (your), and `a` (their) all derive from Old Irish genitive plural forms that ended in a nasal (`*sāron`, `*swāron`, `*esyon`), which is why they cause eclipsis today.
-   **After the genitive plural article `na`**: The Old Irish form was `*sindan`, which ended in a nasal and thus now causes eclipsis: `na gcat` (of the cats).

#### Dialectal Variation after Prepositions

The variation between eclipsis and lenition after a preposition plus the singular article (e.g., `ar an mbord` vs. `ar an bhord`) is also a result of historical forms. In Old Irish, prepositions could be followed by different cases, such as the accusative (for motion towards) or the dative (for location).

-   The **accusative** article was `*sindon`, ending in `-n`. This led to **eclipsis**. This is the standard form in Munster and Connacht: `ar an mbord` (on the table).
-   The **dative** article was `*sindu`, ending in a vowel. This led to **lenition**. This is the standard form in Ulster: `ar an bhord`.

Today, the distinction between accusative and dative after prepositions is gone, but the different mutations remain as a dialectal feature.

### The t-prefix

The t-prefix is a remnant of the `-d-` from the Old Irish masculine article, `*sindos`.

-   **Before masculine nouns starting with a vowel**: The `-os` ending of `*sindos` was lost before consonants, and the `-nd-` sound merged into `-n-`, giving modern `an`. Before a vowel, however, the `-d` of `*sindos` was preserved and eventually became a voiceless `t`. This `t` attached itself to the following vowel-initial noun: `*sindos` > `*sind` > `*sint` > `an t-`. For example, `an t-uisce` (the water).
-   **Before feminine nouns starting with `s`**: The feminine article `*sinda` caused lenition, so `s` became `sh` (pronounced [h]). This `h` sound caused the final `-d` of the article to become a voiceless `t`, which then attached to the `s`. For example, `*sinda súil` > `*sinda shúil` > `*sint shúil` > `an tsúil` (the eye).

> **Note:** The similarity between the Old Irish article `*sindos` and the modern demonstrative pronoun `sin` (that) is not a coincidence. In many languages, including English and German, definite articles evolved from demonstrative pronouns.

### The h-prefix

The h-prefix is often the remnant of a lenited final `-s` from the preceding word. In Old Irish, as in Latin and Greek, many words ended in `-s`. When such a word was followed by a word beginning with a vowel, the `-s` was lenited to an `h` sound.

-   **After the possessive adjective `a` (her)**: The Old Irish form was `*esjas`. The final `-s` became the h-prefix before a following vowel: `a h-ainm` (her name).
-   **After the feminine genitive article `na`**: The Old Irish form was `*sindas`. The final `-s` is preserved as the h-prefix on a following vowel-initial noun: `na h-oíche` (of the night).
-   **After the plural article `na`**: The nominative plural article was also `*sindas`, which explains the h-prefix in cases like `na h-éin` (the birds).
