Lenition, known in Irish as *an séimhiú*, is a sound change that affects the initial consonant of a word in specific grammatical contexts. The word *lenition* comes from Latin *lenire*, meaning "to weaken" or "to soften".

Phonetically, lenition is a form of spirantisation, where a stop consonant (a plosive) is replaced by a fricative (a sound made with continuous airflow) formed at the same place in the mouth. For example, the stop sound `p` becomes the fricative sound `f` (spelled `ph`).

Historically, the lenition of `d` and `t` produced dental fricatives (like the English "th" sounds), but these have since evolved into other sounds in modern Irish.

## Spelling and Pronunciation

Lenition is shown in writing by adding an `h` after the initial consonant. In older texts, a dot (a *ponc séimhithe* or *buailte*) was placed over the consonant instead.

The following consonants can be lenited:

| Consonant | Lenited Form | Broad Pronunciation | Slender Pronunciation |
| :-------- | :----------- | :------------------ | :-------------------- |
| `b`       | `bh`         | [w], [v]            | [v′]                  |
| `c`       | `ch`         | [x]                 | [x′]                  |
| `d`       | `dh`         | [γ]                 | [γ′]                  |
| `f`       | `fh`         | silent              | silent                |
| `g`       | `gh`         | [γ]                 | [γ′]                  |
| `m`       | `mh`         | [w], [v]            | [v′]                  |
| `p`       | `ph`         | [f]                 | [f′]                  |
| `s`       | `sh`         | [h]                 | [h], [x′]             |
| `t`       | `th`         | [h]                 | [h], [x′]             |

The consonants `l`, `n`, and `r` are not lenited in the standard written language, although their pronunciation can change in some dialects in contexts where lenition would be expected. The letters `h`, `j`, `v`, `w`, and `z` are never lenited.

### Pronunciation Notes

*   **`gh` / `dh`**: Broad `gh` and `dh` are pronounced as a voiced velar fricative [γ], similar to a soft, gargled 'g'. Slender `gh` and `dh` are pronounced like the 'y' in "yes" [j]. Historically, `dh` was pronounced like the 'th' in "that" [ð].
*   **`th`**: This was once pronounced like the 'th' in "thing" [θ], but is now pronounced as [h].
*   **`sh` / `th`**: Both are usually pronounced [h]. However, when a slender `sh` or `th` comes before a long `a`, `o`, or `u`, it is often pronounced [x′] (like the `ch` in German *ich*).
    *   *a Sheáin* [ə x′a:n′]
    *   *mo sheol* [mə x′o:l]
*   **`bh` / `mh`**: These are pronounced as `v` or `w`. Broadly, `bh` and `mh` are often a `w` sound, especially at the start of a word, while the `v` sound is more common in Munster. Slender `bh` and `mh` are always a `v` sound. `mh` can also cause nasalisation of the following vowel.
*   **`fh`**: The lenition of `f` results in `fh`, which is completely silent.
*   **`sh`**: The lenition of `s` results in `sh`, which is pronounced as `h`.

## Rules and Exceptions

### Blocking Lenition (An Cadad)

In certain situations, lenition is blocked if the preceding word ends in a consonant that is formed in the same part of the mouth as the consonant that would be lenited. This rule is known as *an cadad*. It is most strictly applied to the coronal consonants, often called the "dentals rule".

**Coronals: `d`, `n`, `t`, `l`, `s`**
A word beginning with `d`, `t`, or `s` is not lenited if the preceding word ends in `d`, `n`, `t`, `l`, or `s`.
*   *don doras* (to the door), not `*don dhoras`
*   *an teach sin* (that house), not `*an teach shin`

This rule does not apply to attributive adjectives or nouns in the definite genitive in the standard language.
*   *bean dheas* (a nice woman)

**Labials: `b`, `m`, `p`**
Lenition of `b`, `m`, or `p` is often blocked after a word ending in `m`.
*   *um bosca* (around a box)

In Munster, this is common after combined prepositions and possessive pronouns like `im` (`i mo`), `dom` (`do mo`), and `lem` (`le mo`).
*   *im póca* (in my pocket) (Standard: *i mo phóca*)

**Velars: `c`, `g`**
Lenition of `c` and `g` is always blocked in surnames after `Mhic` and `Nic`.
*   *Bríd Nic Cárthaigh* (Bridget MacCarthy)
*   *teach Sheáin Mhic Gabhann* (Seán MacGowan's house)

### Other Spelling and Sound Rules

*   **`s` to `ts`**: After the article `an`, lenition of `s` is replaced by the t-prefix, resulting in `ts-`. This happens to feminine nouns in the nominative and masculine nouns in the genitive.
*   **Unlenited Clusters**: Consonant clusters beginning with `s` are never lenited: `sc-`, `sp-`, `st-`, `sm-`, `sf-`, `spl-`, `spr-`, `scl-`, `scr-`, `str-`.
*   **`fh-` + Vowel**: A word beginning with `fh` followed by a vowel is treated as if it begins with a vowel.
    *   *m’fhear céile* (my husband), not `*mo fhear céile`
    *   *D’fhoghlaim mé Gaeilge.* (I learned Irish.)
*   **`gan` + `f`**: The preposition `gan` (without) never lenites a following `f`.
    *   *gan fear* (without a man)
*   **Clarity**: Lenition may be omitted if it would make a word unrecognisable or cause confusion. For example, `fuisce` (whiskey) is not lenited to avoid it sounding like `uisce` (water).
    *   *droch-fuisce* (bad whiskey) vs. *droch-uisce* (bad water)
*   **`l`, `n`, `r`**: While not marked in writing, the pronunciation of these consonants can change from a "tense" to a "lax" version in some dialects where lenition would be expected.

## When to Use Lenition

Lenition is triggered in many different grammatical environments.

### Nouns

*   After the vocative particle `a`: *a Cháit!* (Kate!), *a Sheáin!* (John!)
*   After the article `an` with a feminine singular noun in the nominative case: *an bhean* (the woman).
*   After the article `an` with a masculine singular noun in the genitive case: *hata an fhir* (the man's hat).
*   After a feminine noun when used as an indefinite genitive attribute: *praghas cháis* (the price of cheese).
*   After any noun when a proper noun is used as a genitive attribute: *muintir Cháit* (Kate's family).
*   In the second part of many compound nouns: *scian phóca* (pocket knife).
*   After the numbers one to six (`aon`, `dó`, `trí`, `ceithre`, `cúig`, `sé`): *aon bhád amháin* (one boat), *dhá chat* (two cats).
*   After `beirt` (two people): *beirt pháistí* (two children).
*   After `an chéad` (the first): *an chéad duine* (the first person).
*   After the possessive pronouns `mo` (my), `do` (your), and `a` (his): *mo chara* (my friend), *do dheirfiúr* (your sister), *a thigh* (his house).
*   After many simple prepositions: `ar`, `de`, `do`, `faoi`, `ó`, `mar`, `roimh`, `trí`, `thar`.
*   After the combined prepositions `don` (to the) and `den` (of the/from the).
*   In surnames after `Ní`, `Uí`, `Mhic`, and `Nic`.

### Verbs

*   In the past tense (preterite), imperfect tense, and conditional mood: *bhí sé* (he was), *cheannaigh mé* (I bought), *d’ólfadh sé* (he would drink).
*   After the negative particle `ní`: *ní thuigim* (I don't understand).
*   After past tense particles ending in `-r`: `ar`, `gur`, `nár`, `níor`.
*   After the direct relative particle `a`: *an fear a cheannaigh an teach* (the man who bought the house).
*   After `má` (if): *má thagann sé* (if he comes).
*   Verbal nouns after the preposition `a` (to): *ag iarraidh a dhul abhaile* (trying to go home).

### Adjectives

*   After a feminine singular noun in the nominative case: *an bhean mhór* (the big woman).
*   After a masculine singular noun in the genitive case: *hata an fhir mhóir* (the big man's hat).
*   After a plural noun ending in a slender consonant: *na fir mhóra* (the big men).
*   After numbers two to six when used predicatively: *is trí mheasa é* (it is three times as bad).
*   After a noun following numbers 2-19: *seacht mbád mhóra* (seven big boats).

### Other Contexts

*   After many prefixes: `an-`, `droch-`, `fíor-`, `sean-`.
    *   *an-mhaith* (very good), *seancharr* (old car).
*   In the second part of a compound word.
*   After past/conditional forms of the copula (`ba`): *ba mhaith liom* (I would like).
*   The word `déag` (teen) is lenited in certain contexts: *a dó dhéag* (twelve).
*   Some words are almost always found in their lenited form: `dhá` (two), `bhur` (your [pl.]), `cheana` (already), `choíche` (ever/never), `chomh` (as/so), `chun` (to).

## Historical and Internal Lenition

Lenition can also appear inside or at the end of a word, such as the `th` in `máthair` (mother). This is not the result of a grammatical rule but is a feature of the word's historical development. The spelling reflects an older pronunciation, and these internal lenited consonants are often silent or have merged into vowel sounds in the modern language.

## Note on Terminology: Lenition vs. Aspiration

Lenition is sometimes incorrectly called "aspiration". Aspiration is a phonetic term for a small puff of air that accompanies a consonant sound, like the 'p' in the English word "pit". Irish lenition is a more significant change where one type of consonant (a plosive) becomes another (a fricative). For example, lenited `p` (`ph`) is pronounced `f`, which is a completely different sound, not just an "aspirated p". Therefore, "lenition" is the more accurate term.
