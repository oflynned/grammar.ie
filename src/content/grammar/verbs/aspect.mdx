When you use the progressive aspect to describe an action being done to someone (e.g., "He is hitting me"), the construction involves a preposition, a possessive pronoun, and the verbal noun. The exact form of this construction varies significantly across the dialects of Irish and the other Goidelic languages. This page compares the different forms you will encounter.

## Comparison of Forms

The following table shows the forms used with the verbal noun `bualadh` (striking) across Irish dialects, Scottish Gaelic, and Manx. The structure is `Tá sé` ("He is") + [form] + `bualadh` ("striking").

| Person | Standard Irish | Munster | Connacht | Ulster | Scottish Gaelic | Manx |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **me** | `do mo bhualadh` | `am bualadh` | `do mo bhualadh` | `ag mo bhualadh` | `gam bhualadh` | `dy my vuilley` / `builley mee` |
| **you (sg)** | `do do bhualadh` | `ad bhualadh` | `do do bhualadh` | `ag do bhualadh` | `gad bhualadh` | `dy dty vuilley` / `builley oo` |
| **him** | `á bhualadh` | `á bhualadh` | `dhá bhualadh` | `á bhualadh` | `ga bhualadh` | `dy vuilley (eh)` / `builley eh` |
| **her** | `á bualadh` | `á bualadh` | `dhá bualadh` | `á bualadh` | `ga bualadh` | `dy builley` / `builley ee` |
| **us** | `dár mbualadh` | `ár mbualadh` | `dhár mbualadh` | `ag ár mbualadh` | `gar bualadh` | `dyn muilley (shin)` / `builley shin` |
| **you (pl)** | `do bhur mbualadh` | `bhur mbualadh` | `dho’ur mbualadh` | `ag mur mbualadh` | `gur bualadh` | `dyn muilley (shiu)` / `builley shiu` |
| **them** | `á mbualadh` | `á mbualadh` | `dhá mbualadh` | `á mbualadh` | `gam bualadh` | `dyn muilley (ad)` / `builley ad` |

## Explanations and Variations

The variation between forms using `do` (e.g., `do mo`) and `ag` (e.g., `ag mo`) is a key feature of this construction. Historically, different prepositions were used, but over time their pronunciations merged, leading to the current mix of forms across the dialects. In many areas, the preposition `do` came to be pronounced `[gə]`, making it sound very similar to `ag`. This phonetic convergence led to a blending of the two prepositions in this context.

<Dialect region="Connacht">
In Connemara, the preposition `do` is pronounced `[gə]`, so `do mo` is `[gə mə]` and `do do` is `[gə də]`. The forms `dhá`, `dhár`, and `dho’ur` are all typically pronounced `[ɣɑː]`, though pronunciations like `[dɑː]`, `[gɑː]`, or `[ɑː]` can also be heard.
</Dialect>

<Dialect region="Ulster">
In Ulster Irish, the 'g' in `ag` is often silent before a consonant. In speech, the entire preposition `ag` may be omitted before the possessive pronoun.
</Dialect>

<Note>
The newer Manx forms, such as *Ta eh builley mee* ("He is striking me"), are structurally different from the other Goidelic languages. Instead of using a preposition and possessive pronoun before the verbal noun, the object pronoun is simply placed after it. The older Manx construction is still used, particularly in relative clauses.
</Note>

<Details summary="Historical form: agá">
In older texts, you may encounter the form `agá` or `'ghá`. This was a contraction of the preposition `ag` and the possessive pronoun `a` ("his/her/their"), which is now written as `á`.
</Details>

<Details summary="The 'active' vs 'passive' progressive debate">
Some older grammars attempted to draw a distinction between an "active progressive" using `ag` and a "passive progressive" using `do`. This prescriptive rule suggested that `ghá` (from `ag + a`) should be used for active meanings and `dhá` (from `do + a`) for passive meanings.

-   *Tá sé ghá dhéanamh.* - He is doing it. (Active)
-   *Tá sé dhá dhéanamh.* - It is being done. (Passive)

This distinction is not reflected in modern spoken Irish and is no longer considered a valid grammatical rule. The forms are used interchangeably depending on the dialect.
</Details>
