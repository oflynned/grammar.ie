Verbal particles are small words that come directly before a verb in Irish. They are essential for forming questions, negative statements, and other grammatical constructions. Most particles cause an initial mutation—either lenition or eclipsis—on the verb that follows.

A key pattern to recognise is that many particles add an `-r` to their end when used with the past tense. For example, the interrogative particle `an` becomes `ar` in the past. However, this rule does not apply to most irregular verbs, which often have their own special dependent forms that are used instead.

## The Negative Particle

Negative particles are used to negate a verb, equivalent to "not" or "no" in English. The standard particle is `ní`, but `cha` is used in Ulster Irish.

### `ní / níor`

The most common negative particle is `ní`. Its past tense form is `níor`.

| Context | Particle | Mutation | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Present, Future, etc. | `ní` | Lenition | *Ní ólfaidh tú fuisce arís.* - You will not drink whiskey again. |
| Past | `níor` | Lenition | *Níor ól tú fuisce.* - You did not drink any whiskey. |

<Dialect region="Munster">
In the Dingle peninsula, `ní` is often shortened to `n’` before the verbs `feadair` (to know), `féad` (to be able), and `feacaigh` (saw).

*   *n’fheadar* - I don't know.
*   *n’fhéadfainn* - I couldn't.
*   *n’fheacaigh sé* - He didn't see.

Additionally, in this dialect, a `dh’`-prefix is often added to verbs beginning with a vowel or `fh-` after leniting particles like `ní` and `níor`.

*   *Ní dh’aontaím leis sin.* - I do not agree with that. (Standard: *Ní aontaím leis sin.*)
</Dialect>

### `cha / char`

In Ulster Irish, the particle `cha` is used for negation. Its past tense form is `char`.

| Context | Particle | Mutation | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Before b, c, f, g, m, p, s | `cha` | Lenition | *Cha chreideann sé é.* - He doesn't believe it. |
| Before d, t | `cha` | Eclipsis | *Cha ndéanaim a leithéid.* - I don't do such a thing. |
| Before vowels, fh | `chan` | Lenition | *Chan ólann tú fuisce arís.* - You will not drink whiskey again. |
| Past | `char` | Lenition | *Char ól tú fuisce.* - You did not drink any whiskey. |

<Dialect region="Ulster">
The particle `cha` (often pronounced like `ha`) is used in Ulster Irish, sometimes instead of `ní` and sometimes alongside it for emphasis.

*   Instead of the future tense, the habitual present is used after `cha/chan`. For example, *Chan ólann tú fuisce arís* means "You will not drink whiskey again."
*   The mutation rules after `cha` can vary. While the standard is lenition, some consonants may be eclipsed (e.g., *cha mbíonn sé* as well as *cha bhíonn sé* for "he is not"), and others may not be mutated at all.
*   When used with the copula, `chan` appears before nouns and pronouns without causing mutation (*Chan fear é* - He is not a man), while `cha` is sometimes used before adjectives (*Cha maith liom é* - I don't like it).
</Dialect>

<Details summary="Historical note">
The particle `cha` derives from the Old Irish `ní con`, which became `nocha` and was later shortened to `cha`. It is also the standard negative particle in Scottish Gaelic.
</Details>

## The Prohibitive Particle

The prohibitive particle `ná` is used to form negative commands (the prohibitive mood). It means "don't".

| Context | Particle | Mutation | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Before a consonant | `ná` | None | *Ná déan é!* - Don't do it! |
| Before a vowel | `ná` | h-prefix | *Ná hól fuisce!* - Don't drink whiskey! |

## The Interrogative Particle

Interrogative particles are placed at the beginning of a sentence to form a yes/no question.

### Positive Questions: `an / ar`

To ask a positive question, use `an`. Its past tense form is `ar`.

| Tense | Particle | Mutation | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Present, Future, etc. | `an` | Eclipsis | *An ólfá fuisce?* - Would you drink whiskey? |
| Past | `ar` | Lenition | *Ar ól tú fuisce?* - Did you drink whiskey? |

<Dialect region="Munster">
In the Dingle peninsula, the `-r` is often dropped from past tense particles in speech.
*`A(r) thuig tú?`* - Did you understand?
</Dialect>

### Negative Questions: `nach / nár`

To ask a negative question ("Don't you...?", "Isn't it...?"), use `nach`. Its past tense form is `nár`.

| Tense | Particle | Mutation | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Present, Future, etc. | `nach` | Eclipsis | *Nach n-ólann tú fuisce?* - Don't you drink whiskey? |
| Past | `nár` | Lenition | *Nár ól tú fuisce?* - Didn't you drink whiskey? |

<Dialect region="Munster">
In Munster Irish, `ná` is used instead of `nach` for negative questions and in other contexts (such as negative relative clauses or after the conjunction `go`). Its past tense form is still `nár`.

| Tense | Particle | Mutation | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Present etc. (consonant) | `ná` | None | *Ná fuil sé anso?* - Isn't he here? |
| Present etc. (vowel) | `ná` | h-prefix | *Ná hólair fuisce?* - Don't you drink whiskey? |
| Past | `nár` | Lenition | *Nár ólais fuisce?* - Didn't you drink whiskey? |

<Note>
The particle `ná` is unique because it takes the dependent form of a verb but causes no initial mutation. This is why you see `ná fuil` instead of the standard `nach bhfuil`. It is the only context where the dependent form `fuil` appears without a mutation.
</Note>
</Dialect>

## The Past Particle `do / d'`

The particle `do` was historically used to mark the past, imperfect, and conditional tenses. In modern standard Irish, it is only consistently used as `d'` before a vowel or `fh`.

| Context | Particle | Mutation | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Before consonants | (`do`) | Lenition | *Thuig sé an scéal.* - He understood the story. |
| Before vowels and `fh` | `d'` | Lenition | *D’ól tú fuisce.* - You drank whiskey. |

The full form `do` before a consonant is now considered archaic or dialectal, though it is still common in Munster Irish. In standard Irish, only the lenition remains.

*   *Do thuig sé an scéal.* (Munster)
*   *Thuig sé an scéal.* (Standard)

However, `d'` is always used before vowels and `fh` in all dialects and the standard language. This also applies after conjunctions that take an independent verb form, such as `má`.

*   *D’fhan tú liom.* - You waited for me.
*   *Má d’ól tú é.* - If you drank it.

<Note>
The particle `do` does not lenite the autonomous (impersonal) form of the verb in the past tense. Before a vowel, an h-prefix is sometimes added: `do briseadh` (it was broken), `do himríodh` or `imríodh` (it was played).
</Note>

<Dialect region="Munster">
In Munster, `do` is often lenited to `dho` in speech: *Dho thuig sé*. It can also be doubled before a vowel: *Do dh’olas* (I drank).
</Dialect>

## The Perfect Particle `ro`

The particle `ro` is not used on its own in modern Irish, but its historical presence explains the `-r` ending on many other particles in the past tense. It merged with other particles to create their past tense forms.

*   `ní` + `ro` → `níor`
*   `an` + `ro` → `ar`
*   `go` + `ro` → `gur`
*   `nach` + `ro` → `nár`

This particle is also the reason some irregular verbs have different dependent forms in the past, such as `raibh` (from `ro` + `bhí`).

<Details summary="Historical note">
In Old Irish, `ro` originally added a perfective meaning (indicating a completed action) in the past tense. In other tenses, it could indicate potential or be used in wishes, a function that survives in the optative particle `nár`.
</Details>

## The Relative Particle

The relative particle introduces a relative clause (a clause that describes a noun, like "the man **who**..."). Irish distinguishes between direct and indirect relative clauses.

### Direct Relative Particle

The direct relative particle `a` is used when the noun being described is the subject or direct object of the relative clause.

| Tense | Polarity | Particle | Mutation | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Present etc. | Positive | `a` | Lenition | *an fear a ólann fuisce* - the man who drinks whiskey |
| Past | Positive | `a` | Lenition | *an fear a d’ól fuisce* - the man who drank whiskey |
| Present etc. | Negative | `nach` | Eclipsis | *an fear nach n-ólann fuisce* - the man who does not drink whiskey |
| Past | Negative | `nár` | Lenition | *an fear nár ól fuisce* - the man who did not drink whiskey |

<Note>
When used with the verb `tá`, the relative particle `a` combines with it to form `atá`.
*an fear atá anseo* - the man who is here
</Note>

<Dialect region="Munster">
In older texts and some Munster dialects, `do` can be used instead of the positive relative particle `a`.
*an fear d’ólas* - the man who drinks
</Dialect>

### Indirect Relative Particle

The indirect relative particle is used when the noun being described has a more complex grammatical relationship, such as being the object of a preposition within the clause ("the house **in which**...").

| Tense | Polarity | Particle | Mutation | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Present etc. | Positive | `a` | Eclipsis | *an teach a n-ólann tú fuisce ann* - the house in which you drink whiskey |
| Past | Positive | `ar` | Lenition | *an teach ar ól tú fuisce ann* - the house in which you drank whiskey |
| Present etc. | Negative | `nach` | Eclipsis | *an teach nach n-ólann tú fuisce ann* - the house in which you do not drink whiskey |
| Past | Negative | `nár` | Lenition | *an teach nár ól tú fuisce ann* - the house in which you did not drink whiskey |

<Note>
When a preposition ending in a vowel comes before the relative particle, they often combine. For example, `i` + `ar` becomes `inar`.
*an teach inar ól tú fuisce* - the house in which you drank whiskey
</Note>

<Dialect region="Munster">
In Munster Irish, the conjunctions `go` and `gur` are often used instead of the positive indirect relative particles `a` and `ar`.
</Dialect>

## The Optative Particle

The optative particle is used to express wishes, hopes, blessings, or curses.

| Polarity | Particle | Mutation | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Positive | `go` | Eclipsis | *Go n-éirí an bóthar leat.* - May the road rise with you. |
| Negative | `nár` | Lenition | *Nár fheicimid arís é.* - May we never see him again! |

This particle is essentially the same as the conjunction `go` (that), so the examples can be literally understood as "That the road may rise with you!"

<Note>
The negative particle used with the subjunctive of `bí` (`raibh`) is `ná`, not `nár`. This is because `raibh` historically already contains the particle `ro` (which became the `-r` ending).
*`Ná raibh maith agat.`* - No thanks to you.
</Note>

## Conjunctions That Act Like Particles

Several conjunctions behave like verbal particles, including changing their form by adding `-r` in the past tense.

### `go / gur` (that)

| Tense | Polarity | Particle | Mutation | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Present etc. | Positive | `go` | Eclipsis | *go n-ólann tú fuisce* - that you drink whiskey |
| Past | Positive | `gur` | Lenition | *gur ól tú fuisce* - that you drank whiskey |
| Present etc. | Negative | `nach` | Eclipsis | *nach n-ólann tú fuisce* - that you do not drink whiskey |
| Past | Negative | `nár` | Lenition | *nár ól tú fuisce* - that you did not drink whiskey |

<Dialect region="Munster">
In Munster, `ná` is used instead of `nach`. After a negative main clause, `ná go` (past: `ná gur`) is often used.
*Ní deirim ná go n-ólann tú fuisce.* - I do not say that you do not drink whiskey.
</Dialect>

### `sula / sular` (before)

| Tense | Particle | Mutation | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Present, Future, etc. | `sula` | Eclipsis | *Beidh sé anseo sula dtiocfaidh mé.* - He will be here before I come. |
| Past | `sular` | Lenition | *Bhí sé anseo sular tháinig mé.* - He was here before I came. |

<Dialect>
Regional variants include `sara / sarar` (Munster).
</Dialect>

### `mura / murar` (if not, unless)

| Tense | Particle | Mutation | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Present, Future, etc. | `mura` | Eclipsis | *Mura bhfeiceann sé é...* - If he does not see it... |
| Past | `murar` | Lenition | *Murar bhuail sé leis...* - If he did not meet him... |

<Dialect>
Regional variants include `mara / marar` (Connemara) and `muna / munar`.
</Dialect>

### `ach a / ach ar` (as soon as)

| Tense | Particle | Mutation | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Present, Future, etc. | `ach a` | Eclipsis | *Beidh sé anseo ach a dtiocfaidh mé.* - He will be here as soon as I come. |
| Past | `ach ar` | Lenition | *Bhí sé anseo ach ar tháinig mé.* - He was here as soon as I came. |

## The Locative Interrogative Adverb `cá`

The question word `cá` (where) behaves like a verbal particle, combining with the indirect relative particle to form `cár` in the past tense.

| Tense | Particle | Mutation | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Present, Future, etc. | `cá` | Eclipsis | *Cá gcuirfidh tú é?* - Where will you put it? |
| Past | `cár` | Lenition | *Cár chuir tú é?* - Where did you put it? |
