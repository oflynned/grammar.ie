The second conjugation in Irish covers most regular verbs with two or more syllables. These verbs are often called "weak verbs" and are characterised by endings that contain a long `í` or `aí` vowel in the present and past tenses, and an `ó` or `eo` in the future and conditional.

## Verbs in the Second Conjugation

The second conjugation includes several distinct groups of verbs.

### Polysyllabic Verbs Ending in `-igh`

This is the largest group and includes all polysyllabic verbs ending in `-igh`. The final consonant cluster determines whether the endings are "slender" (front vowel) or "broad" (back vowel).

*   `coinnigh` (to hold)
*   `ceannaigh` (to buy)
*   `fiafraigh` (to ask)
*   `salaigh` (to soil)

<Dialect region="Munster">
  In Munster, a few of these verbs may end in `-imh` in speech, such as `coinnimh` for the standard `coinnigh`.
</Dialect>

<Dialect region="Ulster">
  In some Ulster dialects, a few verbs in this category end in `-óigh`, such as `éalóigh` (to flee) or `fostóigh` (to rent), which are `éalaigh` and `fostaigh` in the standard language.
</Dialect>

### Syncopated Polysyllabic Verbs

This group includes polysyllabic verbs ending in `-il`, `-in`, `-ir`, and sometimes `-is` (after `l`, `n`, or `r`). In most conjugated forms, the second syllable of the verb stem is dropped (a process called syncope) before the ending is added.

*   `oscail` (to open) becomes `oscl-`
*   `seachain` (to avoid) becomes `seachn-`
*   `imir` (to play) becomes `imr-`
*   `inis` (to tell) becomes `ins-`

The imperative 2nd person singular and the past tense 3rd person singular retain the full stem (e.g., `imir`, `d'imir sé`).

<Note>
  Not all verbs with these endings are syncopated. Some undergo a different process where the final consonant becomes broad. For example:
  *   *freastail* (to serve) -> *freastalaíonn sé*
  *   *taistil* (to travel) -> *taistealaíonn sé*
  *   *aithris* (to recite) -> *aithrisíonn sé*

  A few syncopated verbs, such as `achair` (to implore), belong to the first conjugation instead (e.g., `achrann sé`, `achairfidh sé`).
</Note>

<Details summary="Historical note on `éirigh`">
  The verb `éirigh` (to rise) is now treated as a standard second conjugation verb ending in `-igh` (e.g., `éiríonn sé`). Historically, however, it was a syncopated verb (`éirgh-`), as seen in older spellings like `éirgheann sé`. It represents a link between the two groups.
</Details>

### Other Polysyllabic Verbs

A smaller group of polysyllabic verbs, often ending in `-im` or `-ing`, also belong to the second conjugation. In the standard language, these verbs do not undergo syncope.

*   `foghlaim` (to learn)
*   `tarraing` (to pull)
*   `fulaing` (to endure)

### Verbs with an Epenthetic Vowel

The verb `lorg` (to search) belongs to the second conjugation because it is pronounced with two syllables (`[lorəg]`) due to an epenthetic vowel.

<Note>
  Other verbs with an epenthetic vowel, such as `dearg` (to redden), `searg` (to wither), and `tairg` (to offer), belong to the first conjugation in the standard language. However, their conjugation can vary across dialects.
</Note>

## Verbs Not in the Second Conjugation

It is important to distinguish second conjugation verbs from two other groups:

*   **Verbs ending in `-áil`**: These are polysyllabic but belong to the first conjugation.
*   **Monosyllabic verbs**: All single-syllable verbs belong to the first conjugation, even those ending in `-igh` like `nigh` (to wash) or `suigh` (to sit).

## Conjugation Patterns

The endings for second conjugation verbs depend on whether the final consonant of the stem is slender (palatalised) or broad (velarised).

<Note>
  The endings `-(a)igh` in the past tense (3rd person singular) and imperative (2nd person singular) only apply to verbs that end in `-igh`. For other verbs like `imir` or `foghlaim`, the stem itself is used for these forms.
</Note>
