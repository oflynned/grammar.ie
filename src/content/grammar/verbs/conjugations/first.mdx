Irish regular verbs are divided into two main groups, known as conjugations. The first conjugation is the larger of the two and includes most verbs with a one-syllable stem, as well as some specific types of multi-syllable verbs.

This page outlines which verbs belong to this group and how to form their various tenses and moods based on whether their stem ends in a broad or slender consonant.

## Verb Types in the First Conjugation

The first conjugation includes several distinct categories of verbs.

### Monosyllabic Verbs

This is the largest group and includes all verbs with a one-syllable stem ending in either a broad or slender consonant.
- `glan` (to clean)
- `tuig` (to understand)
- `dún` (to close)
- `bris` (to break)

### Monosyllabic Verbs Ending in -igh

Verbs with a single syllable that end in `-igh` also belong to this conjugation.
- `nigh` (to wash)
- `léigh` (to read)
- `dóigh` (to burn)

### Polysyllabic Verbs Ending in -áil

Multi-syllable verbs ending in `-áil` are part of the first conjugation. When adding endings, the final slender consonant of the stem often becomes broad (a process called depalatalisation). This happens before endings beginning with `-f-` but not always before those beginning with `-t-`.

- *sabhálann sé* - he saves (from `sabháil`, to save)
- *sabháiltear* - one saves
- *sabhálfar* - one will save

### Other Polysyllabic Verbs

Several other types of multi-syllable verbs fall into this category, often with specific rules about depalatalisation.

- **Verbs with a long vowel in the final syllable** (e.g., `-áin`, `-óil`, `-úir`) usually depalatalise their final consonant before all endings.
  - *taispeánann sé* - he shows (from `taispeáin`)
  - *tionólann sé* - he gathers (from `tionóil`)

- Some verbs in this group **do not depalatalise**.
  - *tiomáineann sé* - he drives (from `tiomáin`)

- Some verbs **already have a broad stem ending**.
  - *gearánaim* - I complain (from `gearán`)

- **Verbs with a short vowel in the final syllable** and a final consonant other than `l`, `n`, or `r` often depalatalise.
  - *adhlacann sé* - he buries (from `adhlaic`)

- Some verbs in this group **remain slender**.
  - *comhaireann sé* - he calculates (from `comhair`)

### Syncopated Verbs

A few polysyllabic verbs undergo syncope, where a vowel is dropped from the middle of the word when an ending is added.

- Verbs like `deighil` (to separate) always syncopate, and the endings added are slender.
  - *deighleann tú* - you separate
  - *deighlfidh tú* - you will separate
  - *deighltear* - one separates

- Verbs like `adhair` (to adore) syncopate with depalatalisation, and the endings added are broad.
  - *adhrann tú* - you adore
  - *adhrfaidh tú* - you will adore
  - *adhrtar* - one adores

- The modern verbs `siúil` (to walk) and `fón` (to serve) follow this pattern due to their historical spellings (`siubhail`, `fóghain`), which were syncopated.
  - *siúlann sé* - he walks
  - *siúlfaidh sé* - he will walk
  - *siúltar* - one walks
  - *fónann sé* - he serves
  - *fónfaidh sé* - he will serve
  - *fóntar* - one serves

<Details summary="Historical and Phonetic Note">
  In contrast to verbs of the Second Conjugation, many of these syncopated verbs are disyllabic only in their historical spelling. In modern spoken Irish, the middle consonants became silent or merged with vowels to form diphthongs, making the verbs effectively monosyllabic. For example, `adhair` is pronounced [air′] and `deighil` is [d′ail′]. The syncope, therefore, primarily affects the written form, while the main audible change is the quality (broad or slender) of the final consonant.
</Details>

### The Verb `fuirsigh`

The polysyllabic verb `fuirsigh` (to harrow, hurry) is a special case. It only uses the `-igh` ending in the past tense and the 2nd person singular imperative; otherwise, it conjugates like a verb with a broad ending.
- *fuirseann sé* - he hurries
- *fuirsfidh sé* - he will hurry
- *d’fhuirsigh sé* - he hurried
- *fuirstear* - one hurries

### Compound Verbs

Verbs formed by adding a prefix to a first-conjugation verb generally remain in the first conjugation.
- *forghabhann sé* - he seizes (from `for-` + `gabh`)
- *ruadhónn sé* - he singes (from `rua-` + `dóigh`)

<Note>
When the irregular verb `déan` (to do, make) is part of a compound, the resulting verb becomes regular and follows first-conjugation patterns. For example, `comhdhéan` (to form) conjugates regularly: *chomhdhéan sé* (he formed).
</Note>

## Conjugation Patterns

The endings for first-conjugation verbs depend on whether the final consonant of the stem is broad (followed by a, o, u) or slender (followed by e, i).

<Note>
In modern Irish, some verb forms are "synthetic", meaning the ending includes the person (e.g., `glanaim`, "I clean"). Others are "analytic", using a general verb form followed by a pronoun (e.g., `glanann tú`, "you clean"). In the present tense, it is standard to use analytic forms for the second and third persons.
</Note>

### Verbs with Broad Endings

These verbs have a stem ending in a broad consonant, like `glan`.

#### Indicative Mood Endings (Broad)

| Person | Present | Preterite | Imperfect | Future |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1st Sg. | `-aim` | `-as` | `-ainn` | `-fad` |
| 2nd Sg. | `-ann` tú | `-ais` | `-tá` | `-faidh` tú |
| 3rd Sg. | `-ann` sé | sé | `-adh` sé | `-faidh` sé |
| 1st Pl. | `-aimid` | `-amar` | `-aimis` | `-faimid` |
| 2nd Pl. | `-ann` sibh | `-abhair` | `-adh` sibh | `-faidh` sibh |
| 3rd Pl. | `-ann` siad | `-adar` | `-aidís` | `-faidh` siad |
| Autonomous | `-tar` | `-adh` | `-taí` | `-far` |
| Relative | `-as` | — | — | `-fas` |

| Person | Conditional | Imperative | Present Subjunctive | Past Subjunctive |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1st Sg. | `-fainn` | `-aim` | `-a` mé | `-ainn` |

| 3rd Sg. | `-fadh` sé | `-adh` sé | `-a` sé | `-adh` sé |

These verbs have a stem ending in a slender consonant, like `tuig`.
