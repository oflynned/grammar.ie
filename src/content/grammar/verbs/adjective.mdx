The Irish verbal adjective (`an aidiacht bhriathartha`) is a form of a verb that functions like an adjective. It is very similar to the English past participle (e.g., "broken", "seen", "written") and is essential for forming the passive voice and a common type of perfect tense. Colloquially, it is often called the past participle (`an rangabháil chaite`).

As an adjective, it belongs to the third declension, meaning it does not change its form in the genitive case or in the plural.

## The Verbal Adjective (An Aidiacht Bhriathartha)

### Formation of First Conjugation Verbs

The ending depends on the final consonant of the verb stem. The endings are `-tha/-the` or `-ta/-te`, with the choice between `a` and `e` depending on whether the final vowel of the stem is broad or slender.

| Final Sound of Stem | Rule | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `b`, `c`, `f`, `g`, `m`, `p`, `r` | Add `-tha` / `-the` | `fág` → `fágtha` (left)<br/>`gearr` → `gearrtha` (cut) |
| `ch`, `l`, `n`, `s` | Add `-ta` / `-te` | `glan` → `glanta` (cleaned)<br/>`bris` → `briste` (broken) |
| `d` | Add `-ta` / `-te` | `goid` → `goidte` (stolen) |
| `t`, `th` | Drop `t`/`th` and add `-ta` / `-te` | `sciot` → `sciota` (snatched)<br/>`ith` → `ite` (eaten) |
| `bh`, `mh` | Drop `bh`/`mh` and add `-fa` / `-fe` | `scríobh` → `scríofa` (written)<br/>`snámh` → `snáfa` (swum) |
| `-igh` (monosyllabic) | Drop `gh` and add `-te` | `dóigh` → `dóite` (burnt)<br/>`nigh` → `nite` (washed) |
| `-áil` | Add `-te` | `sábháil` → `sábháilte` (saved) |

<Note>
Some spelling and pronunciation points to be aware of:
- The ending `-fa` is a modern simplification of the older `-bhtha` or `-mhtha`. For example, `scríobhtha` became `scríofa`.
- When `-tha` is added to a stem ending in `b` or `g`, the consonant becomes voiceless: `-btha` is pronounced [-pə] and `-gtha` is pronounced [-kə].
- The combination `-dta` is pronounced [-tə].
- The letter sequences `tt` and `tht` do not occur in Irish, so when adding `-ta` to a stem ending in `t` or `th`, the original consonant is dropped.
</Note>

<Dialect>
There are significant regional variations in the pronunciation of these endings.
- **Connacht (Cois Fharraige/Conamara):** The endings `-tha/-the` after a stop consonant are often pronounced as a long `í` sound, [iː]. This also applies to `-dta/-dte` and `-ta/-te` after stems in `t` or `th`. The ending `-fa` is often pronounced [fə] rather than the expected [fiː]. After `r`, `-tha` can be pronounced [ə], as in `curtha` [kurə], or `-ta` [tə] is used instead, e.g., `curta`. After `m`, Conamara uses `-ta`, e.g., `cumta` instead of the standard `cumtha` (formed).
- **Munster:** The `h` sound [hə] can be heard even after stems ending in `-l`, where `-ta/-te` is written.
- **Ulster (Gaoth Dobhair):** After a broad stop, `-tha` is pronounced [iː]. After a slender stop, `-the` is pronounced [ə]. After `l`, `n`, `s`, the ending is [-tə]/[-t′ə]. For verbs ending in `-bh/-mh`, which are often vocalised (e.g., `scríobh` [s′k′r′iːu]), the verbal adjective ending is pronounced [uhə], e.g., `scríobhtha` [s′k′r′iːuhə], instead of the standard [fə].
</Dialect>

### Formation of Second Conjugation Verbs

Second conjugation verbs typically add `-the` or `-te`, often after dropping or changing the final letters of the stem.

| Final Sound of Stem | Rule | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `-igh` | Drop `-gh` and add `-the` | `ceannaigh` → `ceannaithe` (bought)<br/>`coinnigh` → `coinnithe` (kept) |
| `-il` | Add `-te` | `oscail` → `oscailte` (opened) |
| `-in` | Broaden stem and add `-ta` | `cosain` → `cosanta` (defended) |
| `-ir` | Broaden stem and add `-tha` | `imir` → `imeartha` (played) |
| `-im` | Broaden stem and add `-tha` | `foghlaim` → `foghlamtha` (learnt) |
| `-ing` | Add `-the` | `tuirling` → `tuirlingthe` (landed) |

<Note>
For verbs ending in `-in`, `-ir`, and `-im`, the final slender consonant of the stem is made broad before the ending is added (e.g., `imir` → `imear-` + `-tha` → `imeartha`).
</Note>

<Dialect region="Ulster">
In Ulster, the ending `-iste` is sometimes used as an alternative to `-ithe`, especially when the verb stem has slender consonants. For example, `coinniste` can be used instead of `coinnithe`.
</Dialect>

<Note>
A few second conjugation verbs are exceptions:
- `inis` (to tell) → `inste`
- `freastail` (to attend) → `freastalta`
</Note>

### Irregular Verbs

The verbal adjectives of the eleven irregular verbs are mostly regular in their formation, but are based on different stems.

| Verb | Verbal Adjective | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `abair` | `ráite` | said |
| `beir` | `beirthe` | caught, born |
| `bí` | `(beite)` | been |
| `clois` | `cloiste` | heard |
| `cluin` | `cluinte` | heard |
| `déan` | `déanta` | done, made |
| `faigh` | `faighte` | got, found |
| `feic` | `feicthe` | seen |
| `ith` | `ite` | eaten |
| `tar` | `tagtha` | come |
| `tabhair` | `tugtha` | given |
| `téigh` | `dulta` | gone |

<Note>
- `ráite` and `dulta` are formed from the verbal noun stems (`rá` and `dul`).
- `tagtha` and `tugtha` are formed from the present tense stems (`tag-` and `tug-`).
- The verb `bí` (to be) does not have a true verbal adjective. The form `beite` is only used for the "Participle of Necessity" (see below).
</Note>

## Using the Verbal Adjective

The verbal adjective has several key functions in Irish grammar.

### As a Standard Adjective

It can be used attributively (before a noun) or predicatively (after a verb like `tá`).

*   *an cruiscín briste* - the broken jug (attributive)
*   *Tá an bord briste.* - The table is broken. (predicative, passive meaning)
*   *Tá mé ólta.* - I am drunk. (predicative, active meaning)

### Forming the Passive Voice

When used with the verb `tá`, the verbal adjective of a transitive verb creates a passive construction. The agent (the one performing the action) is introduced by the preposition `ag`.

*   *Tá an bord briste agam.* - The table was broken by me. (Literally: The table is broken at me.)

This also works for verbs that are technically intransitive but take a prepositional object.

*   *Tá beirthe agam ort.* - You were caught by me. (From `beir ar`: to catch)

### Forming the Perfect Tense

The same structure (`tá` + verbal adjective + `ag` + agent) is also the most common way to form the perfect tense, equivalent to the English "have done".

**With transitive verbs:**
*   *Tá an bord briste agam.* - I have broken the table.

**With verbs taking a prepositional object:**
*   *Tá beirthe agam ort.* - I have caught you.
*   *Tá curtha isteach acu ar phost.* - They have applied for a post. (From `cuir isteach ar`: to apply for)
*   *Tá scríofa agam chugat.* - I have written to you. (From `scríobh chuig`: to write to)

**With intransitive verbs:**
For intransitive verbs, no agent with `ag` is needed. The subject of `tá` is the one who has performed the action.
*   *Tá mé imithe go hÉirinn.* - I have gone to Ireland.

In conditional clauses, this perfect tense construction can express the certainty of an event happening.
*   *Má fheictear thú, tá tú gafa.* - If you are seen, you are caught.

### In Other Constructions

The verbal adjective can be used predicatively with verbs other than `tá`.

*   *Chonaic mé scríofa mar sin é.* - I saw it written like that.

With the verb `faigh`, it forms a structure borrowed from English, "to get something done".
*   *conas rudaí a fháil déanta* - how to get things done
*   *Fuair sé gortaithe.* - He got hurt.

### With deireadh to Mean "Finished"

Combined with the noun `deireadh` (end), the verbal adjective describes the completion of an action. This is a passive construction where the agent is marked with `ag`.

*   *An bhfuil deireadh ite agat?* - Are you finished eating? (Literally: Is end eaten at you?)
*   *Bhí deireadh ráite aige.* - He had finished speaking.

## Modal Participles (Na Rangabhálacha)

Irish has several "modal participles" which function as adjectives to express the possibility or difficulty of an action. They are similar to English adjectives ending in "-able" or "-ible" (e.g., "breakable", "edible").

### General Formation

These participles are formed by adding a prefix (`in-`, `so-`, or `do-`) to a base form, which is usually identical to the verbal adjective. Sometimes, the genitive of the verbal noun is used as the base.

For example, from `sábháil` (to save), the verbal adjective is `sábháilte` and the verbal noun genitive is `sábhála`. Both can be used as a base: `doshábháilte` / `doshábhála` (unsaveable).

### Participle of Suitability (in-)

The prefix `in-` means "possible" or "-able".

*   `inbhriste` - breakable
*   `inite` - edible
*   `insábháilte` - saveable

It is often used with the copula:
*   *Is intuigthe as sin go...* - From that one can understand that... / That implies that...
*   *Is scéal inchreidte dóibh é.* - It is a story they can believe.

It can also be formed from the genitive of a noun:
*   `intráchta` - passable (from `trácht`, traffic)
*   `inphionóis` - punishable (from `pionós`, punishment)

### Participle of Ease (so-)

The prefix `so-` means "easily possible".

*   `sobhriste` - easily breakable
*   `soshábháilte` - easily saveable
*   `so-bhlasta` - palatable

It can also be formed from the genitive of a noun:
*   `so-iompair` - portable (from `iompar`, transport)
*   `sotheagaisc` - teachable (from `teagasc`, teaching)

### Participle of Difficulty (do-)

The prefix `do-` means "difficult" or "impossible" ("un-").

*   `dobhriste` - unbreakable
*   `doshábháilte` - unsaveable
*   `do-bhlasta` - unpalatable

It can also be formed from the genitive of a noun:
*   `dofhaisnéise` - indescribable (from `faisnéis`, information)
*   `doleithscéil` - inexcusable (from `leithscéal`, excuse)

### Participle of Comparison (in- ... le)

The prefix `in-` can be used with the genitive of a noun and the preposition `le` to express equality, meaning "as...as".

*   *Níl sí incheoil le Pól.* - She is not as musical as Paul. (from `ceol`, music)
*   *Ní raibh aon fhear inchamáin le Pól.* - There was no man who could handle a hurley as well as Paul. (from `camán`, hurley)

### Participle of Necessity

This is a rare form, now identical in shape to the verbal adjective, that expresses the necessity or appropriateness of an action. It is almost always used with the copula in the negative, with the logical subject introduced by `do`.

*   *Ní gearánta dom ar an aimsir.* - I have no reason to complain about the weather.
*   *Ní tógtha orm é.* - I am not to blame for it.
*   *Ní beite duit ann.* - You should not be there. (using the special form `beite` from `bí`)
*   *Ní dulta duit ansin.* - You should not go there.

## Related Adjective Formations

### Prefixes dea- and dí-

Similar to the modal participles, the prefixes `dea-` (good) and `dí-` (un-) can combine with the genitive of a noun to form an adjective.

*   `dea-éadaigh` - well-dressed (from `éadach`, clothing)
*   `dí-airm` - unarmed (from `arm`, weapon)

### Suffix -ach

The adjectival suffix `-ach` can be added to the genitive of a verbal noun to create an adjective that expresses an ability or tendency to do something.

*   `labhair` (to speak) → `labharthach` (talkative)
*   `mínigh` (to explain) → `mínitheach` (explanatory)
*   `tuig` (to understand) → `tuisceannach` (understanding)

<Details summary="Historical note: The lack of a present participle">
Unlike English, Irish has not had a present participle (e.g., "loving", "doing") since ancient times. Remnants of the original Proto-Celtic participle survive in a few nouns, such as `cara` (friend), which comes from Proto-Celtic *`karantos`* (loving). Instead of a present participle, Irish uses the verbal noun, for example in progressive constructions like *Tá mé ag léamh* (I am reading).
</Details>

<Details summary="Historical note: The prefixes so- and do-">
The prefixes `so-` (good, easy) and `do-` (bad, difficult) were once productive in forming pairs of adjectives with opposite meanings. Many of these pairs survive in the modern language, such as `saor` (free, cheap) and `daor` (unfree, expensive), or `sorcha` (bright) and `dorcha` (dark). However, this method of forming new adjectives is no longer active.
</Details>

<Details summary="Historical note: Formation from the genitive">
When modal participles are formed from nouns, the noun is put in the genitive case. Etymologically, this was not a genitive but a shift in the noun's declension class in Old Irish. This shift from a broad to a slender final consonant is now identical to the nominative-to-genitive change in the first declension, so the modern rule is described as using the genitive.
</Details>
