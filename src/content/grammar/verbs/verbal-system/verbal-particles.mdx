Verbal particles, or `na míreanna briathartha`, are short words that appear immediately before a verb. They are essential for forming questions, negations, and certain clauses. Most verbal particles cause an initial mutation (lenition or eclipsis) on the verb that follows.

Many particles have a distinct form for the past tense, typically ending in `-r`. This `-r` is a remnant of the historical perfect particle `ro`. With the exception of the positive direct relative particle and the past particle, verbs that follow a verbal particle use their dependent form.

The irregular verbs `bí`, `abair`, `déan`, `faigh`, `feic`, and `téigh` do not use the past tense forms of the verbal particles that end in `-r`. Instead, they have special dependent forms that incorporate this historical distinction (e.g., `bhí` becomes `raibh`).

When used with the copula, verbal particles often form combined words, or the copula itself is omitted and the particle functions as a copula form.

## Negative Particles: `ní` and `cha`

Negative particles are used to negate a verb, equivalent to "not" in English.

### Standard Negative Particle: `ní`

The most common negative particle is `ní`. Its past tense form is `níor`.

| Tense | Particle | Mutation | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Present, Future, etc. | `ní` | Lenition | *Ní ólfaidh tú fuisce arís.* (You will not drink whiskey again.) |
| Past | `níor` | Lenition | *Níor ól tú fuisce.* (You did not drink any whiskey.) |

**Dialect Note:** In Munster (Dingle), `ní` is often shortened to `n’` before the verbs `feadair` (to know), `féad` (to be able), and `feacaigh` (a regional form of `faca`, saw).
- *n’fheadar* (I don't know)
- *n’fhéadfainn* (I couldn't)
- *n’fheacaigh sé* (he didn't see)

### Ulster Negative Particle: `cha`

In Ulster, `cha` (often pronounced /ha/) is used, sometimes instead of `ní` and sometimes as a more emphatic negation alongside it. Its past tense form is `char`.

| Context | Particle | Mutation | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Before `b, c, f, g, m, p, s` | `cha` | Lenition | *Cha chreideann sé é.* (He doesn't believe it.) |
| Before `d, t` | `cha` | Eclipsis | *Cha ndéanaim a leithéid.* (I don't do such a thing.) |
| Before a vowel or `f` | `chan` | Lenition | *Chan ólann tú fuisce arís.* (You will not drink whiskey again.) |
| Past Tense | `char` | Lenition | *Char ól tú fuisce.* (You did not drink any whiskey.) |

**Usage Notes for `cha`:**
- After `cha`/`chan`, the habitual present tense is often used to express a future meaning.
- The mutation rules can vary in parts of Donegal. Etymologically, eclipsis would always be expected. Forms of `bí` starting with `b-` are often eclipsed (e.g., `cha mbíonn sé`), but lenition is also found (`cha bhíonn sé`).
- `Cha`/`chan` is also the standard negative particle in Scottish Gaelic. It derives from Old Irish `ní con`.

## Prohibitive Particle: `ná`

The prohibitive particle `ná` is used to form the negative imperative (i.e., to tell someone *not* to do something).

| Context | Particle | Mutation | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Before a consonant | `ná` | None | *Ná déan é!* (Don't do it!) |
| Before a vowel | `ná` | h-prefix | *Ná hól fuisce!* (Don't drink whiskey!) |

## Interrogative Particles: `an` and `nach`

Interrogative particles are placed at the beginning of a sentence to form a yes/no question. They do not have a direct one-word translation in English but signal that a question is being asked.

### Positive Interrogative Particle: `an`

Use `an` for positive questions. The past tense form is `ar`.

| Tense | Particle | Mutation | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Present, Future, etc. | `an` | Eclipsis | *An ólfá fuisce?* (Would you drink whiskey?) |
| Past | `ar` | Lenition | *Ar ól tú fuisce?* (Did you drink whiskey?) |

### Negative Interrogative Particle: `nach`

Use `nach` for negative questions (e.g., "Don't you...?"). The past tense form is `nár`.

| Tense | Particle | Mutation | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Present, Future, etc. | `nach` | Eclipsis | *Nach n-ólann tú fuisce?* (Don't you drink whiskey?) |
| Past | `nár` | Lenition | *Nár ól tú fuisce?* (Didn't you drink whiskey?) |

### Munster Interrogative Particle: `ná`

In Munster, `ná` is used instead of `nach`. It is unique because it takes the dependent form of the verb but causes no initial mutation.

| Tense | Particle | Mutation | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Present, etc. (consonant) | `ná` | None | *Ná fuil sé anso?* (Isn't he here?) |
| Present, etc. (vowel) | `ná` | h-prefix | *Ná hólair fuisce?* (Don't you drink whiskey?) |
| Past | `nár` | Lenition | *Nár ólais fuisce?* (Didn't you drink whiskey?) |

Because `ná` does not cause lenition or eclipsis, it is the only context where some dependent verb forms can appear in their unmutated state. For example, the dependent form `fuil` (from `bí`) is usually eclipsed (`bhfuil`), but after `ná` it remains `fuil`.

## Past Particle: `do`

The historical past particle `do` was once used to mark the past, imperfect, and conditional tenses. It is comparable to the augment in Ancient Greek.

Today, its usage is limited. It is only used with independent verb forms.

| Context | Particle | Mutation | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Before consonants | (`do`) | Lenition | *Thuig sé an scéal.* (He understood the story.) |
| Before vowels and `f` | `d’` | Lenition | *D’ól tú fuisce.* (You drank whiskey.) |

- The full form `do` before a consonant is now only common in Munster and is often used only at the very beginning of a sentence. In Standard Irish and other dialects, only the lenition remains.
- The `d’` prefix before a vowel or `fh-` is mandatory in all dialects.
- `Do`/`d’` is not used with dependent verb forms. The past tense marker is instead incorporated into the main particle (e.g., `níor` instead of `ní do`).
- `Do` does not lenite the autonomous verb form in the past tense (e.g., `do briseadh`, it was broken).

## Perfect Particle: `ro`

The perfect particle `ro` is no longer used as a standalone word in Modern Irish. It originally marked perfective aspect. Its main legacy is the `-r` ending found on the past tense forms of other verbal particles and conjunctions.

- It merged with other particles: `ní` + `ro` → `níor`; `an` + `ro` → `ar`; `go` + `ro` → `gur`.
- It is the source of the `-r` in `níor`, `gur`, `nár`, `ar`, `sular`, and `murar`.
- With some irregular verbs, `ro` became part of the verb stem itself, creating distinct dependent past forms like `raibh` (from `ro` + `bhí`).

## Relative Particles: `a`

Relative particles introduce relative clauses (e.g., "the man *who* came"). Irish distinguishes between direct and indirect relative clauses, each with its own particle.

### Direct Relative Particle

The direct relative particle is used when the noun being described is the subject or direct object of the relative clause.

| Polarity | Tense | Particle | Mutation | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Positive | Present, etc. | `a` | Lenition | *an fear a ólann fuisce* (the man who drinks whiskey) |
| Positive | Past | `a` | Lenition | *an fear a d’ól fuisce* (the man who drank whiskey) |
| Negative | Present, etc. | `nach` | Eclipsis | *an fear nach n-ólann fuisce* (the man who does not drink whiskey) |
| Negative | Past | `nár` | Lenition | *an fear nár ól fuisce* (the man who did not drink whiskey) |

- The positive particle `a` takes the independent verb form. With the verb `tá`, it combines to form `atá`.
- The negative particles `nach`/`nár` take the dependent verb form. In Munster, `ná` is used instead of `nach`.

### Indirect Relative Particle

The indirect relative particle is used when the noun being described has a more complex grammatical relationship to the relative clause, often involving a preposition (e.g., "the house *in which*...").

| Polarity | Tense | Particle | Mutation | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Positive | Present, etc. | `a` | Eclipsis | *an teach a n-ólann tú fuisce ann* (the house in which you drink whiskey) |
| Positive | Past | `ar` | Lenition | *an teach ar ól tú fuisce ann* (the house in which you drank whiskey) |
| Negative | Present, etc. | `nach` | Eclipsis | *an teach nach n-ólann tú fuisce ann* (the house in which you do not drink whiskey) |
| Negative | Past | `nár` | Lenition | *an teach nár ól tú fuisce ann* (the house in which you did not drink whiskey) |

- The indirect relative particle always takes the dependent verb form.
- In Munster, `go`/`gur` are used instead of the positive forms `a`/`ar`.

## Optative Particle: `go`

The optative particle is used to express wishes, hopes, blessings, or curses. It is followed by the subjunctive mood.

| Polarity | Particle | Mutation | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Positive | `go` | Eclipsis | *Go n-éirí an bóthar leat.* (May the road rise with you.) |
| Negative | `nár` | Lenition | *Nár fheicimid arís é.* (May we never see him again.) |

The negative `nár` gets its `-r` from the historical particle `ro`. An exception is with the verb `bí`: its subjunctive form is `raibh`, which already contains `ro`, so the negative particle is `ná` (e.g., *Ná raibh maith agat.*).

## Particle-like Conjunctions

Several conjunctions behave like verbal particles, including having distinct past tense forms ending in `-r`.

### `go` (that)

| Polarity | Tense | Particle | Mutation | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Positive | Present, etc. | `go` | Eclipsis | *...go n-ólann tú fuisce* (...that you drink whiskey) |
| Positive | Past | `gur` | Lenition | *...gur ól tú fuisce* (...that you drank whiskey) |
| Negative | Present, etc. | `nach` | Eclipsis | *...nach n-ólann tú fuisce* (...that you do not drink whiskey) |
| Negative | Past | `nár` | Lenition | *...nár ól tú fuisce* (...that you did not drink whiskey) |

### `sula` (before)

| Tense | Particle | Mutation | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Present, etc. | `sula` | Eclipsis | *...sula dtiocfaidh mé.* (...before I come.) |
| Past | `sular` | Lenition | *...sular tháinig mé.* (...before I came.) |

A common Munster variant is `sara`/`sarar`.

### `mura` (if not, unless)

| Tense | Particle | Mutation | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Present, etc. | `mura` | Eclipsis | *Mura bhfeiceann sé é...* (If he does not see it...) |
| Past | `murar` | Lenition | *Murar bhuail sé leis...* (If he did not meet him...) |

Dialectal variants include `mara`/`marar` (Connemara) and `muna`/`munar`.

### `ach a` (as soon as)

| Tense | Particle | Mutation | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Present, etc. | `ach a` | Eclipsis | *...ach a dtiocfaidh mé.* (...as soon as I come.) |
| Past | `ach ar` | Lenition | *...ach ar tháinig mé.* (...as soon as I came.) |

## Locative Interrogative Adverb: `cá`

The interrogative word `cá` (where) behaves like a verbal particle when it is immediately followed by a verb. It is historically a combination of `cá` and the indirect relative particle.

| Tense | Particle | Mutation | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Present, etc. | `cá` | Eclipsis | *Cá gcuirfidh tú é?* (Where will you put it?) |
| Past | `cár` | Lenition | *Cár chuir tú é?* (Where did you put it?) |

## Summary of Dialectal Variants

- **Munster:**
  - The past particle `do` is often used before consonants (*Do thuig sé*).
  - A `dh'`-prefix can appear after leniting particles before a vowel (*Ní dh’aontaím*).
  - The `-r` on past tense particles is sometimes omitted (*Ar thuig tú?* → *A thuig tú?*).
  - `ná` is used instead of `nach` as a negative interrogative, negative relative, and negative conjunction.
  - `go`/`gur` are used as the positive indirect relative particles.

- **Ulster:**
  - The negative particle `cha`/`chan`/`char` is used instead of or alongside `ní`/`níor`.
