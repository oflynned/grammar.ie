The verb `bí` (to be) cannot connect a subject and a predicate noun in the same direct way as the copula `is`. When using `bí` for classification, a preposition is always required to link the subject to its role, state, or category. This page covers the most common prepositions used for this purpose.

## Classification with `i` (in)

The preposition `i` (in) is frequently used in these constructions. The logic is that a person is "in" a particular state or "in" a certain role or function. There are a few common patterns.

### Using `i` with a Possessive Pronoun

This is the most common structure. The pattern is `i` + possessive pronoun + predicate noun. The possessive pronoun always refers back to the subject of the sentence.

*Tá mé i mo dhochtúir.*
(I am in my doctor.)
"I am a doctor."

Although the possessive pronoun makes the noun definite (e.g., `i mo dhochtúir` literally means "in my doctor"), the English translation uses the indefinite article "a".

This structure forms a direct relative clause, even with the pronoun.
*an bhean atá ina dochtúir* - the woman who is a doctor (literally, "the woman who is in her doctor")

<Note>
This sentence structure is identical to the progressive aspect of stative verbs, such as *Tá mé i mo shuí* (I am sitting).
</Note>

### Using `i` with a Subject in a Relative Clause

This structure is often used for emphasis, making it stronger than a standard copula sentence or the possessive pronoun construction above. The pattern is `[Predicate] atá i + [Subject]`.

*Fear atá ionat.*
([Is] a man who is in you.)
"You are a man."

This construction is a type of cleft sentence, where an initial copula (`Is`) is often omitted. It can also be used in definitions.

*Is é atá sa ghaoth ná gluaiseacht chothrománach aeir.*
(Is it that is in the wind but a horizontal movement of air.)
"Wind is a horizontal movement of air."

<Note>
Some grammars suggest that only prepositional pronouns (like `ionam`, `ionat`, `ann`) should follow `i` in this structure. However, in practice, it is frequently used with proper names and other nouns.
</Note>

*Duine maith atá i bPádraig.*
(A good person that is in Pádraig.)
"Pádraig is a good person."

<Dialect region="Connacht">
In the Cois Fharraige dialect of Connacht, `in` is sometimes used instead of `i` before names, which prevents the usual eclipsis. For example, you might hear *in Bríd* instead of the standard *i mBríd*.
</Dialect>

### Ambiguity with `ann`

The word `ann` can be a prepositional pronoun ("in him") or an adverb ("there"). This can lead to ambiguity in sentences describing a male person or a masculine noun.

*Rí atá ann.* can mean:
1.  He is a king. (Classification, where `ann` = "in him")
2.  There is a king. (Existence, where `ann` = "there")

For feminine subjects, this ambiguity does not exist because the prepositional pronoun is `inti` ("in her").

*Bean atá inti.* - She is a woman. (Classification)
*Banríon atá inti.* - She is a queen. (Classification)

However, when describing existence, `ann` is always used, regardless of the gender of the noun.

*Banríon atá ann.* - There is a queen. (Existence)

The adverbial `ann` ("there") can also appear in a standard non-relative clause.
*Tá rí ann.* - There is a king.
*Tá banríon ann.* - There is a queen.

### Using `i` in "Only" Sentences

The structure `i` + subject is typically restricted to relative clauses (as seen above) and semi-negative "only" sentences using `ach`.

*Níl ionam ach amadán.*
(Is-not in-me but a-fool.)
"I am only a fool."

An ordinary affirmative sentence like `*Tá amadán ionam` is incorrect. You must use the possessive pronoun structure instead: *Tá mé i m’amadán* (I am a fool).

<Note>
When `Níl ionam...` is not part of an "only" sentence and is followed by a verbal noun, it means "I cannot...".
*Ní raibh ionam labhairt.* - I could not speak.
</Note>

### When to Use `bí` + `i`

These forms are used in specific contexts:

1.  **For temporary or newly acquired roles:** This construction is used for professions, functions, or states that are not inherent or permanent.
    * *Bhí mé fós i mo chailín beag ag an am sin.* - I was still a little girl at that time.

    <Warning>
    Do not use this structure for permanent, unchangeable classifications. For these, you must use the copula.
    | Avoid | Prefer |
    |---|---|
    | *Tá an bradán ina iasc.* | *Is iasc é an bradán.* (The salmon is a fish.) |
    A salmon will always be a fish; it does not temporarily hold the position of one.
    </Warning>

2.  **For tenses without a copula form:** The copula `is` has a limited number of tenses. For the future, imperative, or sentences requiring a verbal noun, you must use `bí`.
    * *Bí i d’fhear!* - Be a man!
    * *Ná bí i d’amadán!* - Don't be a fool!
    * *Ní maith léi bheith ina cailín beag.* - She doesn't like being a little girl.

3.  **For emphasis:** The relative clause structure (`Fear atá ionat`) is more emphatic than both the standard copula sentence and the possessive pronoun structure.
    * *Dochtúir atá ionam.* - I am a doctor. (Emphatic)
    * *Is dochtúir mé.* - I am a doctor. (Neutral)

## Classification with `ar` (on)

Using the preposition `ar` (on) often expresses the idea of being "one of..." a group. It is very common with comparative adjectives to form a superlative meaning.

This structure often comes from the fuller phrase `ar dhuine de` (on one of...) or `ar cheann de` (on one of...), but `dhuine de` or `cheann de` is frequently omitted.

*Tá sí ar (dhuine de) na cailíní is áille.*
(Is she on (person of) the girls that-are most-beautiful.)
"She is one of the most beautiful girls."

## Identification with `ar` (on)

A similar structure with `ar` is used for identification, particularly with superlatives ("the cleverest") and ordinal numbers ("the first," "the second").

*Tá sé ar an duine is cliste sa rang.*
(Is he on the person most clever in the class.)
"He is the cleverest person in the class."

*Ba í an chéad duine ar an liosta.*
(Was she the first person on the list.)
"She was the first person on the list."

## Classification with `mar` (as/like)

The preposition `mar` (as, like) can also be used in classification sentences. The meaning can be a literal comparison ("to be like something") or a simple classification.

*Tá sé mar athair dom.*
(Is he like a-father to-me.)
"He is like a father to me."

*D'fhóin sé mar shaighdiúir.*
(Served he as a-soldier.)
"He served as a soldier."

## Classification with `de` (of)

This construction is limited to equative sentences, which express that something is "as... as...". An apposition with `de` + an indefinite noun can follow `chomh` + adjective. This structure translates to "such a...".

*chomh mór de theach* - such a big house (literally, "as big of a house")

*Ní fhaca mé riamh a leithéid, chomh beag de dhuine agus an oiread sin misnigh aige.*
(I never saw the like, such a small person and he with so much courage.)
"I never saw the like, such a small person with so much courage."
