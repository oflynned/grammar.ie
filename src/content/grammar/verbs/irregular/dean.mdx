The verb `déan` means "to do" or "to make" and is one of the most common irregular verbs in Irish. Its main irregularities are in the past tense, where it uses the stem `rinne` in independent statements and a different stem, `dearna`, in dependent contexts such as questions and negative statements.

## Indicative Mood

### Present Tense

The present tense is regular and follows the pattern of first-conjugation verbs.

*Déanann sí a cuid oibre gach lá.* - She does her work every day.

| Person | Form |
| --- | --- |
| 1st sg. | `déanaim` |
| 2nd sg. | `déanann tú` |
| 3rd sg. | `déanann sé/sí` |
| 1st pl. | `déanaimid` |
| 2nd pl. | `déanann sibh` |
| 3rd pl. | `déanann siad` |
| Autonomous | `déantar` |
| Relative | `a dhéanas` |

### Preterite (Past Tense)

In the past tense, `déan` uses the independent stem `rinne`. This form is used in simple affirmative statements. For questions and negative statements, see the dependent forms below.

*Rinne mé an ciste inné.* - I made the cake yesterday.

| Person | Form |
| --- | --- |
| 1st sg. | `rinneas` |
| 2nd sg. | `rinne tú` |
| 3rd sg. | `rinne sé/sí` |
| 1st pl. | `rinneamar` |
| 2nd pl. | `rinneabhair` |
| 3rd pl. | `rinneadar` |
| Autonomous | `rinneadh` |

<Note>
The form `dhein` is a common variant for `rinne` in the standard language and is the usual form in Munster Irish. For example, *Dhein sé an obair* instead of *Rinne sé an obair*.
</Note>

### Imperfect Tense

The imperfect tense describes habitual or repeated actions in the past.

*Dhéanainn staidéar gach oíche.* - I used to study every night.

| Person | Form |
| --- | --- |
| 1st sg. | `dhéanainn` |
| 2nd sg. | `dhéantá` |
| 3rd sg. | `dhéanadh sé/sí` |
| 1st pl. | `dhéanaimis` |
| 2nd pl. | `dhéanadh sibh` |
| 3rd pl. | `dhéanaidís` |
| Autonomous | `dhéantaí` |

### Future Tense

The future tense is regular and follows the pattern of first-conjugation verbs.

*Déanfaidh mé é amárach.* - I will do it tomorrow.

| Person | Form |
| --- | --- |
| 1st sg. | `déanfad` |
| 2nd sg. | `déanfaidh tú` |
| 3rd sg. | `déanfaidh sé/sí` |
| 1st pl. | `déanfaimid` |
| 2nd pl. | `déanfaidh sibh` |
| 3rd pl. | `déanfaidh siad` |
| Autonomous | `déanfar` |
| Relative | `a dhéanfas` |

## Dependent Preterite Forms

In the past tense, `déan` uses the special stem `dearna` in dependent contexts. This includes negative statements with `ní`, questions with `an`, and clauses introduced by `go` or `nach`. Note that the past tense particle `d'` is not used with these forms.

*Ní dhearna mé an obair.* - I did not do the work.
*An ndearna tú an ciste?* - Did you make the cake?

| Person | Negative (`ní`) | Interrogative (`an`) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1st sg. | `ní dhearnas` | `an ndearnas` |
| 2nd sg. | `ní dhearna tú` | `an ndearna tú` |
| 3rd sg. | `ní dhearna sé/sí` | `an ndearna sé/sí` |
| 1st pl. | `ní dhearnamar` | `an ndearnamar` |
| 2nd pl. | `ní dhearnabhair` | `an ndearnabhair` |
| 3rd pl. | `ní dhearnadar` | `an ndearnadar` |
| Autonomous | `ní dhearnadh` | `an ndearnadh` |

The conditional mood expresses what would happen under certain conditions.

The imperative is used for giving commands or instructions.

## Verbal Noun and Adjective

### Verbal Noun

The verbal noun is `déanamh`.

*Tá mé ag déanamh an dinnéir.* - I am making the dinner.
The genitive of the verbal noun is `déanaimh` when used as a noun (*e.g.,* `fear déanaimh an bháid` - the man who made the boat) and `déanta` when used as part of a verbal phrase (*e.g.,* `chun é a dhéanamh` - to do it).

### Verbal Adjective

The verbal adjective is `déanta`.

*Tá an obair déanta.* - The work is done.

## Dialectal Variations

<Dialect region="Munster">
In Munster Irish, the regular verb `déin` or `dein` is often used instead of `déan` in most tenses. However, the future and conditional stems are still based on `déan-`. The past tense form is typically `dhein`.
</Dialect>

<Dialect region="Ulster">
In Ulster Irish, older absolute forms are sometimes retained in the present tense, such as `ním` (I do) and `níonn sé` (he does). In this dialect, forms like `déanaim` and `déanann` are used only as dependent forms (e.g., after `ní`, `an`, `go`).
</Dialect>

<Details summary="Historical Forms">
Historically, Irish distinguished between absolute and dependent verb forms in most tenses. For `déan`, the absolute forms were derived from the older verb `do-ghní`. The forms based on `déan-` were originally only the dependent forms.

| Tense | Dependent | Absolute (Older Form) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Present** | `déanann` | `(do-)ghníonn` |
| **Imperfect** | `déanadh` | `(do-)ghníodh` |
| **Future** | `déanfaidh` | `(do-)ghéanaidh` |
| **Conditional** | `déanfadh` | `(do-)ghéanadh` |

These absolute forms are now mostly obsolete in the standard language but have influenced some dialectal forms, particularly in Ulster.
</Details>
