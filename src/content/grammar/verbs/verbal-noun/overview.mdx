The Irish verbal noun, or *an t-ainm briathartha*, is a important part of the language that functions similarly to the infinitive ("to do") or the gerund ("doing") in English. It is a noun derived from a verb, but it retains some verb-like properties, such as the ability to take an object or be modified by an adverb. Unlike the simple infinitive in many languages, the verbal noun in Irish is a fully-fledged noun that can be masculine or feminine, have a plural form, and be declined for case.

The verbal noun differs from a typical infinitive in several ways:
- It can describe both the action and the result of the action.
- It has a wide variety of endings (e.g., `-acht`, `-iú`, `-áil`).
- It has a grammatical gender (masculine or feminine).
- It can be put into the genitive case.
- It can take objects, which are expressed as genitive attributes or governed by possessive pronouns.
- It is used with a wide range of prepositions, such as `ag`, `a`, `do`, `chun`, `le`, and `tar éis`.

At the same time, it behaves like a verb by taking adverbs and direct objects.

## Formation of the Verbal Noun

The formation of the verbal noun is highly irregular, especially for common verbs. While there are regular patterns, many verbs have unique endings that must be learned individually. Dialects may also use different forms for the same verb.

<Dialect>
  For example, the verbal noun for "to see" can be `feiceáil` (Standard/Munster) or `feiscint` (Connacht), and "to open" can be `oscailt` (Standard) or `foscladh` (Ulster).
</Dialect>

## First Conjugation Verbs

### Regular Formation: `-adh` or `-eadh`

The most common regular ending for first conjugation verbs is `-adh` or `-eadh` (masculine).

- `bris` → `briseadh` (to break)
- `mol` → `moladh` (to praise)
- `cas` → `casadh` (to turn)
- `póg` → `pógadh` (to kiss)

Often, the verb root is broadened (depalatalised) before the ending is added.

- `buail` → `bualadh` (to strike)

### Verbs Ending in `-áil`

Verbs that end in `-áil` use the same form for their verbal noun (feminine).

- `péinteáil` (verb) → `péinteáil` (verbal noun) (to paint / painting)

### Verbs Ending in a Long Vowel + `-igh`

For verbs ending in `á`, `é`, `í`, `ó`, `ú` followed by `-igh`, the verbal noun is formed by dropping the `-igh`, leaving just the long vowel (masculine).

- `báigh` → `bá` (to drown)
- `pléigh` → `plé` (to discuss)
- `cloígh` → `cloí` (to conquer)
- `dóigh` → `dó` (to burn)
- `dreoigh` → `dreo` (to wither)

### Verbs Ending in a Short Vowel or Consonant + `-igh`

For verbs ending in a short vowel or consonant followed by `-igh`, the verbal noun often ends in `-í` (masculine).

- `suigh` → `suí` (to sit)
- `figh` → `fí` (to weave)
- `nigh` → `ní` (to wash)

### Irregular and Other Common Formations

#### No Ending

Some verbal nouns have the same form as the verb stem.

- `ól` → `ól` (to drink)
- `fás` → `fás` (to grow)
- `léim` → `léim` (to jump)

In other cases, the verb stem is broadened (depalatalised) but no ending is added.

- `cuir` → `cur` (to put)
- `coisc` → `cosc` (to prevent)
- `tomhais` → `tomhas` (to measure)

#### Ending in `-amh`

This is a common masculine ending.

- `déan` → `déanamh` (to do/make)
- `seas` → `seasamh` (to stand)
- `caith` → `caitheamh` (to throw, to spend)
- `léigh` → `léamh` (to read)

#### Ending in `-áil`

This feminine ending also appears on verbs that do not end in `-áil` themselves.

- `fág` → `fágáil` (to leave)
- `gabh` → `gabháil` (to take)
- `tóg` → `tógáil` (to lift)

#### Other Common Endings

- **-úint** (feminine): `lean` → `leanúint` (to follow)
- **-an** (masculine): `lig` → `ligean` (to allow), `leag` → `leagan` (to knock down)
- **-int** (feminine): `féach` → `féachaint` (to look), `tuig` → `tuiscint` (to understand)
- **-t** (feminine): `bain` → `baint` (to take out), `roinn` → `roinnt` (to divide)
- **-im / -m** (feminine): `tit` → `titim` (to fall), `seinm` → `seinm` (to play music)
- **-acht** (feminine): `fan` → `fanacht` (to wait), `éist` → `éisteacht` (to listen)
- **Rarer endings**: `-achtáil`, `-íl`, `-ach`, `-achan`, `-idh`.

## Second Conjugation Verbs

### Regular Formation: `-ú`

The regular ending for second conjugation verbs is `-ú` (masculine).

- `saothraigh` → `saothrú` (to work hard)

### Verbs Ending in Slender `-l`, `-n`, `-r`

Verbs whose stems end in a slender `l`, `n`, or `r` often take the ending `-t` (feminine).

- `oscail` → `oscailt` (to open)
- `imir` → `imirt` (to play)

### Irregular and Other Common Formations

#### Ending in `-í`

This masculine ending is common for verbs ending in `-igh`.

- `éirigh` → `éirí` (to rise)
- `cónaigh` → `cónaí` (to reside)
- `fiafraigh` → `fiafraí` (to ask)

#### No Ending

Some verbal nouns are formed by dropping the `-igh` suffix or are identical to the verb stem. In some cases, the stem is also broadened.

- `damhsaigh` → `damhsa` (to dance)
- `foghlaim` → `foghlaim` (to learn)
- `ceangail` → `ceangal` (to connect)
- `siúil` → `siúl` (to walk)

#### Other Common Endings

- **-ach** (masculine): `ceannaigh` → `ceannach` (to buy), `cuardaigh` → `cuardach` (to search)
- **-acht** (feminine): `dúisigh` → `dúiseacht` (to awaken), `imigh` → `imeacht` (to go away)
- **-áil / -achtáil** (feminine): `coinnigh` → `coinneáil` (to keep), `airigh` → `aireachtáil` (to perceive)
- **-amh** (masculine): `smaoinigh` → `smaoineamh` (to think), `áirigh` → `áireamh` (to count)

## Verbal Nouns without a Verb

Some verbal nouns exist without a corresponding finite verb. These are often formed from nouns for people (especially professions) and are typically used to describe the activity associated with that person, most commonly in the progressive aspect (`ag` + verbal noun).

Common endings include `-óireacht`, `-aireacht`, and `-aíocht`.

- *`Tá sé ag feirmeoireacht.`* - He is working as a farmer. (from `feirmeoir`, a farmer)
- *`Tá sé ag siopadóireacht.`* - He is shopping. (from `siopadóir`, a shopkeeper)
- *`Bíonn sé ag Gaeilgeoireacht ó dhubh go dubh.`* - He speaks Irish from dawn till dusk. (from `Gaeilgeoir`, an Irish speaker)
- *`Tá siad ag cabaireacht.`* - They are chattering. (from `cabaire`, a chatterbox)
- *`Tá sé ag scéalaíocht.`* - He is telling stories. (from `scéalaí`, a storyteller)

This can also happen with diminutives ending in `-ín`.

- *`Tá sé ag sláimínteacht.`* - He is taking small amounts. (from `sláimín`, a small amount)
- *`Bhí sé ag croisínteacht.`* - He was harvesting seaweed. (from `croisín`, a tool for harvesting seaweed)

## Declension of the Verbal Noun

As a noun, the verbal noun can be declined for case.

### The Genitive Case

The genitive of the verbal noun is complex because its form depends on how it is used. It can either take the form of the verbal adjective or a regular noun genitive.

#### Using the Verbal Adjective as the Genitive

The verbal adjective form is used as the genitive when the verbal noun is transitive, meaning it takes a direct object (expressed as a genitive attribute or a possessive pronoun).

- *`lucht ólta fuisce`* - whiskey drinkers (literally, "people of the drinking of whiskey")
- *`lucht foghlamtha na Gaeilge`* - Irish learners (literally, "people of the learning of Irish")
- *`chun a mbuailte`* - to strike them

Verbal nouns ending in `-adh`, `-ú`, and other long vowels (`-á`, `-é`, `-í`, `-ó`) **always** use the verbal adjective form for their genitive, regardless of usage.

- `glanadh` (cleaning) → genitive `glanta`
- `saothrú` (working hard) → genitive `saothraithe`
- `suí` (sitting) → genitive `suite`

#### Using a Substantive Genitive

A regular noun genitive (formed according to the noun's declension class) is used when the verbal noun is intransitive (takes no object) or is used in a purely noun-like sense.

- *`lá breithe`* - birthday (literally, "day of birth")
- *`lucht óil`* - drinkers (people of drinking, in general)
- *`lucht foghlama`* - learners (people of learning, in general)

<Note>
  Verbal nouns ending in `-áil` always use a substantive genitive (`-ála`) and never the verbal adjective form. The same applies to many verbal nouns ending in `-áint` (gen. `-ána`), `-úint` (gen. `-úna`), and `-cht` (gen. `-chta`).
</Note>

#### When Both Forms Coincide

For some second conjugation verbs (those ending in `-l, -n, -r` with verbal nouns in `-t`), the substantive genitive and the verbal adjective happen to have the same form.

- `oscailt` (opening) → genitive `oscailte`
- `imirt` (playing) → genitive `imeartha`

<Details summary="Historical Note: Why use the verbal adjective for the genitive?">
  In Irish, a noun in the genitive case often functions as an attribute, similar to an adjective (e.g., `teach Sheáin`, "Seán's house," where `Seáin` modifies `teach`). The verbal adjective is the primary attributive form of a verb (e.g., `teach tógtha`, "a built house"). When a verbal noun is used in a verb-like way (transitively) and needs to be in the genitive case, the language defaults to the verb's existing attributive form: the verbal adjective. Historically, sound changes also caused the genitive forms of older verbal nouns ending in `-adh` to become identical to their verbal adjectives.
</Details>

### The Dative Case

In Standard Irish, the dative form of a verbal noun is always the same as its nominative form. This is especially relevant when forming the progressive aspect with the preposition `ag`, which governs the dative case.

<Dialect>
  In some dialects, certain feminine verbal nouns of the second declension have a distinct dative form. This is most common with intransitive verbal nouns that describe sounds and end in `-ach`. The dative is formed by changing the ending to `-aigh` or `-igh`.
  - *`Tá na capaill ag seitrigh.`* - The horses are neighing. (from `seitreach`)
  - *`Tá na ba ag géimnigh.`* - The cows are lowing. (from `géimneach`)
  - *`Bhí sí ag cogarnaigh go séimh.`* - She whispered gently. (from `cogarnach`)
  - *`Thosaigh m’athair ag béicigh.`* - My father began to shout. (from `béiceach`)

  Masculine verbal nouns ending in `-ach`, however, do not have a special dative form.
  - *`Tá mé ag ceannach glasraí.`* - I am buying vegetables. (from `ceannach`)
</Dialect>

### The Plural

A verbal noun can have a plural form only when used in a purely noun-like sense, referring to multiple instances of an action or result.

#### Verbal nouns in `-adh`

The plural is typically formed by adding `-í` to the verbal adjective form.

- `glanadh` (a cleaning) → `glantaí` (cleanings)
- `briseadh` (a breaking) → `bristeacha` (breakings, outbreaks)

#### Verbal nouns in `-ú`

The plural ending is usually `-uithe` or `-ithe`.

- `saothrú` (a cultivation) → `saothruithe` (cultivations)

<Note>
  Be careful not to confuse the plural `saothruithe` with the genitive singular/verbal adjective `saothraithe`.
</Note>

#### Verbal nouns in `-áil`

The plural ending is `-álacha`.

- `gabháil` (a capture) → `gabhálacha` (captures)

## Short and Long Forms

Some verbs have two different verbal nouns: a standard short form and a special long form. The long forms, often created with endings like `-chán`, `-nach`, or `-arnach`, tend to have a more abstract, general, or durational meaning. The short forms are more concrete and verbal.

- `léamh` / `léitheoireacht` (reading)
- `ní` / `níochán` (washing)
- `léim` / `léimneach` (jumping)
- `luascadh` / `luascarnach` (swinging)

Syntactically, the short form is often used transitively (with an object), while the long form is used intransitively (without an object).

- *`Bhí sí ag fí an éadaigh.`* - She was weaving the cloth. (short, transitive)
- *`Chaith sí an lá ag fíochán.`* - She spent the day weaving. (long, intransitive)
