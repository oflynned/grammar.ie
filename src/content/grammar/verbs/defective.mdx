Defective verbs, or `na briathra uireasacha`, are verbs that cannot be fully conjugated because they only exist in a limited number of forms. Unlike irregular verbs, which have a full but unpredictable set of conjugations, defective verbs are missing most of their tenses and persons. Some even appear in only a single form.

## Feadair (to know)

The verb `feadair` is a remnant of an older verb system and is the only surviving deponent verb in Irish. This means its forms look passive but have an active meaning. It is timeless, so a form like `feadramar` can mean "we know" or "we knew" depending on context.

Today, `feadair` is almost exclusively used in questions, negative sentences, and the answers to them.

*   *An bhfeadair tú?* - Do you know?
*   *Ní fheadar.* - I don't know / I wonder.

It often merges with the negative particle `ní` to become `n’fheadair`.

*   *N’fheadar an bhfuil sé ann fós.* - I wonder if he is still there.

<Note>
In speech, the first person form `ní fheadar` is often shortened further to `n’fhead’` or `n’fhea’`.
</Note>

When used with the preposition `do` (to), it means "to be able to tell someone" or "to let someone know".

*   *An bhfeadraís seo dom?* - Can you tell me this?

### Conjugation

| Person | Verb form | Alternatives |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1st Sg. | `feadar` | `feadair mé` |
| 2nd Sg. | `feadraís` | `feadair tú` |
| 3rd Sg. | `feadair sé/sí` | |
| 1st Pl. | `feadramar` | `feadramair`, `feadair muid` |
| 2nd Pl. | `feadrabhar` | `feadrabhair`, `feadair sibh` |
| 3rd Pl. | `feadradar` | `feadraid`, `feadair siad` |

<Dialect region="Munster">
The verb `feadair` is primarily used in Munster Irish. In this dialect, an epenthetic vowel is often inserted between the `d` and `r`, so a word like `feadramair` is pronounced as if it were spelt `feadaramair`.
</Dialect>

<Details summary="Historical note on conjugation">
The original stem of this verb was `fead-`. The `-r-` was originally part of an old passive ending. Over time, this `-r-` was reanalysed as part of the stem, becoming `feadr-`. Forms without the `-r-` before the plural endings (e.g., `feadamar`) were common in Classical Irish but are rare now.
</Details>

<Note>
In most of Ireland, the idiom `tá a fhios ag...` (literally "his knowledge is at...") is far more common for expressing "to know". It is used for all positive statements, and often for questions and negatives too.

*   *Tá a fhios agam.* - I know.
</Note>

<Dialect region="Connacht">
In Connemara, a different verb, `ní`, can be heard with the meaning "to wonder" or "to be surprised" (e.g., *Ní mé* - I wonder). This is likely a form of the verb `déan`.
</Dialect>

## Ar, arsa (to say)

The verb `ar` or `arsa` is used to mean "said", but only immediately after a line of direct speech. It is timeless, so `arsa mise` can mean "I say" or "I said".

The main rules for its use are:
*   **`ar`** is used only before third-person pronouns: `ar seisean` (said he), `ar sise` (said she), `ar siadsan` (said they).
*   **`arsa`** is used in all other cases, including with nouns and other pronouns: `arsa mise` (said I), `arsa an fear` (said the man). An older form, `ars`, is sometimes seen before the article.

<Note>
Only the emphatic forms of personal pronouns are used with `ar` and `arsa`: `mise`, `tusa`, `seisean`, `sise`, `sinne`, `sibhse`, `siadsan`.
</Note>

*   *"Tuigim anois", arsa Seán.* - "I understand now," said Seán.
*   *"Ní thuigeann tú ar chor ar bith", ar sise.* - "You don't understand at all," she said.

<Warning>
The verb `ar/arsa` cannot be used for indirect (reported) speech. For that, you must use a different verb, such as `abair` (to say).

*   *Dúirt sé léi gur thuig sé.* - He told her that he understood.
*   *Dúirt sí leis nár thuig sé ar chor ar bith.* - She told him that he didn't understand at all.
</Warning>

The verb `abair` can also be used instead of `ar/arsa` for direct speech, often in the relative present tense form `a deir`.

## Dar (to seem)

This verb exists only in the single, timeless form `dar`, meaning "it seems" or "it seemed". It is always used with the preposition `le` (with) to indicate whose opinion it is.

*   `dar liom` - it seems to me / I think
*   `dar leis` - it seems to him / he thinks

It can be placed after a statement or before a `go`/`nach` clause.

*   *Dar leis an bhfear go bhfuil sé ann.* - The man thought that he was there. (Literally: "It seemed to the man that he is there.")
*   *Sin a bhfuil le rá, dar liom.* - That's all there is to say, it seems to me.

## Dóbair (it almost happened)

This verb, which has variants like `d'fhóbair`, `fhobair`, and `hóbair`, exists only in a single form derived from the third-person singular past tense. It now functions more like an adverb meaning "almost" or "nearly".

It can be followed by a verbal noun construction (with the subject introduced by `do`) or by a `go`/`nach` clause.

*   *Dóbair dom titim.* - I almost fell.
*   *Dóbair go dtitfinn.* - I almost fell.

<Dialect region="Munster">
In Munster, `dóbair` is treated entirely as an adverb and is used with the copula: `ba dhóbair`.
</Dialect>

## Aitim (I implore)

This formal verb only exists in two present tense forms for the first person.
*   `aitim` - I implore
*   `aitimid` - we implore

Alternative spellings `aicim` and `aicimid` are also found. The older spelling was `aithchim`/`aithchimid`.

## Tarlaigh (to happen)

This verb is only used in the third person; it has no forms for "I", "you", or "we". Its verbal noun is `tarlú` (a happening, an event).

The past tense is irregular: **`tharla`** (though the regular-looking `tharlaigh` is also heard in some dialects).

When `tharla` is followed by a clause, it does not use the dummy subject `sé` (it).

*   *Tharla timpiste.* - An accident happened.
*   *Tharla go raibh mé ann.* - It happened that I was there.

If the event happens *to* someone, that person is introduced with the preposition `do`.

*   *Tharla dom a bheith ann nuair a tháinig sé.* - I happened to be there when he came.

The phrase `ó tharla (go)` means "since" or "because".

*   *Agus ó tharla muid ag fanacht le fada le freagra...* - And since we had been waiting a long time for an answer...

## Téana (come)

This verb is only used in the imperative mood to mean "come on" or "let's go".

*   *Téana ort!* - Come on! (to one person)
*   *Téanaigí oraibh!* - Come on! (to more than one person)

A common form for the first-person plural ("let's...") is `téanam`.

*   *Téanam abhaile!* - Let's go home!

<Note>
The form `téanam` is sometimes used as a general imperative, even when addressing one or more people directly: `Téanam ort!` or `Téanam oraibh!`.
</Note>

A verbal noun, `téanachtaint`, exists for reporting the command in indirect speech.

*   *Dúirt sé liom téanachtaint leis.* - He told me to come with him.

## Ráinig, ráinigh (reached)

This verb is rare today but common in older literature. It means "to reach", "to arrive", or "to happen".

<Dialect region="Munster">
The verb was used primarily in Munster. The original past tense form was `ráinig`. Because final `-ig` and `-igh` are pronounced the same in Munster, it was re-spelt as `ráinigh`, making it look like a regular second-conjugation verb. From this, new "regular" forms like `ráiníonn sé` (he reaches) were sometimes created.
</Dialect>

### Usage

*   **To reach a place or time:** It has mostly been replaced by `sroich`.
*   **To happen:** *Conas mar a ráinig go raibh tú anseo?* - How did it happen that you were here?
*   **To manage or succeed (with `le`):** *Ráinigh liom é a dhéanamh.* - I managed to do it.
*   **To be able to (impersonally with `le`):** *sara ráinig linn-ne dul abhaile* - before we could go home.

The verb is best known today from its appearance in the Irish national anthem.

*   *...buíon dár slua thar toinn do ráinig chugainn...* - ...a group of our host that came to us over the waves...

<Details summary="Historical note">
The verb `ráinig` is etymologically related to `tháinig`, the past tense of `tar` (to come). Its verbal noun, `riachtain`, survived with a different meaning in the words `riachtanach` (necessary) and `riachtanas` (necessity). The old subjunctive form survives in the rare preposition `go ruige` (until, literally "until you reach").
</Details>

## The Copula Is

The copula, `is`, is also considered a defective verb because it lacks a full set of conjugations. It is a complex topic and is covered in its own dedicated chapter.
