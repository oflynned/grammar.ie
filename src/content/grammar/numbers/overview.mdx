The Irish number system has several distinct categories of numbers and two different counting systems, one traditional and one modern. This guide provides a comprehensive overview of how to form and use numbers in Irish.

The main types of numbers are:
- **Cardinal Numbers**: Used for counting. They have two forms:
    - **Abstract/Substantival Numbers** (`maoluimhreacha`): Used for counting aloud, numbering, telling time, etc. (e.g., `a haon, a dó, a trí...`).
    - **Adjectival Numbers** (`bunuimhreacha`): Used to count nouns (e.g., *trí bhád* - three boats).
- **Personal Numbers** (`uimhreacha pearsanta`): Special forms for counting people (e.g., *triúr fear* - three men).
- **Ordinal Numbers** (`orduimhreacha`): Used to indicate order (e.g., *an chéad lá* - the first day).

## The Two Counting Systems

Irish uses two systems for counting, particularly for numbers above 20.

### The Decimal System (`An Córas Deachúlach`)

This is the modern system, taught in schools and used in official contexts. It is based on tens (20, 30, 40, etc.). Numbers between the tens are formed by addition.

- *Seachtó a dó* (72, abstract)
- *Dhá bhád is seachtó* (72 boats, lit. "two boats and seventy")

### The Vigesimal System (`An Córas Fichiúlach`)

This is the traditional system, still common in the Gaeltacht and older texts. It is based on twenties (20, 2x20, 3x20, etc.). Numbers between the twenties are formed by adding numbers from 1 to 19.

- *Trí fichid a dó dhéag* (72, abstract, lit. "three twenties and twelve")
- *Dhá bhád déag is trí fichid* (72 boats, lit. "twelve boats and three twenties")

Historically, the decimal system was common in Old Irish, but the vigesimal system became dominant until its recent revival as the modern standard.

## Cardinal Numbers: Counting Things

Cardinal numbers are used to state a quantity. They have two distinct forms depending on whether they are used abstractly or with a noun.

### Abstract Numbers (`Maoluimhreacha`)

These numbers are used when counting aloud or when the number stands alone without a noun. They are also used for numbering, telling time, and in arithmetic.

#### The Number Particle `a`

When counting from 1 to 19, the abstract numbers are usually preceded by the particle `a`. This particle causes an h-prefix on numbers that begin with a vowel.

- `a haon` (one), `a dó` (two), `a trí` (three), `a hocht` (eight)

In many dialects, the `a` is dropped in casual speech, but the h-prefix often remains: `haon, dó, trí... hocht`.

The full tens (20, 30, 40, etc.) are nouns and do not take the particle `a`. However, when forming compound numbers, the particle reappears before the unit.

- `fiche` (20), `tríocha` (30)
- `fiche a haon` (21)
- `caoga a naoi` (59)

#### Form Differences

Some numbers have different forms when used abstractly versus with a noun.

| Abstract Form | Adjectival Form | Example with Noun |
| :------------ | :-------------- | :---------------- |
| `a dó`        | `dhá`           | *dhá bhád* (two boats) |
| `a ceathair`  | `ceithre`       | *ceithre fhuinneog* (four windows) |

#### Numbers 11–19

These are formed by adding `déag` ("-teen") after the base number. `Déag` is lenited after `dó`.

- `a haon déag` (11)
- `a dó dhéag` (12)
- `a trí déag` (13)

#### Usage of Abstract Numbers

- **Counting:** *A haon, a dó, a trí, a ceathair...* (1, 2, 3, 4...)
- **Numbering:** *Seomra a trí* (room no. 3), *Bus fiche a haon* (bus 21).
- **Telephone Numbers:** Digits are read individually. A double digit can be expressed with `péire` (pair).
    - *A náid, a dó, péire sé* (0266...)
- **Arithmetic:** *A haon agus a dó, sin a trí* (1 and 2 is 3).
- **Years:** Years from 1101 to 1999 are often read as two two-digit numbers.
    - *sa bhliain ocht déag dó dhéag* (in the year 1812)
    - *naoi déag fiche a trí* (1923)
    - From 2000 onwards, `dhá mhíle` is used: *dhá mhíle is a seacht* (2007).
- **Dates:** *Iúil a ceathair, 1935* (4th July 1935).
- **Time:** *Tá sé a haon a chlog* (It is one o'clock), *ag a trí a chlog* (at 3 o'clock).

### Adjectival Numbers (`Bunuimhreacha`)

These numbers are used directly before a noun to specify its quantity. They often cause initial mutations.

#### Grammar Rules for 1–10

| Number | Mutation | Noun Form | Example |
| :----- | :------- | :-------- | :------ |
| 1 `aon` | Lenition | Singular | *aon bhád amháin* (one boat) |
| 2 `dhá` | Lenition | Singular/Dual | *dhá bhád* (two boats), *dhá láimh* (two hands) |
| 3–6 | Lenition | Singular | *trí bhád* (three boats), *cúig chat* (five cats) |
| 7–10 | Eclipsis | Singular | *seacht mbád* (seven boats), *deich gcat* (ten cats) |

**Note on `aon`**:
- To mean "one", `aon` is usually paired with `amháin`: *aon bhád amháin*.
- On its own, `aon` means "any" in questions (*An bhfuil aon bhád agat?* - Do you have any boat?) and "no" in negative statements (*Níl aon charr agam* - I don't have a car).

#### The Counting Plural

While the noun is usually singular after numbers 3–10, some common nouns (especially units of measurement and time) use a special plural form called the **counting plural**.

- The noun is **not lenited** after 3–6.
- The noun is **eclipsed** after 7–10.

Examples:
- *trí bliana* (three years) vs. *trí bhád* (three boats)
- *seacht mbliana* (seven years)
- *trí cinn* (three things/items, from `ceann`)
- *seacht gcinn* (seven things/items)

#### Numbers 11–19

The word `déag` is placed *after* the noun. The mutation and noun form are determined by the base number (1-9).

- *trí bhád déag* (13 boats)
- *seacht mbád déag* (17 boats)
- *naoi mbliana déag* (19 years)

The lenition of `déag` itself depends on the ending of the noun preceding it.

#### Numbers Over 20

The structure depends on the counting system used.

- **Decimal:** *trí bhád is seachtó* (73 boats, lit. "3 boats and 70")
- **Vigesimal:** *trí bhád déag is trí fichid* (73 boats, lit. "13 boats and 3x20")

The tens (`fiche`, `tríocha`, etc.) are nouns, so the following noun is in the singular and is not mutated: *fiche bád* (20 boats).

#### Adjectives Following the Noun

An adjective describing the counted noun follows these rules:
- **After `dhá`**: The adjective is in the plural and is lenited.
  - *dhá bhád mhóra* (two big boats)
- **After 3–10 + singular noun**: The adjective is in the plural and is lenited.
  - *trí bhád mhóra* (three big boats)
  - *seacht mbád mhóra* (seven big boats)
- **After `déag`**: The adjective comes after `déag` and is in the plural.
  - *trí bhád déag bheaga* (13 small boats)
- **After tens (e.g., `fiche`)**: The adjective is in the singular. It is lenited after a feminine noun.
  - *fiche fear mór* (20 big men)
  - *fiche bean mhór* (20 big women)

### Cardinal Numbers with the Definite Article

- **`an t-aon` (the one/only)**: *an t-aon bhád amháin* (the one/only boat).
- **`an dá` (the two)**: The `dh` of `dhá` is removed. *an dá bhád* (the two boats).
- **`na` + 3-10**: The plural article `na` is used. *na trí bhád* (the three boats), *na hocht mbád* (the eight boats).
- **`an` + tens**: The singular article `an` is used. *an fiche bliain* (the 20 years).

In the genitive case, these forms change:
- Genitive of `an dá bhád` is *seolta an dá bhád* (the sails of the two boats).
- Genitive of `na trí bhád` is *seolta na dtrí bhád* (the sails of the three boats, with eclipsis on the number).

## Personal Numbers (`Uimhreacha Pearsanta`)

These are special nouns used for counting people from 2 to 10, plus 12.

| Number | Personal Number | Example |
| :----- | :-------------- | :------ |
| 2      | `beirt`         | *beirt fhear* (two men) |
| 3      | `triúr`         | *triúr ban* (three women) |
| 4      | `ceathrar`      | *ceathrar páistí* (four children) |
| 5      | `cúigear`       | *cúigear múinteoirí* (five teachers) |
| 6      | `seisear`       | *seisear mac léinn* (six students) |
| 7      | `seachtar`      | *seachtar gardaí* (seven guards) |
| 8      | `ochtar`        | *ochtar dochtúirí* (eight doctors) |
| 9      | `naonúr`        | *naonúr imreoirí* (nine players) |
| 10     | `deichniúr`     | *deichniúr turasóirí* (ten tourists) |
| 12     | `dháréag`       | *an dáréag aspal* (the twelve apostles) |

#### Grammar of Personal Numbers

- They are nouns, not adjectives.
- The noun `beirt` is feminine and lenites a following noun: *beirt bhan*.
- The others (`triúr`, `ceathrar`, etc.) are masculine and do not cause mutation: *triúr fear*.
- The following noun is typically in the genitive plural (*triúr ban*) but can sometimes be in the nominative singular (*ochtar banaltra*).
- They can stand alone: *Bhí triúr ann* (There were three people there).
- They can be used in apposition: *siad triúr* (the three of them).
- In some dialects, they can be used to count inanimate objects: *Cé mhéad leabhar? - Triúr.* (How many books? - Three.)

## Ordinal Numbers (`Orduimhreacha`)

Ordinal numbers indicate position or rank (first, second, third, etc.). They are used with the definite article and a noun.

| Number | Ordinal Form | Example |
| :----- | :----------- | :------ |
| 1st    | `an chéad`   | *an chéad lá* (the first day) |
| 2nd    | `an dara`    | *an dara teach* (the second house) |
| 3rd    | `an tríú`    | *an tríú huair* (the third time) |
| 4th    | `an ceathrú` | *an ceathrú háit* (the fourth place) |
| 5th    | `an cúigiú`  | *an cúigiú bean* (the fifth woman) |
| 8th    | `an t-ochtú` | *an t-ochtú haois* (the eighth century) |
| 11th   | `an t-aonú ... déag` | *an t-aonú lá déag* (the eleventh day) |
| 20th   | `an fichiú`  | *an fichiú bliain* (the twentieth year) |

#### Grammar of Ordinal Numbers

- `an chéad` always lenites the following noun.
- From `an dara` onwards, there is no lenition, but an h-prefix is added to a following vowel.
- A t-prefix is used before vowel-initial ordinals like `aonú` and `ochtú`.
- For numbers 11-19 and above 20, `déag`, `is fiche`, etc., are placed after the noun: *an cúigiú lá déag* (the 15th day).
- When written with digits, the suffix `-ú` is used: `1ú`, `2ú`, `3ú`, `17ú`.

## Fractions, Prefixes, and Other Forms

### Fractions (`Codáin`)

- **`leath`** (half), **`trian`** (a third), **`ceathrú`** (a quarter) are special forms.
- Other simple fractions are formed like ordinal numbers: `cúigiú` (a fifth), `séú` (a sixth).
- For numerators greater than one, a cardinal number precedes the fraction: *dhá thrian* (two thirds), *trí cheathrú* (three quarters).

### Mixed Numbers (`Uimhreacha Measctha`)

- "And a half" is expressed with `go leith`: *uair go leith* (an hour and a half).
- "And a third" is `go dtrian`: *dhá uair go dtrian* (two and a third hours).
- Other fractions use `is` (and): *uair is ceathrú* (an hour and a quarter).

### Numbers as Prefixes

Numbers can be used as prefixes, usually causing lenition.
- `aon-`: *aontheangach* (monolingual)
- `dá-`/`dé-`: *dátheangach* (bilingual)
- `trí-`: *trídhathach* (tricolour)
- `ceathair-`: *ceathairchosach* (four-legged)

### Derived Adjectives and Adverbs

- **Multiplicatives**:
  - *faoi dhó* (twice), *faoi thrí* (three times)
  - *trí huaire* (three times)
  - *seacht n-uaire níos mó* (seven times bigger)
- **Adverbs from Ordinals** ("firstly, secondly..."):
  - *ar an gcéad dul síos* (firstly, in the first instance)
  - *sa dara háit* (secondly, in the second place)
