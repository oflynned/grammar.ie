In Irish, adverbs are words that modify verbs, adjectives, or other adverbs. They are generally invariable, meaning they do not change their form for case or number. However, adverbs that are derived from adjectives can be graded or compared, similar to the adjectives themselves.

## Word Order in the Sentence

The position of an adverb in an Irish sentence depends on what it is modifying.

### Modifying Adjectives and Adverbs

Adverbs that modify an adjective or another adverb are placed immediately before the word they are modifying.

*   *réasúnta dona* - quite bad
*   *pas beag rómhór* - a little too big

### Modifying Verbs and Sentences

Adverbs that modify a verb or the entire sentence usually come at the end of the sentence. If there are several adverbs, they typically appear in the order: place, manner, then time.

However, there are several important exceptions to this rule:

*   **Before simple pronoun objects:** An adverb comes before a simple personal or prepositional pronoun that functions as the object.
    *   *Bhí mo mhac inné agam.* - My son was with me yesterday.

*   **After stressed pronouns:** An adverb comes after an emphatic or demonstrative pronoun.
    *   *Bhí mo mhac agamsa inné.* - My son was with me yesterday.

*   **At the beginning of the sentence (Clefting):** Any part of a sentence, including an adverb, can be moved to the front for emphasis using a cleft sentence structure.
    *   *(Is) inné a bhí mo mhac agamsa.* - It was yesterday that my son was with me.
    *   *Inné (is ea) a bhí mo mhac agamsa.* - It was yesterday that my son was with me.

### Adverbs After the Subject

Some common adverbs can be placed directly after the subject, regardless of what follows. These include:

*   `anois` (now)
*   `riamh` (ever, before)
*   `i gcónaí`, `choíche` (always)
*   `go fóill`, `fós` (still, yet)
*   `go minic` (often)

- *An raibh tú riamh i mBéal Feirste?* - Have you ever been to Belfast?

### Adverbs with Bí ... Ann

When using the construction `bí ... ann` (to be, to exist), these adverbs can stand between the verb and an indefinite subject.

*   *Bíonn i gcónaí drochaimsir ann Lá Fhéile Pádraig.* - There is always bad weather on St. Patrick's Day.

### Interjections

Adverbial interjections like `cinnte` (certainly) and `ar ndóigh` (of course) can come between the verb and the subject, as long as the subject is not a pronoun.

*   *Tá cinnte Gardaí ar an sráid.* - There are certainly police officers on the street.
*   *Tá ar ndóigh daoine a chreideann…* - There are of course people who believe…

### Sentence-Initial Adverbs

Some adverbs, particularly those referring to the entire sentence or to time and place, can stand at the beginning of a sentence without changing its structure.

*   *Ar aon chuma, bhí sé ag obair go cúramach.* - In any case, he worked carefully.
*   *Amárach, beidh muid ag labhairt faoin ábhar sin.* - Tomorrow, we will talk about that topic.

When such a sentence becomes a subordinate clause with `go` or `nach`, the adverbial phrase comes before the conjunction.

*   *An chéad Nollaig eile tiocfaidh sé aníos.* - Next Christmas he will come up.
*   *Deiridís an chéad Nollaig eile **go** dtiocfadh sé aníos.* - They said that he would come up next Christmas.

### Fronting Adverbs with Cleft Sentences

Some adverbs can only be moved to the front of a sentence using a cleft structure. For some, this is the most common or required way to front them. These include `minic` (often), `annamh` (rarely), `fada` (for a long time), `ar éigean` (hardly), and `fánach` (rarely).

*   *Is minic a bhí sé ag obair go cúramach.* - Often he worked carefully.
*   *(Is) ar éigean a chreid sé mé.* - He hardly believed me.

In these structures, the relative forms of `bí` are often omitted, creating a "small clause".

*   *B’annamh é ag gearán.* - He rarely complained. (from *B’annamh a bhí sé ag gearán.*)
*   *B’annamh gan é ag gearán.* - He rarely did not complain. (from *B’annamh nach raibh sé ag gearán.*)

### Adverbs with Copula and Go/Nach Clauses

Some adverbs require a copula sentence followed by a `go`/`nach` clause instead of a relative clause.

*   *Is gearr go mbeimid abhaile.* - We will soon be home.
*   *B’fhéidir go bhfuil sé anseo.* - Perhaps he is here.
*   *Is dócha go dtiocfadh sí ar ais am éicínt.* - She would probably come back sometime.
*   *Is léir nach bhfuil a fhios agat.* - Clearly you don't know.
*   *Is cosúil go raibh sé sásta.* - Apparently he was satisfied.

The adverb `minic` uses a cleft sentence with regular verbs, but a `go`/`nach` clause with copula sentences.

*   *Is minic gur fearr an toil ná an t-eolas.* - Often the will is better than the knowledge.

<Note>
Placing an adverb at the beginning of a sentence gives it emphasis. Using a simple fronting or a cleft sentence creates slightly different nuances.

*   *Amárach, beidh muid ag labhairt faoin ábhar sin.* - Tomorrow, we will talk about that topic. (Simple emphasis on "tomorrow".)
*   *Is amárach a bheidh muid ag labhairt faoin ábhar sin.* - It is tomorrow that we will talk about that topic. (Stronger emphasis, implying *not* today or the day after.)
</Note>

## Adverbs of Place and Direction

Irish makes a very strict distinction between adverbs of place (location) and direction (movement). This system distinguishes between a state of rest, movement towards the speaker, and movement away from the speaker.

There is often a four-way distinction based on prefixes:
*   `th-`: Indicates a state of rest at a location.
*   `an-`: Indicates movement towards the speaker.
*   `s-`: Indicates movement away from the speaker.
*   `las-`: Indicates a position relative to a reference point.

### Movement: Towards and Away (an- vs s-)

The distinction between `an-` and `s-` marks whether the movement is towards the speaker (`an-`) or away from the speaker (`s-`). The reference point is always the location distant from the speaker.

*   `aníos`: up (towards the speaker, literally "from below hither")
*   `suas`: up (away from the speaker, literally "to above thither")
*   `anuas`: down (towards the speaker, literally "from above hither")
*   `síos`: down (away from the speaker, literally "to below thither")

### Position: Defined and Undefined (th- vs s-)

The `th-` prefix marks a defined point where something is located. If the location is undefined and only in a general direction, forms with `s-` are used instead.

*   *Tá sé thuas ansin.* - He is up there. (at a specific point)
*   *Tá sé suas ansin.* - He is up there. (somewhere up in that direction)

### Relative Position (las- and taobh)

To indicate a position relative to another object, adverbs can begin with `las-`/`lais-`. The reference point is introduced with the preposition `de`.

*   *lastuas den staighre* - above the stairs
*   *laistigh de na ballaí* - inside the walls

Alternatively, the noun `taobh` (side) can be used with a positional adverb, also followed by `de`.

*   *taobh thiar de* (or `laistiar de`) - behind, west of
*   *taobh istigh de* (or `laistigh de`) - inside of

### Change of Perspective

The choice of directional adverb is based on the speaker's perspective. However, in narratives where the speaker is not present, the perspective often shifts to the subject or agent of the sentence.

### Usage with Verbs of Motion

Certain verbs of motion pair logically with specific directional adverbs.

*   `tar` (come) describes movement towards the speaker, so it pairs with `an-` adverbs, not `s-` adverbs.
    *   *Tar aníos!* - Come up!
    *   An exception is when moving together with the speaker: *Tar suas liom.* - Come up with me!
*   `téigh` (go) describes movement away from the speaker, so it pairs with `s-` adverbs.
    *   *Téigh suas!* - Go up!

These combinations can also form phrasal verbs with idiomatic meanings.

*   *éirigh amach* - to get out; to rebel, revolt
*   *cuir síos ar* - to lay something down
*   *cuir anuas ar* - to spread something out over a surface (e.g., a sheet on a bed)

<Warning>
Be cautious of direct translations from English phrasal verbs. While some have entered the language as Anglicisms, they are not always considered standard. For example, `glan suas` for "clean up" is a direct borrowing from English.
</Warning>

### Other Grammatical Roles

*   **As Prepositions:** Adverbs with `s-` and `an-` can function like prepositions when followed directly by a noun.
    *   *anuas an staighre* - down the stairs
    *   *síos an bóthar* - down the road
*   **As Adjectives:** Adverbs with `th-` and `an-` can be used as attributes to modify nouns.
    *   *an teach thuaidh* - the northern house
    *   *an ghaoth aniar* - the west wind
*   **Nominalisation:** The phrase `an taobh` (the side) can be used to turn these adverbs into nouns.
    *   *an taobh istigh* - the inside
    *   *an taobh thuas* - the upper part

<Details summary="Historical Note">
The second part of some directional adverbs, such as `uas` (over), `íos` (under), and `iar` (behind), are derived from prepositions that are no longer in independent use in Modern Irish.
</Details>

## The Cardinal Directions

The system of directional adverbs is used to express the cardinal directions.

| Direction | At Rest (in the...) | From... (towards speaker) | To... (away from speaker) | Relative (of...) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **North** | `thuaidh` | `aduaidh` | `ó thuaidh` | `lastuaidh` |
| **South** | `theas` | `aneas` | `ó dheas` | `laisteas` |
| **East** | `thoir` | `anoir` | `soir` | `lastoir` |
| **West** | `thiar` | `aniar` | `siar` | `laistiar` |

<Details summary="Note on 'ó thuaidh' and 'ó dheas'">
The use of `ó` to mean "to" in `ó thuaidh` (to the north) and `ó dheas` (to the south) can be confusing, as the preposition `ó` usually means "from". This `ó` is a reduction of the older preposition `fó` (under, in the direction of).
</Details>

The terms for east and west are used frequently in everyday descriptions of movement.

*   *Tá mé ag dul siar an bóthar.* - I am going along the road (westwards).
*   *Tháinig láir bhán ar cosa in airde anoir an trá.* - A white mare came galloping along the beach (from the east).

<Dialect region="Connacht">
In the Cois Fharraige dialect of Connacht, `ó dheas` and `ó thuaidh` are used for any location or movement related to the south or north (to, from, or in). The forms `aduaidh` and `aneas` are used for compound directions, e.g., `siar aduaidh` (to the north-west).
</Dialect>

### Intermediate Cardinal Directions

Intermediate directions are formed by combining the main directions. Note that East/West comes before North/South.

*   **North-East:** `thoir thuaidh` (in the NE), `anoir aduaidh` (from the NE), `soir ó thuaidh` (to the NE)
*   **South-West:** `thiar theas` (in the SW), `aniar aneas` (from the SW), `siar ó dheas` (to the SW)

### Nominal Forms and Abbreviations

The noun forms for the cardinal directions are different from the adverbs.

*   `an tuaisceart` (the North)
*   `an deisceart` (the South)
*   `an t-oirthear` (the East)
*   `an t-iarthar` (the West)
*   `an t-oirdheisceart` (the South-East)
*   `an t-iardheisceart` (the South-West)
*   `an t-oirthuaisceart` (the North-East)
*   `an t-iarthuaisceart` (the North-West)

These noun forms provide the standard abbreviations: **T** (`tuaisceart`), **D** (`deisceart`), **O** (`oirthear`), **I** (`iarthar`).

### Up and Down

| Location | At Rest | From (towards speaker) | To (away from speaker) | Relative (of) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Up** | `thuas` | `anuas` | `suas` | `lastuas` |
| **Down** | `thíos` | `aníos` | `síos` | `laistíos` |

<Note>
The adverbs `suas` and `amach` can also be used figuratively to mean "completely" or "entirely".
- *Tá mé caite amach.* - I am completely exhausted.
</Note>

### Front and Back

In the traditional Irish orientation, a person faces east (towards the rising sun), meaning west is behind them.

*   `thiar` / `laistiar de` - behind, in the west
*   `thoir` - in front, in the east (less common, `ar aghaidh` is usually preferred)

### Left and Right

Following the same east-facing orientation, south is to the right.

*   `deas` - right; southern; nice
*   `clé` - left

`Deas` and `clé` are adjectives. The adverbial forms are typically `ar dheis` (on the right) and `ar chlé` (on the left).

### Clockwise and Anti-clockwise

The roots for south (`deas`) and north (`tuath`) also appear in words for circular motion.

*   `deiseal` - clockwise (in the direction of the sun)
*   `tuathal` - anti-clockwise (against the sun's direction)

### Beyond and This Side

| Location | At Rest | From (towards speaker) | To (away from speaker) | Relative (of) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Beyond** | `thall` | `anall` | `sall` / `anonn` | `lastall` |
| **This Side**| `abhus` | - | - | `lasbhus` |

### In and Out

| Location | To/Into | At Rest | Relative (of) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **In** | `isteach` | `istigh` | `laistigh` |
| **Out** | `amach` | `amuigh` | `lasmuigh` |

| Adverb | Meaning | Adverb | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `anois` | now | `ansin` | then |
| `inniu` | today | `anocht` | tonight |
| `inné` | yesterday | `aréir` | last night |
| `arú inné` | the day before yesterday | `arú aréir` | the night before last |
| `amárach` | tomorrow | `anuraidh` | last year |
| `arú amárach`| the day after tomorrow | `arú anuraidh` | the year before last |

## Adverbs from Adjectives

Adverbs of manner are typically formed from adjectives using the particle `go`.

### Formation with Go

The particle `go` is placed before the base form of the adjective. It prefixes `h` to adjectives beginning with a vowel.

| Form | Examples |
| :--- | :--- |
| `go` + Adjective | `go maith` (well), `go cliste` (cleverly) |
| `go` + `h` + Vowel-initial Adjective | `go hiontach` (wonderfully), `go hard` (loudly) |

The particle `go` is omitted in several situations:
*   When the adverb itself is modified by another adverb: *D’oibrigh sé réasúnta dona.* (He worked quite badly.)
*   With a series of adverbs not joined by `agus`: *Roinn siad go fial fairsing linn.* (They shared generously and amply with us.)
*   When the adverb modifies an attributive adjective: *an teach measartha mór* (the fairly large house).
*   Often before `ró-` (too): *D’oibrigh sé ródhona.* (He worked too badly.)
*   Often when the adverb is fronted in a cleft sentence: *Ní dona a d’oibrigh sé.* (It was not badly that he worked.)

### Adverbial Use of the Comparative

To compare how an action is done, the comparative form of the adjective is used as an adverb.

*   **Comparative:** Use `níos` + the comparative form of the adjective.
    *   *D’oibrigh sé níos measa ná Pól.* - He worked worse than Paul.
*   **Superlative:** Use `is` + the comparative form. This forms a relative clause.
    *   *Is é Seán is luaithe a ritheann.* - Seán runs the fastest. (Literally: "It is Seán who is fastest who runs.")

## Adverbial Pronouns

Adverbial pronouns (e.g., "therein", "thereupon", "hereby") are formed using prepositions.

### From Conjugated Prepositions

For simple prepositions, the adverbial pronoun is usually the 3rd person singular masculine form of the prepositional pronoun.

*   `aige` (at him) -> thereby
*   `air` (on him) -> thereupon
*   `ann` (in him) -> therein
*   `dó` (to him) -> thereto

To make the meaning more specific (like "herein" vs "therein"), the demonstrative pronouns `seo` (this/here), `sin` (that/there), or `siúd` (that/yonder) are added.

| Preposition | `... sin` (there-) | `... seo` (here-) | `... siúd` (yonder-) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `ag` | `aige sin` | `aige seo` | `aige siúd` |
| `ar` | `air sin` | `air seo` | `air siúd` |
| `as` | `as sin` | `as seo` | `as siúd` |
| `chuig` | `chuige sin` | `chuige seo` | `chuige siúd` |
| `de` | `de sin` | `de seo` | `de siúd` |
| `do` | `dó sin` | `dó seo` | `dó siúd` |
| `fara` | `fairis sin` | `fairis seo` | `fairis siúd` |
| `faoi` | `faoi sin` | `faoi seo` | `faoi siúd` |
| `i` | `ann sin` | `ann seo` | `ann siúd` |
| `idir` | `eatarthu sin` | `eatarthu seo` | `eatarthu siúd` |
| `le` | `leis sin` | `leis seo` | `leis siúd` |
| `ó` | `uaidh sin` | `uaidh seo` | `uaidh siúd` |
| `roimh` | `roimhe sin` | `roimhe seo` | `roimhe siúd` |
| `trí` | `tríd sin` | `tríd seo` | `tríd siúd` |
| `thar` | `thairis sin` | `thairis seo` | `thairis siúd` |
| `um` | `uime sin` | `uime seo` | `uime siúd` |

<Note>
Some of these forms have developed common, specific meanings:
*   `fairis sin` became `freisin` (also), used in Connacht. In Munster, `leis` is used for "also".
*   `thairis sin` means "furthermore" or "moreover".
*   `ann` is very commonly used with `bí` to mean "there is" or "to exist": *Tá teach ann.* (There is a house).
*   `ann sin`, `ann seo`, and `ann siúd` are usually written as one word: `ansin` (then, there), `anseo` (here), and `ansiúd` (over there).
</Note>

### From Compound Prepositions

*   **Two-part prepositions** (e.g., `ar son`) form adverbs using the 3rd person masculine possessive pronoun `a`.
    *   `ar son` (for the sake of) -> `ar a shon sin` (for that reason)
    *   `de bharr` (as a result of) -> `dá bharr sin` (as a result of that)
*   **Three-part prepositions** (e.g., `in aice le`) form adverbs using the 3rd person masculine prepositional pronoun of the final preposition.
    *   `in aice le` (beside) -> `in aice leis` (beside it)
    *   `mar gheall ar` (because of) -> `mar gheall air` (because of it)

## Adverbs from Nouns

Many adverbs in Irish originated as nouns, often combined with the preposition `i` (in). The original noun is often no longer obvious.

*   `amuigh` (outside) < `i maigh` (in a plain)
*   `amach` (out) < `i mach` (into a plain)
*   `istigh` (inside) < `sa tigh` (in the house)
*   `isteach` (in) < `sa teach` (into the house)

Some nouns can also be used as adverbs without a preposition, often to indicate a quantity.

*   *beagán fuar* - a little cold
*   *Ísligh beagán é!* - Lower it a bit!

## Adverbs from Weekdays

To use a day of the week as a time adverb (e.g., "on Monday"), the word `Dé` is placed before the genitive form of the day's name.

| Day (Noun) | Adverb | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `Luan` | `Dé Luain` | (on) Monday |
| `Máirt` | `Dé Máirt` | (on) Tuesday |
| `Céadaoin` | `Dé Céadaoin` | (on) Wednesday |
| `Déardaoin` | `Déardaoin` | (on) Thursday |
| `Aoine` | `Dé hAoine` | (on) Friday |
| `Satharn` | `Dé Sathairn` | (on) Saturday |
| `Domhnach` | `Dé Domhnaigh` | (on) Sunday |

Compare the usage:
*   `an Luan` - Monday (the noun)
*   `Dé Luain` - on Monday
*   `gach Luan` - every Monday

Adverbs indicating frequency ("how many times") are formed using `uair` (time/occasion) or the preposition `faoi`.
