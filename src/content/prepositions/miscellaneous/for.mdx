## Overview

A single, direct equivalent for the English preposition 'for' does not exist in Irish. The word 'for' is used in many different contexts in English, and Irish uses a variety of prepositions and phrases to express these different meanings.

## Temporal and Spatial Contexts

The context of time and distance often dictates which phrase to use.

### Duration of an Event

- **Total Duration**: Use `ar feadh` (literally 'on duration') when the total length of an event is the focus, whether in the past or future.
    - *Bhí sé anseo ar feadh seachtaine.* - He was here for a week.
    - *Tháinig sé anseo ar feadh seachtaine.* - He came here for a week.
- **Past Endpoint**: Use `ar feadh` or `le` ('with') when the event has a clear endpoint in the past.
    - *Bhí sé anseo le seachtain agus fuair sé bás.* - He was here for a week and then died.
- **Future Endpoint**: Use `go ceann` (literally 'until head') when the event will end at some point in the future.
    - *Beidh sé anseo go ceann seachtaine.* - He will be here for a week (from now).
- **Continuing to Present**: Use `le` when an event continues up to the present moment, corresponding to the English Present Perfect tense.
    - *Tá mé anseo le seachtain.* - I have been here for a week.

Other prepositions like `le linn` ('during') and `i rith` ('in the course of') can also be used.

- *Bhí sé ansin le linn an chogaidh.* - He was there for the duration of the war.

### Distance

To express 'for' a certain distance, use `ar feadh`.

- *ar feadh trí mhíle* - for three miles

## Purpose, Benefit, and Goals

These phrases are used when something is done for a specific purpose, person, or objective.

### For the Benefit or Purpose of

Use `le haghaidh` (literally 'with face') or `i gcomhair` (literally 'in counting').

- *Cheannaigh mé sin le m’aghaidh.* - I bought that for myself.
- *Rinne siad féasta faoina chomhair.* - They held a feast for him.

Dialectal variants include `faoi chomhair` and `faoi choinne` (or `fá choinne` in Ulster).

- *litir faoi do choinne* - a letter for you
- *áit faoi choinne leabhar* - a place for books

### As a Favour

Use the preposition `do` ('to') when an action is done as a favour for someone.

- *Rinne mé sin dó.* - I did that for him.

### For a Goal or Objective

Use `ar son` (literally 'on well-being') when an action is done to achieve a goal.

- *Rinne mé ar son an airgid é.* - I did it for the money.
- *Glacaigí agus ithigí uile é seo: óir is é seo mo chorp a thabharfar ar bhur son.* - Take this, all of you, and eat of it, for this is my Body, which will be given up for you.

In Ulster, `de/mar gheall ar` is also used.

### To Get Something

Use `faoi dhéin` (literally 'under fetching') or `le haghaidh` when going somewhere to get something.

- *Bhuail mé isteach sa siopa faoi dhéin siúcra.* - I went into the shop for some sugar.

## Exchange and Transaction

These prepositions cover contexts involving money, payment, and work.

### For a Sum of Money

Use the preposition `ar` ('on') for exchanges involving a price.

- *Cheannaigh mé ar phunt amháin é.* - I bought it for one pound.
- *Thug sé trí ba ar an gcapall sin.* - He gave three cows for that horse.

### In Return For

Use `as ucht` (literally 'out of breast') or simply `as` ('out of') to mean 'in return for'.

- *Go raibh maith agat as an gcabhair.* - Thank you for the help.
- *D’íoc mé fiche punt as.* - I paid 20 pounds for it.

In Ulster, `ar son` is more common in this context.

### Working For

Use `le` ('with') or `do` ('to') to describe working for a company or person.

- *ag obair leis an gcomhlacht árachais* - working for the insurance company

## State and Evaluation

These forms express judgement or relation.

### Good or Bad For

Use `do` ('to') or `ag` ('at') to describe if something is good, bad, easy, or difficult for someone or something.

- *maith dó* - good for him
- *olc duit* - bad for you
- *éasca aici* - easy for her
- *róthrom ag an bpáiste* - too heavy for the child

### As Regards or Concerning

Several phrases can mean 'for' in the sense of 'considering' or 'as regards'.

- **mar le** ('as with'): *is breá an aimsir í, mar le geimhreadh!* - For a winter, it's lovely weather!
- **maidir le**: *Maidir le Sasanach níl sé ag déanamh go holc.* - For an Englishman, he's not doing badly.
- **de** ('of'): *Is fear maith é d’fhear Sasanach.* - He is a good man for an Englishman.

## Representation and Substitution

These phrases are used when one thing or person stands in for another.

### Instead Of or In Place Of

Use `thar cheann` (literally 'over head') or `in ionad`.

- *Bhí sé ann thar mo cheann.* - He was there for me (in my place).
- *Labhair sé thar ceann an náisiúin.* - He spoke for the whole nation.

### In Favour Of

To express being 'for' or in favour of something, use `i leith` (literally 'in side'), `i bhfabhar` ('in favour'), or `ar thaobh` (literally 'on side').

- *Tá mé i leith fanacht anseo.* - I am for staying here.
- *Tá sé ar thaobh Shasana.* - He is for England.

## Advanced and Idiomatic Usage

These are more specific or less common uses.

### For All That

The phrase `ar feadh a` combined with the relative particle `a` can mean 'for all that', often in a disapproving sense. `Ar scáth a` is used similarly.

- *ar feadh a bhfuil de thalamh aige* - for all the land that he has
- *Ar feadh a ndearnadh de dhochar duit* - for all the harm that was done to you

### Responsible For

The preposition `um` ('about/around') is often used in official titles to denote responsibility.

- *An Institiúid Éireannach um Chosaint Raideolaíoch* - Radiological Protection Institute of Ireland (RPII)

### Man For Man

The preposition `sa` ('in the') is used in reciprocal phrases.

- *fear san fhear* - man for man
- *súil sa tsúil* - eye for an eye
