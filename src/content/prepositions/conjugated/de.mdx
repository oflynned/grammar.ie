## Overview

The preposition `de` governs the dative case.

In most dialects, the simple prepositions `de` and `do` are pronounced identically, typically as [gə] in Galway and Mayo, and [də] in Donegal, Cork, and Kerry. Only the conjugated forms are consistently distinguished. Before a vowel or `fh`, `de` becomes `d’`, as in *d’Úna* (from Úna).

### Forms and Conjugations

The following tables show how `de` combines with pronouns and articles.

#### With Personal and Possessive Pronouns

| Person      | Standard Form | Emphatic Form | Translation | Possessive Form | Translation |
|:------------|:--------------|:--------------|:------------|:----------------|:------------|
| 1st Sg.     | `díom`        | `díomsa`      | from me     | `de mo` (`dem`) | from my     |
| 2nd Sg.     | `díot`        | `díotsa`      | from you    | `de do` (`ded`) | from your   |
| 3rd Sg. (m) | `de`          | `desean`      | from him    | `dá`            | from his    |
| 3rd Sg. (f) | `di`          | `dise`        | from her    | `dá`            | from her    |
| 1st Pl.     | `dínn`        | `dínne`       | from us     | `dár`           | from our    |
| 2nd Pl.     | `díbh`        | `díbhse`      | from you    | `de bhur`       | from your   |
| 3rd Pl.     | `díobh`       | `díobhsan`    | from them   | `dá`            | from their  |

**Dialectal Notes:**
*   These forms are often lenited after vowels, e.g., `dhíom` instead of `díom`.
*   In Connemara, short forms are common: `’íom`, `’íot`, `’e`, `’í`, `’ínn`, `’íbh`, `’íobh`.
*   Forms of `do` are sometimes used instead, such as `dom` for `díom`.
*   In Ulster, `dena` is used instead of `dá` (from his/her/their).

#### With the Article

| Formula     | Result                  |
|:------------|:------------------------|
| `de` + `an` | `den`                   |
| `de` + `na` | `de na` (also `des na`) |

#### With Relative Pronouns

| Tense   | Relative Pronoun | Copula Form                    | Translation     |
|:--------|:-----------------|:-------------------------------|:----------------|
| Present | `dá`             | `dar` (`darb` before a vowel)  | from which/whom |
| Past    | `dár`            | `dar` (`darbh` before a vowel) | from which/whom |

Note: Some sources write both the pronominal and copular forms as `dar`.

### Interrogatives

*   `cé de`? = from whom?
*   `cad de` / `cé de` / `cá de`? = from what?

## Core Functions: Space, Material, and Content

### Separation and Cessation

`De` is used to indicate removal, distance, or stopping an action. It focuses on the separation itself, whereas `ó` often emphasises the point of origin.

*   *Titim den chathaoir.* - Falling from the chair.
*   *Stad sé den ól.* - He stopped drinking.
*   *Tóg den bhord é.* - Take it off the table.
*   *Bain dhíot an cóta.* - Take off your coat.

### Attachment and Continuation

Conversely, `de` can indicate attachment or continuation with an activity.

*   *Greamaíonn sé den bhalla é.* - He fastens it to the wall.
*   *Leanaim den obair.* - I continue with the work.
*   *Lean de sin!* - Stick with that!

### Relative Position

It is used with adverbs of place to specify a position relative to something else.

*   *Laistiar den teach.* - Behind the house.
*   *Taobh abhus den abhainn.* - This side of the river.

### Material and Substance

`De` indicates what something is made of.

*   *Rinneadh d’adhmad é.* - It was made of wood.
*   *Déanta de phrás.* - Made of brass.
*   *Rinne fíon den uisce.* - Wine was made from the water.

### Content and Filling

It is used to describe what something is filled with.

*   *Líonaim d’uisce é.* - I fill it with water.
*   *Lán de chlocha.* - Full of stones.

## Cause, Agency, and Instrument

### Cause

`De` can express the cause or reason for a state or action.

*   *Fuair sé bás den ocras.* - He died of hunger.
*   *Briseadh de thaisme é.* - It broke by accident.
*   *Tá mé tuirseach den chaint.* - I am tired of the talk.

### Instrument and Means

It can specify the tool or method used for an action.

*   **Instrument**: *Buille de mhaide.* - A blow with a stick.
*   **Sudden Movement**: *D’éirigh sé de léim.* - He rose with a jump.
*   **Transport**: *Chuaigh sé ann de thraein.* - He went there by train.

## Identity and Relation

### Origin and Descent

`De` is used to indicate family or group origin.

*   *Bean de Ghearaltach.* - A woman of the Fitzgeralds.
*   *Cér díobh thú?* - What family are you from?

### Depiction

It is used when describing a picture or photograph of someone.

*   *Pictiúr de Sheán.* - A picture of Seán.
*   *Tóg grianghraf díom!* - Take a photo of me!

### Apposition

`De` is used in various appositional structures to add descriptive information.

*   **General**: *A leithéid de lá.* - Such a day.
*   **Hypernym**: *Amadán de mhac.* - A fool of a son.
*   **With `bí`**: *Tá sagart de mhac aige.* - He has a priest for a son.
*   **Comparatives**: *Is fearr d’fhear é ná Seán.* - He is a better man than Seán.
*   **Degree of Difference**: *Is sine de chúig bliana é.* - He is older by five years.
*   **Idiomatic Comparative**: *Ní troimide an loch an lacha.* - The lake is none the heavier for the duck.
*   **Concession**: *Dá dheacracht é.* - However difficult it may be.
*   **Correlative**: *Dá dheacracht é is ea is fearr.* - The more difficult, the better.

### Partitive Use

`De` is used to denote a part of a whole, often where English uses "of" or "some of".

*   *Cuid de na daoine.* - Some of the people.
*   *Triúr de na mná.* - Three of the women.
*   *Leabhar de chuid Joyce.* - A book of Joyce's.
*   **Characteristics**: *Is den mhúineadh é.* - It is part of good manners.
*   **Rights/Duties**: *Tá sé de cheart agam é a dhéanamh.* - I have the right to do it.

A key partitive structure appears in semi-negative sentences with `ní ... ach` (not ... but/only). Here, `de` introduces the whole from which a part is being singled out.

*   *Ní bhfuair mé d’fhreagra ach sin.* - I only got that for an answer.
*   *Níl de chairde aige ach a mhadra.* - He has no friends other than his dog.

## Temporal and Abstract Functions

### Time and Duration

`De` can mark a point in time or duration.

*   *Ag obair de ló is d’oíche.* - Working by day and by night.
*   *Nuair a tháinig mé anseo den chéad uair.* - When I came here for the first time.

### Opinion and Disposition

It appears in many idiomatic phrases related to opinion, will, and accordance.

| Concept         | Formula          | Example                         |
|:----------------|:-----------------|:--------------------------------|
| According to    | `de réir`        | *de réir an dlí*                |
| In my opinion   | `de mo dhóighse` | *de mo dhóighse, tá sé ceart*   |
| Of my own will  | `de mo dheoin`   | *rinne mé de mo dheoin é*       |
| Against my will | `de m’ainneoin`  | *bhí orm imeacht de m’ainneoin* |

### Proleptic Pronoun

The form `de` (from him/it) can be used proleptically, anticipating a following clause.

*   *Bí cinnte de go dtiocfaidh sé.* - Be sure of it that he will come.

## Special Cases

### In Surnames

`De` appears in many surnames of Anglo-Norman origin, such as `de Búrca` (Burke) or `de Buitléir` (Butler). This `de` is a borrowing and does not cause lenition. It is typically pronounced [ə] or [də].
