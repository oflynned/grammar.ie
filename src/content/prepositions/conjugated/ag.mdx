## Overview

The preposition `ag` is most commonly used to mean "at" or "by", but its most significant function is to express possession, similar to the verb "to have" in English. It also forms the continuous aspect of verbs (e.g., "I am eating").

Although written as `ag`, it is usually pronounced as if it were spelt `aig`. In its use in the progressive aspect of verbs, however, it is pronounced `[ə]`, or `[əg]` before a vowel.

In Munster, `aige` is often used instead of `ag`, except when forming the progressive aspect. This also leads to combinations with the article, such as `aigen` for `ag an` and `aigesna` for `ag na`.

### Conjugated Forms

Like all Irish prepositions, `ag` combines with personal pronouns to form a single word.

| Person      | Standard Form | Emphatic Form | English |
|:------------|:--------------|:--------------|:--------|
| 1st sg.     | `agam`        | `agamsa`      | at me   |
| 2nd sg.     | `agat`        | `agatsa`      | at you  |
| 3rd sg. (m) | `aige`        | `aigesean`    | at him  |
| 3rd sg. (f) | `aici`        | `aicise`      | at her  |
| 1st pl.     | `againn`      | `againne`     | at us   |
| 2nd pl.     | `agaibh`      | `agaibhse`    | at you  |
| 3rd pl.     | `acu`         | `acusan`      | at them |

In Connemara, shortened forms are sometimes used, such as `’am` for `agam`, `’ad` for `agat`, and `’ainn` for `againn`.

### Question Forms

- **cé aige...?** - at whom...?
- **cad aige / cé aige / cá haige...?** - at what...?
- **cé acu...?** - which of them...?
- **cé againn/agaibh/acu...?** - who of us/you/them...?

The preposition `ag` governs the dative case.

## Core Functions

### Spatial and Temporal Location

`Ag` can denote a specific point in space or time, translating to "at" or "by".

- *ag an bhfuinneoig* - at the window
- *ag a trí a chlog* - at 3 o'clock

### Possession

The most common function of `ag` is to express possession using the structure `tá + noun + ag + owner`.

- *tá teach aige* - he has a house

This structure extends to figurative possession, such as abilities, permissions, obligations, and emotions.

- **Ability**: *tá Gaeilge agam* - I speak Irish.
- **Permission**: *tá cead agam* - I have permission / I am allowed.
- **Obligation**: *tá rud le déanamh agam* - I have something to do.
- **Obligation (Negative)**: *níl agat ach...* - you only have to...

| Concept          | Formula                         | Example                 | Translation (Literal)        |
|:-----------------|:--------------------------------|:------------------------|:-----------------------------|
| To love          | tá grá ag (Subject) ar (Object) | *tá grá agam uirthi*    | I have love for her          |
| To desire        | tá dúil ag (Subject) i (Object) | *tá dúil agam ann*      | I have desire in it          |
| To be interested | tá súim ag (Subject) i (Object) | *tá súim agam sa scéal* | I have interest in the story |

### Continuous Aspect

`Ag` is used with the verbal noun to form the progressive or continuous aspect of a verb.

- *tá mé ag ithe* - I am eating (literally, "I am at eating").

In this context, `ag` is often considered a grammatical particle rather than a preposition with a spatial meaning.

## Agency and State

### Agent and Cause

`Ag` is used to mark the agent (the doer) in passive-like constructions, or the cause of a state.

- **Agent**: *Chuala mé agat féin é* - I heard it from you yourself.
- **Cause**: *Tá tinneas cinn orm aige* - I have a headache because of him.
- **Cause with Adjectives**: *tinn ag an ngrian* - sick from the sunshine.

This is particularly common in perfect tense and passive constructions. The phrase can often be translated in two ways.

- *Tá an bord briste ag Máirtín* - The table was broken by Martin / Martin has broken the table.
- *Tá sé á dhéanamh agam* - It is being done by me.

It can also follow an adjective and verbal noun to indicate the cause of a feeling.

- *Tá mé tinn tuirseach ag breathnú ort* - I am sick and tired of watching you.

### Describing Attributes

`Ag` can be used with adjectives to attribute a quality to someone's action or character.

- *deas agat* - nice of you
- *éasca agam* - easy for me
- *maith againn* - good for us
- *trom aici* - difficult for her

### Expressing Opinion

`Ag` can be used in phrases to express an opinion or perception.

- *tá sé ina laoch acu* - they see him as a hero (literally, "he is in his hero at them")
- *is beag agam é* - I think little of him/it (literally, "is little at-me he")

## Advanced and Dialectal Usage

### Partitive Use

`Ag` can be used instead of the more common preposition `de` to indicate a subset of a group, especially with the plural pronouns `againn`, `agaibh`, and `acu`.

- *go leor acu* - a lot of them
- *roinnt acu* - some of them

### Possessive Substitute

When a noun is modified by a demonstrative pronoun (`seo`, `sin`, `siúd`) or a superlative/ordinal number, `ag` is used instead of a standard possessive pronoun.

- *an teach sin agamsa* - that house of mine
- *mac is sine atá agam* - my eldest son
- *an chéad leabhar agam* - my first book

### Demonstrative Use

The phrases `ag seo` and `ag sin` can be used to mean "here is" and "there is" in certain contexts, often to introduce a definition or example.

- *Ag seo mar a shainmhíníonn sé é* - Here is how he defines it.

Today, the `ag` is often omitted in these sentences.

### Specific Verbs

`Ag` is used idiomatically with certain verbs.

- **clois ag** - to hear from
- **faigh ag** - to get from
- **fág ag** - to leave with/at

### Dialectal Substitute for Chuig

In Connacht and Ulster, `ag` is sometimes used as a substitute for the preposition `chuig` (to), due to the similar pronunciation of `chuig` as `aig` in those dialects.
