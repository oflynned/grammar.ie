## Overview

In Irish, a clause's "statement" is known as the predicate, or `faisnéis`. In a standard clause, the verb itself forms the predicate. However, some verbs, like the English verb "to be", are non-predicative; they simply introduce the main statement. These statements are typically either an *identification* ("He is Paul") or a *classification* ("He is a donkey").

In these cases, the predicate is a noun (a predicate noun) or an adjective (a predicate adjective). The verb merely connects this predicate to the subject.

- In the sentence "He is a student," the predicate is "a student". The verb "is" connects it to the subject, "he".
- In "He is tall," the predicate is "tall".

In Irish, the copula (`is`) is used in place of the verb "to be" for these types of sentences.

While English uses a Subject-Verb-Object (SVO) word order, Irish uses a Predicate-Subject-Object (PSO) order. In a copular clause, this means the predicate usually comes first, followed by the copula and then the subject.

## Word Order Deviations

In certain identification and classification clauses, the standard PSO word order is altered.

### Identification Clauses with Pronouns

When the 1st and 2nd person pronouns (`mé`, `tú`, `sinn`, `muid`, `sibh`) are involved, they almost always appear immediately after the copula, whether they are the subject or the predicate.

- *Is mé an fear.* - I am the man.

Here, `mé` is considered the subject, despite its position. This structure is often avoided in declarative sentences but is common in questions:

- *An mé an fear?* - Am I the man?

To make the pronoun the predicate and restore a clear PSO structure, the emphatic (stressed) form of the pronoun is used. This is the more common construction for declarative sentences.

- *Is mise an fear.* - I am the man. (Literally: The man is *me*.)

The unstressed pronoun (`mé`) signals it is the subject, while the emphatic form (`mise`) signals it is the predicate.

### Identification Clauses with Nouns

When an identification clause involves proper nouns or nouns with demonstrative pronouns, the subject often appears before the predicate. However, a "sub-predicate" (`é`, `í`, `iad`) is inserted after the copula to formally maintain the PSO structure.

- *Is é Pól an múinteoir.* - Paul is the teacher.
- *Is é an fear sin an dalta.* - That man is the student.

Demonstrative pronouns used as the subject also follow this pattern:

- *Is é sin an dalta.* - That is the student.
- *Is iad seo na múinteoirí.* - These are the teachers.

A common short form combines the copula and the demonstrative pronoun at the start of the sentence:

- *Sin é an dalta.* - That is the student.
- *Seo iad na múinteoirí.* - These are the teachers.

Placing the subject at the end of the sentence is rare but possible if the predicate needs special emphasis:

- *Is é an múinteoir Pól.* - Pól is *the* (famous, important) teacher.

### Clauses with Two Nouns

In identification clauses with two definite nouns, like "The man is the student," it can be difficult to distinguish the subject from the predicate.

Because of the PSO rule, the noun following the copula and sub-predicate is typically the predicate:

- *Is é an dalta an fear.* - The man is *the student*. (Predicate: `an dalta`)

However, the roles can be reversed, with the subject preceding the predicate. The sub-predicate `é` still separates the subject from the copula, maintaining the formal PSO structure.

- *Is é an fear an dalta.* - *The student* is the man. (Predicate: `an dalta`)

Context, such as answering a question, often clarifies the intended meaning.

- *Cé hé an fear?* - *Is é an dalta an fear.* (Who is the man? - The man is *the student*.)
- *Cé hé an dalta?* - *Is é an dalta an fear.* (Who is the student? - *The man* is the student.)

### Classification Clauses

In classification clauses, short forms with a demonstrative pronoun as the subject are also common. The demonstrative pronoun, seen as a combination of the copula and pronoun, starts the sentence.

- *Sin amhrán.* - That is a song.
- *Seo fear.* - This is a man.

The longer, more formal structure places the demonstrative pronoun at the end of the sentence:

- *Is amhrán é sin.* - That is a song.
- *Is fear é seo.* - This is a man.

In rare cases, the order of an indefinite subject and predicate can be switched, as seen in the proverb:

- *Tír gan teanga tír gan anam.* - A country without a language is a country without a soul.

## Grammatical Perspectives

The assignment of "subject" and "predicate" in copular clauses is a matter of grammatical debate. The approach described above aligns with modern standard grammars, which equate the subject with the *topic* (known information) and the predicate with the *comment* (new information).

An alternative view holds that the element immediately following the copula must *always* be the predicate. From this perspective, a subject can never directly follow the copula without a sub-predicate (`é`, `í`, `iad`) intervening. In this model, a pronoun like `mé` in `Is mé an fear` would always be the predicate, regardless of its form. This interpretation results in a nearly exceptionless PSO rule.

Historically, the modern form `is mé` may have replaced older conjugated forms of the copula, such as `am` ("I am"). In Old Irish, a sentence like *Am fer* ("I am a man") was common. In identification clauses, however, `is` was already used, leading to the structure *Is mé in fer* ("I am the man"). This was likely interpreted as "It is I, the man," where `is` is an impersonal form, `mé` is the predicate, and `in fer` is an apposition.
